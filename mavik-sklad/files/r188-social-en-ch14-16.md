## CHAPTER 14

Twice during the meeting she caught herself missing the last few words.

Not the whole conversation. Just the end of a sentence. Someone would be talking about the schedule for visits to two communities; she would write down the date, the number of families, who was providing transport, and then suddenly realise she had no idea what had been said for the last five seconds.

“So the twenty-seventh?” she asked again.

“I just said they can’t do the twenty-seventh,” the social-services worker replied. “There’s something at the village council. I don’t know what. I don’t think they know either.”

“The twenty-eighth.”

“Oh. The twenty-eighth works.”

She corrected the date.

The bag of syrnyky stood beside her laptop. The assistant had been right: they were cold. She ate one during the meeting and did not want the second. The little lid from the sour cream had stuck to a sheet of paper, and when she pulled at it the bag tipped over.

She set it upright.

The meeting went on.

A bus.

A room.

Two trainers.

A list that had to be confirmed by Friday.

Nothing difficult. Work she knew how to do.

When the call ended, she closed the current project files and returned to the copy of the hospital table.

The column she had added the day before was still empty except for the heading:

**Follow-up status.**

She started with case 01.

The internal foundation archive did not contain an ending. It contained a date, a request, correspondence with partners, and then silence.

She searched by case number.

Then by date.

Then by the hospital name.

A later email appeared in a different thread.

It was from the volunteer.

Two months after the original request.

She opened it.

The message was short. The attachment was shorter still.

The cases were listed one after another. The wording varied. In one, financing had not been found in time. In another, another organisation had covered treatment, but the child had died later. Somewhere the fundraiser had remained open until the end. Somewhere there had been partial support.

The endings did not vary.

She moved down the list.

First.

Second.

Third.

Then the lines became shorter.

The next sentence was three words long.

Ninth.

Tenth.

She returned to the top and counted again. She had understood it the first time, but now she was checking whether she had somehow skipped even one row with something different written in it.

She had not.

All ten had died.

At the bottom, the volunteer had written:

“Please take these cases into account when planning a possible medical programme in the future.”

The foundation had replied the same day.

“Thank you for the information. We are very sorry. We will pass this on to management.”

Below that was the standard foundation signature line with the logo, job title and phone numbers.

She looked at the date.

By then, the residential event was already over.

Its final report was open in another tab.

She did not open it.

First she dialled the volunteer’s number.

It rang for a long time. She was about to cancel the call when someone answered.

“Hello?”

Something beeped in the background. Then the woman’s voice said to someone else, “No, without a bag.”

“Hello. This is…”

She gave her name.

There was silence for a second or two.

“God. Yes. I remember.”

She looked at the list.

“Is this a good time?”

“I’m in a shop, but go ahead. One second.” The voice moved away. “No, I don’t need the receipt. Thank you. Right, I’m outside. What happened?”

“I found the old correspondence about that hospital request. The one with ten children.”

The volunteer did not answer at once.

“Which one? We had… Oh, wait. Yours came as part of a batch, didn’t it?”

“Yes.”

“Ten at once.”

“Yes.”

A car seemed to pass close to her on the other end. She pressed the phone closer to her ear.

“I can see your email,” she said. “Two months later. With the outcomes.”

“Yeah.”

The word was so ordinary that for a moment she did not know what to do with it.

“All ten.”

“Yes.”

“Do you remember them?”

“Not all of them anymore. I remember yours. I remember one boy — his mother called every day herself. And the girl… I think they found the money for her. Wait. I don’t want to make things up. It’s been a long time.”

“I can see it here. The second case was paid for through another organisation.”

“Could be. I couldn’t tell you now. I had spreadsheets then. Everything was in spreadsheets. Half those files probably wouldn’t even open anymore.”

She rested an elbow on the desk.

“Did you know then that the foundation could have redirected money from another project?”

“No.”

The answer came immediately.

“They told you the same thing they told me?”

“That the budget had already been approved and they weren’t taking medical cases outside the plan at that point. Yes. Why?”

She looked at the foundation logo in the corner of her screen.

“I work there now.”

There was a pause.

“Seriously?”

“Yes.”

“How long?”

She told her.

“Wow.”

Another pause.

“And what do you do there?”

“I run a programme area.”

“Wow.”

This time the word sounded different.

“I found the internal documents,” she said. “They discussed reallocating the money. Technically, they could have done it.”

“Right.”

“But another project was already under way. Hotel, trainers, printing, transport. They decided not to cancel it and to look for other money for us.”

The volunteer was quiet longer this time.

“I see.”

“You see?”

“No. I just don’t know what to say to you.”

A dog barked somewhere near her.

“We were looking too,” the volunteer went on. “It’s not like we were sitting around. You know what it was like with your little girl. Same with the others. Someone would give part of the amount. Someone would ask for the documents for the third time. At one foundation the manager went on holiday and everything froze for a week. That kind of thing happened all the time.”

“You wrote to them afterwards that they had all died.”

“If the email is there, then I wrote it.”

“It’s there.”

“I wrote to everyone back then. I thought if they knew, maybe next time they’d change something.”

She heard the volunteer sigh.

“Maybe they did change something. I didn’t keep track.”

She opened the foundation calendar for the following year almost automatically. It was full of events that had not existed yet when those emails were being written.

“Do you still volunteer?” she asked.

“Less. I work in logistics now. Proper logistics, with boxes,” the woman laughed. “Although that’s insane too. We’ve spent half the day looking for two pallets that are apparently sitting right under our noses.”

She smiled as well.

“Right.”

“What are you going to do?”

It was a simple question.

She had no answer.

“For now, I’m trying to understand.”

“Then good luck.”

“Thanks.”

“And… I’m glad you somehow… well…”

The volunteer stopped.

“Work?” she offered.

“Yes. That. You work.”

They said goodbye.

For several minutes after the call, she sat with the phone in her hand.

Then she opened the copy of the table and began filling in **Follow-up status**.

Not word for word from the email. Briefly.

For the first case she wrote:

“Died.”

For the second she wrote the same thing at first, then added:

“Financing found through another organisation; died later.”

Third.

Fourth.

At the fourth row, the cursor blinked longer.

She wrote exactly what the source said.

“Fundraiser not completed. Child died.”

No name.

When she finished the tenth row, she saved the file to her desktop. Not to the archive.

She called it `medical_request_analysis`.

She did not like the name.

Renamed it `medical_request_10`.

Did not like that either, but left it.

Then she opened the final report for the residential project.

The first pages were familiar. Programme title. Implementation period. Partner. Budget. Geography. Target group.

Five days.

Thirty-seven families.

Training blocks.

Children’s programme.

Accommodation.

Meals.

Materials.

The photographs were embedded in the report.

People sitting in a semicircle.

Children drawing.

A group photograph outside.

A man holding a small girl who was pulling at his ear.

The results section said the planned activities had been completed.

The participant numbers matched the approved plan.

The questionnaires were positive.

The financial section balanced to the last kopeck.

10,000,000.00 UAH.

Used for the intended purpose.

Below the report were the approvals from the foundation and the partner.

A few pages later came a thank-you letter for the successful implementation of the project.

She read the whole thing.

She did not find a single false sentence.

Her phone buzzed.

The assistant had sent a photograph from the library. Ordinary tables that really could be moved. Heavy burgundy curtains hung in the corner.

Under the picture:

“They found curtains.”

She replied:

“Now we can run it.”

The assistant sent a smiley.

She closed the messenger and looked again at the two files.

In the first were ten rows that all ended the same way.

In the second was a project that had ended successfully.

She did not put them side by side on the screen.

Instead, she opened the calendar and checked whether the second trainer had replied about swapping the sessions.

He had.

“I can do it, but only if we finish by 16:30 on day three.”

She forwarded the message to the first trainer and wrote:

“Then we swap. Update the programme by tomorrow.”

## CHAPTER 16

“Right… what have we got here?”

“Can I ask something that’s not exactly on the slide?”

“You’re saying we shouldn’t react immediately when a child provokes us. Fine. What if at that moment he’s hitting a younger child with a chair?”

“The children enjoyed it.”

Nobody had written: **this wasn’t needed**.

Not even that man.

She opened yesterday’s sentence:

**The event was useful to participants.**

Did not change it.

Beside it, she created a new folder.

Called it `Training`.

At first she copied in only programmes from the previous year.

There were more than she expected.

Prospective carers.

Foster families.

Family-type homes.

Patronage carers.

Separate modules for social services.

Supervision meetings.

Peer exchanges.

Burnout prevention.

Competency development.

Training of trainers.

She opened the last one.

The same two surnames she had just seen in the videos.

And three more familiar ones.

She returned to the list.

Then opened the previous year.

The surnames repeated.

Not all of them.

Enough.

Her phone rang. She answered automatically.

“Yes.”

“Hello, it’s about the library. We wanted to check whether you need a projector screen or whether you have your own?”

“Our own.”

“Because ours is old. It has a bit of a green tint.”

“We’ll bring ours.”

“Good. And on Thursday there’s chess after you. You’ll definitely be done by five?”

She looked at the programme.

“By five.”

“They’re nervous.”

“The chess players?”

“Very.”

“Tell them we won’t run over.”

“Good.”

She ended the call.

There were already seventeen programmes in the folder.

Opened another.

Familiar trainer.

Another.

Again.

In several events the topic changed, but the people did not.

She did not write down conclusions.

Instead, she created a table.

Date.

Programme name.

Topic.

Trainers.

Number of participants.

Cost.

Suppliers.

After a pause, she added the last column.

**Outcome**

Looked at it and added in brackets:

**not an indicator**

The assistant came in without knocking.

“You’re making a spreadsheet?”

“Yes.”

“This is serious.”

“Why?”

“You make spreadsheets when you’re nervous.”

“I’m not nervous.”

“Then everything is just very bad.”

“Get out.”

“Already gone.”

She left.

The heroine opened the first row and started filling it in.

CHAPTER 5. THE LETTER TO A PUBLISHER

The word “Hello” sat on the screen, lonely and formal.
Artur had been staring at it for ten minutes.
In a novel, he could begin a scene with a corpse in a hotel room, an escape from a night train or a sentence after which the hero lost everything. But a letter to a publisher began with the word “Hello,” and after that life became complicated.
Because after “Hello” you had to sound normal.
Not servile.
Not arrogant.
Not like a genius in exile.
Not like someone who had already spent the advance in his head.
And not like an office manager accidentally selling his own soul as a PDF.
Artur opened ChatGPT.
“Write a letter to a publisher. Short, professional, but not dead. Don’t use the phrase ‘good time of day’. If you use it, I’m closing the laptop.”
ChatGPT replied:
“Understood. Here is one version.”
Artur tensed.
The screen displayed:
“Hello! My name is Artur. I would like to submit my novel for your consideration…”
— Still alive, — Artur said.
The rest of the text moved smoothly.
Too smoothly.
That was how people wrote when they wanted to offend no one but had no intention of being remembered either.
“I would be grateful for the opportunity to have it considered…”
“I hope my work may be of interest to your editorial team…”
“Kind regards…”
Artur finished reading and grimaced.
— That isn’t a letter. That’s a person waiting in line at a notary’s office.
He typed:
“Make it more alive.”
ChatGPT thought and produced:
“Hello! I have written a novel that combines psychological depth, sharp conflict and a contemporary voice…”
— Stop, — Artur said. — You sound like you’re selling laundry detergent to people with childhood trauma.
He deleted everything after “Hello.”
There was a strange unfairness to letters. A novel was allowed to be complicated. A letter had to be understandable. Preferably from the first sentence.
Artur opened publishers’ websites.
The first said:
“Manuscript submissions are temporarily closed.”
— Temporarily, — Artur repeated. — That’s “I’ll call you back,” but with a logo.
The second said:
“We consider only works that fit our editorial list.”
Artur began looking for the editorial list. The website behaved like an escape room for people with too much free time. Seven minutes later he found a page where historical trauma, urban fantasy, culinary memoir and one novel with a title that made him want to wash his hands were all standing side by side.
— Broad list, — Artur said. — Like a conscience at a corporate party.
The third website had clear requirements:
“Send a blurb, synopsis, the first 30 pages, a short author biography and information about your social-media presence.”
Artur froze at the last item.
“Information about your social-media presence.”
He opened his Instagram.
His last post was three years old: a photograph from the Carpathians in which Artur stood in front of a mountain trying to look as though he had not nearly suffocated on the climb.
His stories were mostly from the gym, coffee and random screenshots of books he bought faster than he could read them.
Followers — 412.
Active among them — his mother, Denys, a former university classmate, a sports-nutrition shop and a bot with the profile picture of a woman who desperately wanted to meet him, but for some reason only in English.
Artur imagined a publisher opening his profile.
“Author has three novels, good physical condition and weak media presence.”
He messaged Denys:
“The publisher wants my social media.”
Denys replied almost immediately:
“Send them your biceps.”
“Very funny.”
“I’m serious. If the novel doesn’t work, maybe the cover will.”
Artur put the phone down.
He needed a biography.
He opened a new document:
“Author_biography”
And wrote:
“Artur, 33. Works as a manager. Has written three novels. Unmarried. Has a respectable bench press and a complicated relationship with the literary market.”
He reread it.
It was honest.
Too honest.
He asked ChatGPT:
“Write a short author biography. No pathos. 500 characters.”
ChatGPT replied:
“Artur is a Ukrainian author working at the intersection of psychological fiction, social drama and the philosophical novel. His writing explores identity, freedom of choice, inner dignity and the pressure the modern world places on the individual.”
Artur rubbed his face.
— I sound like a conference.
He added:
“In human language.”
The new version was simpler:
“Artur is the author of three completed novels. He writes about people trying to remain themselves in circumstances where doing so becomes a difficult choice. He lives in Kyiv and works in business, so he knows well how the modern world can turn a person into a function.”
Artur reread it.
That already sounded like him.
Almost.
He returned to the letter.
“Hello. My name is Artur. I would like to submit my novel for your consideration…”
Then he inserted the title of the novel.
Looked at it.
Beside the phrase “submit for your consideration,” the title looked as though it had been brought in for a job interview.
He added:
“It is a psychological novel about a man who has the chance to get everything he wants, but to do so must become the kind of person he despises.”
There.
That worked.
The sentence had a spine.
Denys’s baking soda had turned out to be useful.
Artur finished the letter. Added the blurb, synopsis, first thirty pages, biography and a link to Instagram, although doing the last made him feel faintly embarrassed, as though he were showing a doctor the wrong test results.
Then he returned to ChatGPT.
“What other options do I have if publishers don’t respond?”
ChatGPT produced a list.
Literary competitions.
Manuscript pitching.
Publishing excerpts.
An author blog.
Finding a literary agent.
Printing at his own expense.
Artur stopped at the last item.
“Tell me about printing at my own expense.”
ChatGPT obediently expanded: editing, proofreading, layout, cover, ISBN, print run, logistics, storage, sales, promotion, bookshop commissions, delivery, returns of unsold copies.
Artur read and felt the word “writer” slowly putting on a delivery driver’s jacket.
He opened a calculator.
Then a printer’s website.
Then a spreadsheet.
He calculated editing.
Proofreading.
Layout.
Cover.
Printing at least five hundred copies.
Delivery.
And “unforeseen expenses,” because life dearly loves hiding in that particular column.
The total was large enough that Artur stared at it for several seconds without a single philosophical thought.
Then said:
— That isn’t a print run. That’s a car.
Not a new car, but a perfectly real one. And most importantly, once you bought a car, it could at least take you somewhere by itself.
A print run had to be taken places by you.
And then sold.
Artur imagined himself at a book fair: standing behind a table, boxes of novels rising behind him, wearing the smile of a man who had just invested the price of a car in a dream and was now explaining to a passer-by why the book did not cost “too much” but “a fair price.”
— Buy the novel, — he told the imaginary customer. — It’s about freedom of choice.
— Is there a discount?
Artur closed his eyes.
Then wrote to ChatGPT:
“So I pay as much as for a car, and then I have to sell the books myself?”
ChatGPT replied:
“Yes. With self-funded printing, the author assumes not only production costs but also the risks involved in selling the print run.”
— Wonderful, — Artur said. — I wanted to become a writer and they’re offering me a career as a travelling salesman with a boot full of literature.
He opened a new column in the spreadsheet and wrote:
“Self-publishing at my own expense — possible, but not now.”
In the comments he added:
“The price of a car that then has to be sold for parts in the form of books.”
He did not reject the option completely.
But placed it on the shelf labelled “when I finally lose my mind or find a sponsor who loves psychological fiction.”
After that Artur created a spreadsheet:
“Publisher_submissions”
Columns:
Publisher
Contact
Requirements
Date sent
Response
Comments
Artur looked at the spreadsheet.
— So the dream has officially moved into Excel.
It was a strange moment. As if he had not moved closer to literature but entered negotiations with its accounting department.
But it worked.
He checked the first letter three times.
Then once more.
Then opened every attachment to make sure he had not attached a photo of his gym membership instead of the synopsis.
Then checked the name of the editorial team.
Then the subject line:
“Novel manuscript for consideration — Artur…”
It sounded normal.
Too normal.
Artur was almost disappointed. He wanted something more dramatic:
“Rescue Three Novels from Laptop Captivity”
or
“Ukrainian Literature’s Last Chance, No Pressure”.
He left the normal version.
His finger hovered over “Send.”
And then everything went quiet.
Not in the flat. The flat was already quiet.
It went quiet inside him.
While the novel sat in a folder, it was safe. He could consider it strong, underrated, unfairly overlooked. It was territory on which Artur was right.
Once he pressed the button, everything changed.
The text went out into the world.
And the world had the disgusting habit of answering.
Or not answering, which could be even worse.
Artur stood up.
Walked to the window.
Kyiv glowed below him like a vast control panel for other people’s lives. Behind every window someone was beginning something, abandoning something, waiting, fearing, buying, losing, promising themselves “from Monday.”
Artur returned to the laptop.
Sat down.
Looked at the button.
— Well, — he said. — If I’m going to fail, at least let it not be inside the drafts.
And clicked “Send.”
The letter disappeared.
A few seconds later the mail service produced an automatic message:
“Your message has been delivered.”
It was not recognition.
It was not a contract.
It was not victory.
But for the first time in a long while, his novel existed somewhere beyond his laptop.
His phone flashed.
Denys:
“You alive?”
Artur wrote:
“Sent it.”
The reply came instantly:
“Oh. Now you’re officially a writer in waiting mode.”
Artur opened the spreadsheet and entered the first row.
Date sent: today.
Response: pending.
In the “Comments” column he wrote:
“Didn’t die.”
Thought about it.
And added:
“Not yet.”

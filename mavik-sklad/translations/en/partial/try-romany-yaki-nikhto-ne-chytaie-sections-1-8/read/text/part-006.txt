CHAPTER 6. THE BAR WHERE EVERYONE WRITES SOMETHING

On Saturday evening Artur stood in front of the mirror trying to look like a man who was simply going to a bar, rather than temporarily leaving his dream unattended.
It proved harder than he had expected.
He changed three times.
The first outfit was too office-like. In it, Artur looked as though he was going out not for a beer but to approve a budget.
The second was too sporty. It made him want to look for a treadmill rather than meet people.
The third was normal: dark jeans, a T-shirt, an unpretentious blazer. Exactly how a man looks when he has not given up yet but no longer wants to explain that to everyone around him.
His phone flashed.
Denys:
“Are you coming, or have you already decided a bar is the commercialisation of human loneliness?”
Artur replied:
“On my way.”
Denys:
“Don’t forget to bring yourself. Sometimes you turn up and leave the rest of you inside the laptop.”
Artur looked at the phone.
— Practical people are a curse. They don’t let you deteriorate beautifully.
The bar was in Podil, in a semi-basement with brick walls, warm light and music that knew how to behave: it was there, but it did not force its way into the conversation.
A sign by the entrance read:
“Tonight’s signature cocktail: ‘Editorial Revision’.”
Artur read the ingredients:
gin, lemon, tonic, pepper, bitter.
— Sounds like a review, — he said.
Denys was already at a table, waving to him. Four other people sat with him: a man in a black turtleneck, a woman with short hair, a guy in a hoodie that said “content is king”, and a brunette in a green dress who was typing something into her phone.
— Oh, he made it! — Denys stood up. — Everyone, this is Artur. He writes novels.
— Published? — the man in the turtleneck asked immediately.
Artur took off his jacket.
— Good evening to you too.
— I’m just asking.
— I’ve written three. Sent the first one to a publisher.
— Ah. — The man nodded as though Artur had said he was building a rocket in his garage. — So you’re still in the process.
— We’re all in the process, — Denys said. — Some of us are in the process of living, others in the process of making excuses.
— Denys, — Artur said, — I came here to relax, not to listen to your motivational poison.
— It isn’t poison. It’s vitamins without the sugar coating.
The woman with short hair held out her hand.
— Marta. I work in marketing. I write short copy for brands that think they’re people.
— Artur. I write long texts about people brands haven’t managed to replace yet.
— Good opening, — Marta said. — You could put that on a cover.
The guy in the hoodie leaned forward.
— I’m Vlad. I run a blog about film, storytelling and neural networks. I’m writing a screenplay now.
— About what?
— Don’t know yet. But I’ve already got a mood board.
Artur looked at Denys.
Denys raised both hands: not my fault, life itself showed up.
— And you? — Artur asked the brunette in the green dress.
She looked up from her phone.
— Olya. I’m an editor.
For a second, the table grew quieter.
Artur felt his inner manuscript automatically straighten its back.
— At a publishing house? — he asked.
— No. I work with nonfiction, commercial copy, sometimes fiction manuscripts. Mostly I rescue authors from themselves.
— That’s a separate profession?
— More of a calling with elements of sanitation work.
Denys clapped his hands happily.
— There. I knew you and Artur would find common ground. He needs sanitation too, but he’s still resisting.
Artur sat down.
— I already like this evening. I haven’t ordered anything yet and I’ve already been diagnosed.
A waiter came over.
— What can I get you?
Denys ordered beer.
Marta — a dry white.
Vlad — something craft, but “not too bitter”.
Olya — water and a gin and tonic.
The waiter looked at Artur.
— And for you?
Artur glanced at the menu.
— What’s in the “Editorial Revision”?
— Gin, lemon, tonic, pepper and bitter.
— Does it hurt afterwards?
— Depends on the text.
— I’ll have one.
The waiter left.
Marta leaned towards Artur.
— So you really wrote three novels?
— Yes.
— Finished them?
— Yes.
— As in actually finished? Not “almost”, not “just need to write the ending”, not “structurally it’s all clear”?
— Actually finished.
Marta whistled.
— Strong opening move. These days a finished novel is practically a mythical creature. Like a unicorn, only with revisions.
Vlad perked up.
— I want to write a novel too. But I’m thinking of starting with a blog about how I’m writing a novel.
— And the novel itself? — Artur asked.
— The blog will help me build an audience.
— For what?
— For the novel.
— The one that doesn’t exist yet?
— It will.
Denys leaned towards Artur.
— See? The man’s building a bridge to an island he hasn’t discovered yet. That’s strategy.
Vlad did not take offence.
— That’s how it works now. First you create anticipation.
— Anticipation of what? — Artur asked.
— Content.
— I thought content was supposed to contain something.
Marta laughed.
— Old-fashioned approach. Now content can contain anticipation of the next piece of content.
— So modern culture is people queuing for the queue?
— More or less, — Olya said. — And making stories about it.
The waiter brought the drinks.
Artur took a sip of his “Editorial Revision” and coughed.
— Well? — Denys asked.
— Feels like someone cut my third act.
— At least there’s no padding, — Olya said.
Artur raised his glass.
— To texts without padding and people without illusions.
— Dangerous toast, — Marta said. — Half the country would have to fall silent after that.
The conversation sped up.
Vlad said short videos were the new literature.
Artur replied that short videos were more like the new weather: everyone discussed them, but nobody controlled them.
Marta explained that every product needed packaging.
Artur said he did not like the word “product” when it came to a novel.
— What word do you like? — Marta asked.
— Work.
— It’s a work while you’re writing it. It becomes a product when someone is supposed to buy it.
— Brutal.
— No. The market isn’t brutal at all. It just has no imagination.
Olya nodded.
— The market isn’t against beauty. It just asks, “Who’s paying for the printing?”
— I’ve already priced printing it myself, — Artur said. — It costs as much as a car, except afterwards you still have to sell the car yourself. One wheel at a time, in crowded places.
Denys raised his glass.
— To Ukrainian self-publishing: buy the dream wholesale, sell it retail.
— Preferably with a card terminal, — Marta added. — Cashless payment improves conversion.
Artur looked at her.
— You just killed poetry with the word “conversion”.
— Poetry has only itself to blame. It should have had a business model.
Vlad cut in:
— An author has to be multi-format now. Novel, blog, video, podcast, newsletter, merch…
— Merch? — Artur repeated.
— T-shirts with quotes.
— I don’t have readers yet and you already want to dress them in my sentences?
— You have to think ahead.
— I am thinking ahead. That’s precisely why I don’t want to own a warehouse full of T-shirts bearing a quote nobody recognises.
Olya smiled.
— Vlad isn’t entirely wrong, actually.
— That is the most frightening way to begin a sentence, — Denys said. — Common sense usually dies immediately afterwards.
— An author’s visibility really does matter, — Olya continued. — But a lot of people confuse visibility with noise.
Artur turned towards her.
— There. Thank you. That’s worth writing down.
— Write it down, — Denys said. — You’ll finally have content.
Olya set down her glass.
— An author needs to be visible not because he jumps around in front of a camera. Because the reader understands there’s a living person behind the text. Not a profile picture, not a pose, not “I create meanings”. A person.
— Is “I create meanings” a crime? — Vlad asked.
— Not a crime. But it’s already close to an administrative offence.
Marta laughed.
— I’d fine people in tax-free metaphors.
For the first time that evening, Artur relaxed.
He liked this conversation. It was silly, alive, sharp and useful all at once. That was rare. Usually a conversation was either useful or enjoyable. Combining the two was suspicious.
— So what’s your novel about? — Olya asked.
Artur automatically took a deeper breath.
A whole army of explanations lined up in his head: theme, structure, conflict, the protagonist’s inner transformation, social context, moral choice, and the symbolism he had so carefully planted throughout the text like mines for an attentive reader.
Then he remembered Denys.
Synopsis.
X-ray.
Spine.
And said:
— A man gets the chance to have everything he wants, but to get it he has to become the kind of person he despises.
Olya did not say “atmospheric”.
Marta did not say “cool”.
Vlad did not immediately suggest making a teaser.
They simply fell silent for two seconds.
They were good seconds.
In the modern world, two seconds of silence after a sentence is practically an award.
— That works, — Olya said.
Artur carefully set down his glass.
— Are you saying that as an editor or as a person?
— As a person. The editor would say: I need to see the text.
— Those are different answers?
— Very. A person can be interested. An editor has to check whether the author has hidden three hundred pages of fog behind one good sentence.
Denys pointed at Artur.
— His fog is disciplined. Marches in formation.
— Send me an excerpt, — Olya said.
Artur looked at her.
— Seriously?
— No, I collect the first thirty pages of strange men.
— Sorry. It’s just that usually after someone says “send it over, I’ll read it”, they go into spiritual evacuation mode.
— I’m not promising a twenty-page review. But I’ll read an excerpt.
— By the weekend? — Denys asked.
— Denys, — Olya said, — don’t interfere with the man’s trauma.
Artur took out his phone.
— Where should I send it?
Olya dictated her email address.
Vlad immediately leaned over.
— You can send it to me too. I might review it.
Marta narrowed her eyes.
— You just said you don’t read long texts unless there’s an audio version.
— I’m developing.
— You’re adapting to the presence of an editor.
— That’s development too.
Artur sent the excerpt to Olya.
Then, after a pause, to Vlad as well.
Denys looked at him like a coach whose trainee had managed, for the first time, not to fall off the bicycle.
— There. Two submissions in one evening. One in a bar. One potentially into the void. Statistics are improving.
— I still haven’t decided whether that counts as progress, — Artur said.
— Progress is when you do something that makes it impossible to keep suffering beautifully in the same old place.
Marta raised her glass.
— That’s a good line.
— Don’t write it down, — Artur said. — If you do, he’ll start thinking he’s a philosopher.
— Too late, — Denys said. — I already think of myself as salt.
— And baking soda, — Marta added.
— I’m multifunctional.
Vlad had opened his phone again.
— Listen, maybe Artur needs TikTok? Something like “writer reads one powerful line from his novel every day”.
Artur shook his head.
— I don’t want to stand in front of a camera with the face of a man who has just invented depth.
— You don’t need a face. Hands, coffee, laptop, rain outside the window.
— That’s not TikTok. That’s a coffee-shop obituary.
Marta said:
— You could actually do it properly. No clowning. No dancing with the manuscript. Just show thoughts, scenes, the process.
— So I haven’t published a book yet, but I’m already supposed to document how I haven’t published it?
— Exactly.
Denys raised a finger.
— “Chronicles of an Unpublished Author.” I’d watch that.
— Because you know me.
— No. Because other people’s attempts are always more interesting than other people’s victories. People lie in victory. In the attempt they haven’t had time yet.
Olya nodded.
— A reader doesn’t always need a successful author.
Sometimes they need a living one.
Artur looked at her.
— Have all of you conspired to say useful things tonight?
— Don’t exaggerate, — Marta said. — We’re drinking. It’s an accident.
The conversation flowed on.
About books everyone had bought and nobody had opened.
About people who said “I love reading” but meant “I love thinking of myself as someone who reads”.
About authors who put “writer” in their bios after three posts and one trauma.
About publishers who wanted new voices, preferably ones that already had an audience.
About audiences that wanted novelty but clicked only on what they already knew.
— The world wants risk without risk, — Olya said. — A new book, but one that’s already been tested. A new author, but one everybody already knows.
— So a debut author is supposed to arrive with previous debut experience? — Artur asked.
— And sales figures before the first print run, — Marta said.
— Wonderful. Literature is now a job vacancy: “Seeking author under thirty with ten years of successful publications.”
Denys tapped his glass against the table.
— Write that one down. That definitely belongs in the quote book.
Artur laughed.
He genuinely laughed. Not ironically. Not defensively. Not like a man trying to prove that something did not hurt. He simply laughed.
And suddenly realised he had not done that so easily in a long time.
Not because life had become easier.
Because for the first time in a long while, his dream was sitting at a table with other people instead of lying alone inside his laptop.
Olya looked at the time and stood up.
— I should probably go.
Artur nodded with the careful indifference men sometimes switch on when they are anything but indifferent.
— Yes, of course.
— I got the excerpt, — she said. — I’ll read it tomorrow.
— A phrase with a dangerous reputation.
— I know. So I’ll be precise: I’ll read it by tomorrow evening. If I don’t, I’ll message you and say I didn’t. That’s another adult form of life.
Artur smiled.
— People do that?
— Some do.
— I thought it was a myth.
— No. Just a rare species.
She picked up her bag but did not hurry away. She looked at him more closely.
— Are you always like this?
— Like what?
— As if you want to be read and are terrified someone might actually read you at the same time.
Denys, who had been pretending not to listen, said quietly:
— Oh. We’ve moved on to editing the personality.
— Denys, — Olya said without turning towards him, — don’t interrupt the scene.
— Sorry. I’m just a secondary character with a commentator function.
— Then comment inside your head.
— That goes against my nature.
Artur set down his glass.
— I’m not afraid of being read.
Olya raised an eyebrow.
— Artur, you just said that in the voice of a man who’s afraid even of the short blurb about himself.
Marta laughed.
— That one hurt, but beautifully.
— Is this a literary evening or group therapy? — Artur asked.
— In Kyiv, those are often the same thing, — Olya said.
Vlad leaned towards Denys.
— We could film this for a Reel.
— Try it, — Marta said. — And we’ll film you being buried in the comments.
Vlad put down his phone.
— I respect the privacy of the creative process.
— No, — Denys said. — You respect the threat of physical conflict. That’s progress too.
Olya finally stood.
— I’m calling a taxi.
Artur stood as well.
— I’ll walk you out.
— To the door?
— To the taxi.
— Careful. This is starting to look like good manners.
— My mother is strict. She believes that if a man doesn’t walk a woman to her taxi, he should be sent back to the draft.
Olya smiled.
— Tell your mother she edits characters well.
They left the bar.
Podil was wet after a light rain. The stones shone under the street lamps. Near the entrance someone was smoking, someone was arguing about music, and someone was searching for a lost scarf with the drama of a person who had misplaced not a piece of fabric but part of their identity.
Olya opened the taxi app.
— Where are you headed? — Artur asked.
— Lukianivka.
— That’s almost on my way.
— “Almost” is what people say when they want intention to look accidental.
Artur looked at her.
— Are you always this precise?
— Occupational deformation. I see unnecessary words even in people’s looks.
— Do I have a lot of unnecessary ones?
— Not in the look. Plenty in the defensive jokes.
The taxi arrived three minutes later.
They sat in the back. At first they left a polite space between them — a small neutral zone where indecision, good manners and other people’s coats usually live.
The driver asked:
— Where first?
Olya gave her address.
Artur gave his.
The driver nodded and pulled away.
The car smelled of mint, wet asphalt and cheap air freshener pretending to be a forest.
For a few minutes they were silent.
Not awkwardly.
Simply as though the conversation in the bar had not ended yet, but needed a new form.
Olya broke the silence first:
— Know what’s funniest?
— What?
— You spent all evening talking about the novel as though it were your great love. But you treat it as if it’s incriminating evidence.
— Maybe love is incriminating evidence.
— Good line. Write it down.
— No. If I wrote down everything that sounded good tonight, I’d have to open a separate publishing house.
She laughed.
The taxi stopped at a traffic light. Red light washed over her face, then turned green. Artur suddenly thought there were too many signals in life, yet people still drove mostly on intuition.
— You don’t have to read it, — he said.
Olya turned towards him.
— What?
— The excerpt. If you don’t feel like it. I’ll take it fine.
— No, you won’t.
— I will.
— Artur, you’re the kind of person who says “fine” and then spends three days walking around with an internal symphony orchestra of hurt feelings.
— You’ve known me for two hours.
— People who live in text give themselves away quickly.
He smiled.
— And do editors pass judgement quickly?
— No. Good editors read first.
The taxi turned onto a wider street. Shop windows, pharmacies, closed cafés, wet trees and people under hoods flickered past. Kyiv looked as if it too had just left a bar and did not particularly feel like talking.
Olya glanced at her phone.
— Listen, do you only have the excerpt in email?
— It’s on my laptop.
— At home?
— Yes.
She was silent for a few seconds.
Then she said:
— We could stop by your place.
Artur looked at her.
The driver seemed to become more attentive too, though he professionally kept his eyes ahead.
— My place? — Artur repeated.
— Yes.
— To…?
— So I can read the first pages on a proper screen instead of on my phone in a taxi.
— Ah.
— Don’t make that face. I said “read”, not “ruin your life”.
— Sometimes they’re the same thing.
— We’ll see.
Artur did not answer at once.
Three creatures woke inside him simultaneously.
The first was a man who understood perfectly well that a beautiful woman had just suggested going to his place.
The second was an author terrified that his manuscript was about to be read aloud.
The third was an adult who ought to behave calmly but had clearly arrived late to the scene.
— I can make tea, — he said.
Olya looked at him with a faint smile.
— That is the most cautious response to an invitation to a man’s home I’ve ever heard.
— I’m versatile. I can offer tea, coffee, water, some depressing leftover chicken and a four-hundred-page psychological novel.
— Not the chicken.
— The novel?
— We’ll see whether it survives the first ten pages.
Artur leaned towards the driver.
— Could we go to my address first?
The driver nodded.
— Sure.
And said nothing else.
But his silence had the quality of a man who had already misunderstood everything and was not judging them for it.
Olya noticed and laughed softly.
— Now even the driver thinks we’re not going there to read.
— Are we going there to read?
— Artur, suspense isn’t only a tool for novels.
He looked at her.
— You’re dangerous.
— No. I’m an editor. That’s worse.
The taxi stopped outside his building.
They stepped out into the fine rain.
Artur walked Olya to the entrance. The concierge behind the glass looked up from her phone and assessed them with the instant precision available only to concierges, grandmothers outside apartment blocks and experienced customs officers.
— Good evening, — Artur said.
— Evening, — she replied, examining Olya as though a new plot had just entered the building.
They stood side by side in the lift.
Twenty-first floor.
The numbers changed slowly.
The silence became different.
Denser.
Artur thought that if anyone asked him to write the blurb for this scene now, he would fail completely.
“A man and a woman ride in a lift. They have a night, a manuscript and far too many possible genres.”
The lift chimed.
The doors opened.
— This is it, — Artur said.
— I guessed. The lift ran out.
He unlocked the flat.
Olya stepped over the threshold.
The door clicked softly shut behind her.
And if life really did love templates, at that moment it had every right to expect the oldest plot of them all.
But on the table in the room, a laptop was already waiting.
And inside it — a novel that, for the first time that night, had a chance not to remain alone.

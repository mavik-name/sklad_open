Chapter 1. Dirt on a Child’s Hands

Children did not cry in reports.

In reports they “were in difficult life circumstances,” “showed signs of neglect,” “required a safety assessment,” or “refused to provide an explanation.”

Marta was only beginning to learn that language.

At the police academy, they taught it as necessary precision. A way not to lose fact among emotion. In practice, though, the language sometimes sounded as if pain had to take off its shoes, wipe its feet, and speak in a whisper before it was allowed into a document.

The Sunflower shop huddled in an annex to a late-eighties nine-story apartment block. The building was not old in the way stone tenements with arches, stucco, and cracks were old, buildings that had already had several eras live inside them. It was simply tired. One of those buildings made for a predictable life and then left alone with people, debts, damp stairwells, and notices about utility shutoffs.

The paint had peeled in places on the facade. An ashtray by the entrance was full of cigarette butts. There was a ramp, but it was so steep you could have used it to slide sacks of potatoes down, not push a stroller up. A sign blinked above the door: two letters had already gone dark, so in the evening “Sunflower” became “Sun ower.”

Inside, it smelled of wet jackets, coffee from a machine, and chlorine from where the floor by the register had just been mopped.

Nazar sat on a plastic chair in the back corridor between the storeroom and the sales floor.

He was about eleven. Maybe twelve. Children from neighborhoods like this rarely looked their age: they either grew up too early or got stuck in an unlived childhood where the voice was still a child’s but the eyes had already learned to calculate danger faster than adults.

Two batteries lay on his knees.

He held them in both hands as if they were not batteries but something far more important. A small reserve of light. Two round promises that the dark would not be complete tonight.

His backpack stood behind the cashier, next to the register table. That was why he had not run.

Marta understood it immediately.

A child planning to run watches the door. Nazar watched the backpack.

“There he is,” the cashier said as soon as Marta and Serhii entered the back corridor. “If Mykola hadn’t caught him by the door, you’d be chasing the wind by now.”

Mykola, a broad man in a gray sweater, stood by the service entrance. He was obviously not a security guard. More likely the owner, or the owner’s husband. But he carried himself as if the shop were a fortress and Nazar an enemy unit.

Serhii Kravets took off his hat, shook the wet snow from it, and looked at the boy.

“Name?”

The boy said nothing.

Serhii was about fifteen years older than Marta. His face looked like he had once laughed a lot, then eventually realized laughter did not help write reports, did not reduce the number of calls, and did not repair other people’s families. He was not cruel. He had simply learned long ago not to lean his whole body into every disaster.

“I’m not asking so I can eat you,” he said. “Name.”

The boy looked at his boots. Then Marta’s. Then back at the batteries.

“Nazar,” he said quietly.

Marta crouched so her eyes were level with his.

At the police academy, they taught this as a technique for establishing contact with a child. In real life it was simpler and harder: you had to become something other than another adult wall.

“Nazar, my name is Marta. I’m a police lieutenant. I’m not going to shout at you. I need to understand what happened.”

The boy did not look up.

His jacket sleeves were too short. Not so short that a stranger would notice at once, but short enough to leave his wrists exposed. Dirt was black beneath his fingernails—not the kind you got from playing outside, but the kind that worked its way in when home had no time for clean towels and warm water.

And that dirt caught Marta more than anything else.

Not the theft.

Not the back room.

Not the cashier looking at the boy as if he were not a child but damaged merchandise.

The dirt on a child’s hands.

It was more honest than every adult in the room.

“He took a loaf, a chocolate bar, and batteries,” the cashier said. “Put them in his backpack and tried to walk out. I saw it and called Mykola. The loaf’s crushed now, the chocolate too.”

“Did you see him put them in?” Marta asked.

“I did.”

“You have cameras?”

“Yes. Over the register and by the exit.”

“Good. Keep the recording.”

The cashier sighed.

“I’ve got nothing against kids. But you can’t do that. My salary isn’t ministerial either.”

Marta did not answer.

The phrase “I’ve got nothing against kids” always sounded suspicious. Usually people followed it by explaining why one particular child did not deserve sympathy.

“Was the loaf for you?” Marta asked Nazar.

The boy shrugged.

“The chocolate?”

Silence.

“The batteries?”

His fingers tightened around them.

“For a flashlight,” he whispered.

“What do you need a flashlight for?”

For the first time he looked at her.

His eyes were gray. Not cold. Just tired. Children’s eyes had no right to be tired that deeply. It was wrong, like snow in June or a prayer spoken without faith.

“Because it’s dark at home.”

Serhii stopped writing.

“No electricity?” Marta asked.

Nazar nodded.

“How long?”

“Three days.”

“Who do you live with?”

The boy lowered his head again.

“My mom.”

“Anyone else?”

A pause.

Longer than it needed to be.

“Stepfather,” he said.

Marta did not ask the next question right away. Children often answered not with words, but with the exact place where their voice broke off.

“Your backpack?” she asked.

Nazar nodded.

“Flashlight in it?”

“Yes.”

“That’s why you didn’t run?”

He looked at her quickly. Frightened. As if she had pulled out a secret he had not meant to show.

“I didn’t want to steal,” he said suddenly.

The cashier snorted.

“Right. It jumped into your backpack by itself.”

Serhii turned his head toward her.

“You’re not helping.”

“I’m not a psychologist.”

“That’s obvious.”

Marta kept her eyes on the boy.

“I hear what you’re saying. But I still have to document what happened.”

“Are they taking me home?”

There was no hope in the question. Only fear.

Marta knew that tone from somewhere other than textbooks. Children used it when they were afraid not of police, but of the moment when the police would eventually leave.

“First we’re going to see what it’s like there,” she said.

Nazar squeezed the batteries until his knuckles went white.

“He’ll be angry.”

“Your stepfather?”

The boy did not answer.

Serhii closed his notebook.

“Address?”

The cashier answered:

“He lives here. Same building. Third entrance, third floor, I think. He’s from this courtyard.”

Nazar looked at her with such quiet outrage it was as if she had given away not his address, but the place where he had hidden the last part of himself.

Marta noticed.

“Nazar, we have to check your living conditions anyway. It isn’t punishment.”

He looked at her.

And did not believe her.

Children who are often punished “for their own good” stop hearing the difference between help and sentence very early.

The entrance was around the corner from the shop.

Nazar walked ahead, but not quickly. He did not run.

Serhii stayed beside him. Marta followed half a step behind, carrying his backpack. The flashlight really was inside: plastic, scratched, with a cracked battery cover wrapped in electrical tape.

In the vestibule, a list of utility debtors hung on the notice board, partly covered by a Mother’s Day drawing. Someone had once taped it directly over the names, and now a smiling paper sun shone above other people’s debts.

On the third-floor landing Nazar stopped.

“Here?” Marta asked.

He nodded toward the door on the left.

A television was audible through it. A man on some talk show spoke too loudly and too confidently, the way all television people did when they would never have to pay for their words. Then something thudded. Not a body falling. More like a bottle or a stool.

Nazar pulled his head down into his shoulders.

Marta saw it.

Serhii did too.

He knocked on the door.

Once.

Then again—harder, knuckles against metal.

The television went quiet.

A short pause followed.

Then heavy steps.

The door did not open at once. Someone looked through the peephole, assessed the uniforms, assessed Nazar behind them, and had enough time to choose which mask to put on.

A man in a stretched-out T-shirt and sweatpants stood in the doorway. His face was not drunk to the point of helplessness. It was drunk to the point of self-confidence. There was a kind of drunkenness where alcohol no longer kept you from standing, but still kept you from being ashamed of yourself.

“What happened?” he asked.

His voice was steadier than Marta expected. She understood immediately: this man knew how to sober up quickly for other people’s eyes.

“Police,” Serhii said. “What are you to the child?”

The man shifted his gaze to the boy.

There was no surprise in it.

Only anger postponed until later.

“Stepfather. What’s he done now?”

Nazar stared at the floor.

Marta felt something tighten inside her. Not pity. Pity was too soft a word. It was something else: an almost physical urge to put herself between the child and a look that was already preparing punishment even though not a finger had moved.

“There was a report from the shop,” Marta said. “We need to speak with the child’s mother and look at the living conditions.”

“His mother’s asleep.”

“Wake her.”

“She worked a shift.”

Serhii looked past him into the apartment.

“Can we come in?”

The man smiled.

“You got a warrant?”

Serhii sighed.

“Don’t start.”

“I know my rights.”

“Fine. Then we call a patrol unit, child services, draw up an inspection report, take statements from the neighbors, and decide what to do about the child’s safety. In the meantime you can remember some more rights. Your duties ought to be somewhere nearby too.”

The smile disappeared.

The man stepped away from the door.

“Come in. Just don’t take long.”

The apartment had two rooms.

On the hall cabinet lay bills, an empty cigarette pack, keys, and a child’s exercise book with bent corners. There really was no electricity.

The television in the room ran from an old car battery set on a folded newspaper by the windowsill. Wires led from it to a cheap inverter. The screen glowed as a blue rectangle, and in that light people’s faces looked lifeless.

Serhii stopped by the battery.

“Interesting.”

The stepfather tensed at once.

“What’s interesting?”

“Power’s cut, television works.”

“Old battery. Was lying around in the garage.”

“Yours?”

“What difference does it make?”

Serhii looked at him a little longer than necessary.

“So far, none.”

Marta said nothing. But she noticed Nazar look away. Quickly, almost invisibly. Children looked away like that not when they did not know the truth, but when they had already learned not to confirm it with an unnecessary movement.

On the kitchen table stood an empty pot, two cups, dried bread crusts, and a child’s plate with a crack in it. Nazar’s school shirt hung over the back of a chair. Only the collar was clean. That was how clothes were cleaned when you wanted everything to look normal from a distance.

A woman came out of the room.

Nazar’s mother was thin, with a pale face and hair gathered in a careless knot. She really did look as if she had just been woken. But it was not sleep after a shift. It was the sleep of someone hiding inside it from life.

“What happened?” she asked.

“Your son took food and batteries from a shop,” Marta said. “We need to establish the circumstances.”

The woman looked at Nazar.

Not with alarm.

With fatigue.

The way you look at one more problem when the previous ones are still open.

“Nazar…”

There was so much helplessness in that one word that the boy shrank more than he had under his stepfather’s stare.

The man laughed sharply.

“I told you. You spoiled him. Poor little thing, leave him alone, don’t touch him. Here’s the result.”

“Not in front of the child,” Marta said.

He turned toward her.

“Why not? Let him hear it. I’m making a man out of him. Otherwise he’ll grow up to be nobody.”

Marta looked straight into his eyes.

“You don’t make a man with fear.”

“With what, then? Your paperwork?”

“At least not with darkness and hunger.”

The kitchen went quiet.

Serhii turned his head toward Marta almost imperceptibly. A warning look: hold the line.

She knew.

But sometimes the line was not where the instructions drew it. Sometimes it ran between a child and an adult who was already raising a hand even if his fingers had not moved yet.

“Is there food in the house?” she asked, more dryly.

Nazar’s mother nodded.

“Yes.”

“Show me.”

The woman opened the refrigerator.

Inside was a jar of pickles, a piece of margarine, half a bottle of milk, and a bag with two eggs. In the freezer lay dumplings frozen together into one white stone.

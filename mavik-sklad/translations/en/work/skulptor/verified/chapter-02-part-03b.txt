Marta stayed.

The activity lasted forty minutes.

In those forty minutes, nothing happened that could have been written into a report.

Nobody shouted. Nobody revealed a terrible secret. Nobody was healed. Nobody said, “I feel better now.”

The children simply worked with clay.

And somehow that had more effect than talking.

The girl with the cat gave it a tail that looked more like a hook. The teenager in the hoodie spent a long time shaping a knife, but when Olena looked at him, he crushed the blade himself and turned it into a fish. The two younger children never did agree whether theirs was a mountain or a dragon, so they decided it was a dragon pretending to be a mountain.

Nazar made a square.

Then pressed a hollow into it with his finger.

Then crushed it.

Then made a square again.

Lucas did not hurry him.

Marta watched the way he worked: he almost never touched a child’s piece without permission; he did not hand out empty praise; he did not say “beautiful” when a child was waiting not for praise but for truth. When one boy showed him something shapeless and asked whether it was a dog, Lucas answered:

“If you know it’s a dog, then it isn’t lost anymore.”

The boy accepted that seriously.

Marta did not.

She did not want to accept anything from Lucas.

Chapter 4. The Guide

The guide had to fit on one page.

That was Viktoriia’s requirement.

“Volunteers won’t read three pages,” she had written in a morning message. “And even if they do, they’ll forget half. Write it so a person understands before they have time to get offended.”

Marta read the message at the kitchen table, with a cup of coffee beside her and the laptop open in front of her.

A blank page blinked on the screen.

She had already written a heading:

Rules for Working with Children in the Workshop Program

And stopped there.

Not because she did not know the rules.

The opposite.

She knew too many of them.

Do not be alone with a child. Do not touch without need and permission. Do not give personal contact information. Do not accept secrets that involve danger. Do not promise what you cannot control. Do not ask “what happened to you?” just because you are curious. Do not call a child “poor thing.” Do not ask them to smile for a photo. Do not turn someone else’s wound into proof of your own goodness.

The problem was something else.

How do you write all of that without people hearing: you are potential abusers?

And at the same time make sure those who really could do harm do not find a gap in softer wording.

Marta deleted the heading.

Wrote a new one:

A Child Is Not Obliged to Trust You

She looked at it.

Too sharp.

Deleted it.

Wrote:

Boundaries That Protect Children and Volunteers

That was better.

Drier.

Less honest.

She leaned back in the chair.

Wet snow was falling outside. Down below, the janitor pushed slush along the curb with a shovel. Cars moved slowly, as if the city had not yet decided whether it had woken up or was simply continuing yesterday.

Her phone vibrated.

Serhii:

Battery confirmed. There’s a report from the garages. The stepfather says he “found it by the dumpster.” Very talented dumpster.

Marta let out a short breath.

Marta:

Will Nazar get in trouble over the theft?

The reply did not come immediately.

Serhii:

Formally, the paperwork exists. In practice—the child is in danger, the amount is small, the circumstances are obvious. It’ll be prevention work, child services, the family. Don’t turn this into a crime novel.

Marta looked at the screen.

Marta:

We’re already in a novel. Just not a crime novel.

Serhii replied:

Don’t start.

She almost smiled.

Then returned to the guide.

The first point wrote itself:

1. A child has the right not to speak.

Marta stopped.

It was right.

And unbearably simple.

She added:

A volunteer does not pull stories out of a child, ask intrusive questions, or force a child to explain fear, silence, or refusal.

The second point:

2. Gifts are given only through the responsible staff.

The third:

3. No photos of faces, names, or personal stories without an official need and permission.

The fourth:

4. A volunteer does not remain alone with a child.

The fifth:

5. If a child speaks about danger, it is not a secret between the child and the volunteer. It is a signal for the responsible adults.

Marta read it again.

Five points.

One page.

Maybe even Viktoriia would not complain.

She was about to save the file when she remembered Lucas.

Not a specific phrase.

Not his face.

His hands.

How at the workshop he had barely touched the children’s work without permission.

How he never stayed alone with the children.

How he did not take photos.

How he did not hand out gifts directly.

How he did everything right.

To the letter.

That was exactly what bothered her.

People who behave correctly by accident usually make a mistake somewhere.

Lucas did not make mistakes.

She added a sixth point:

6. Rules do not replace attentiveness. If an adult’s behavior feels troubling, even without a formal violation, tell the responsible person.

She looked at that one longer than all the others.

Then saved the file.

The name came out simple:

Volunteer_Guide_Boundaries.docx

Marta closed the laptop.

And realized she already knew how her day would begin.

At the station, Serhii sat at his desk looking as if the battery had personally offended his professional dignity.

A folder, several printouts, and a paper cup of coffee that had long since stopped being hot lay in front of him.

“We’ve got a wonderful hero,” he said when Marta came in. “Nazar’s stepfather. Denies stealing the battery, but gives explanations as if even he doesn’t believe himself.”

“The mother?”

“Crying. Says she knew nothing.”

“Did she?”

Serhii looked up.

“Are you asking as a police officer right now, or as the person who saw her kitchen yesterday?”

“Both.”

“I think she suspected. But between ‘suspected’ and ‘can be proved’ there’s a long corridor where the truth usually gets lost.”

Marta sat down at her desk.

“Nazar?”

“At the center. Olena sent the report. No incidents overnight. Ate poorly this morning. Keeps the flashlight close. Refers to the stepfather as ‘he,’ without using his name.”

Marta turned on her computer.

“I want to check Lucas Martyn.”

Serhii stopped turning the pages.

“Who?”

“The volunteer from the center. Works with children. Was at the fund meeting yesterday.”

“Grounds?”

“Access to children.”

“That’s not grounds. That describes half the adults in your life.”

“Serhii.”

He leaned back in his chair.

“Are there documents?”

“Olena says yes. Passport, volunteer application, recommendation.”

“Then what’s wrong?”

Marta did not answer at once.

Because the answer—he talks as if he knows where a person hurts—would have sounded bad in a police station.

Even to Serhii.

“He’s too correct,” she said.

Serhii blinked slowly.

“Is that a new risk category?”

“Sometimes, for people around traumatized children.”

“Marta.”

“I’m not going anywhere I have no right to go. But I want to check the public records, compare the application, the recommendation, the address. No freelancing.”

“And if everything’s clean?”

“Then everything’s clean.”

“Can you leave a person alone if everything’s clean?”

She looked at him.

Serhii was not smiling.

The question was not a joke.

“I’m learning,” she said.

He watched her for another few seconds, then pulled the keyboard closer.

“Last name, first name.”

“Lucas Martyn.”

“Patronymic?”

“I don’t know.”

“Date of birth?”

“It’s in Olena’s application. I’ll request a copy officially for the program’s volunteer screening.”

Serhii nodded.

“Now that sounds like work. Not hunting.”

“I’m not hunting.”

“Good.”

He turned back to the papers.

“But if it turns out your Lucas is simply a strange man with normal documents, you’re not going to turn him into the devil.”

Marta froze.

Serhii did not notice immediately. Then he looked up.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“I meant it figuratively.”

“I understand.”

She lowered her eyes to the monitor.

The devil.

The word landed somewhere it was not supposed to.

It did not frighten her.

Only a brief, unpleasant feeling of recognition—not in her mind, but in her body. As if somewhere in a dark room someone had quietly turned a key in a lock.

“Marta?”

“Everything’s fine.”

“You’ve got the face you make when I accidentally say something intelligent.”

“Don’t exaggerate.”

Serhii grunted.

“Better.”

Olena answered on the third ring.

In the background, someone was laughing, someone was asking not to have the blue toy car taken away, and another voice was indignantly insisting that the soup was “too wet.”

“I’m listening,” Olena said.

“This is Marta Koval. I need a copy of Lucas Martyn’s application and the documents for his volunteer clearance. As part of preparing the guide and reviewing the program’s safety.”

“Officially?”

“Yes.”

“All right. I’ll send them to your work email. But I’ll tell you right away: everything was normal with him on our end.”

“I’m not saying anything was wrong.”

“No. You’re saying it in the voice of someone who has already managed not to say it three times.”

Marta said nothing.

Olena sighed.

“I understand you. I really do. But don’t look only at him. Look at the children’s reactions. They’re often wrong about adults because they want warmth. But a child’s body is less often wrong about danger. Around him, they don’t tighten up.”

“For now.”

“Yes. For now. That’s exactly why I’m sending you the documents.”

“How’s Nazar?”

Olena did not answer immediately.

Something fell in the background. Someone said, “I didn’t mean to!” in a voice that should make every adult in the world alert at once.

“Nazar is quiet,” Olena said. “Too quiet. But this morning he asked for paper.”

“What for?”

“He wrote a list.”

“What kind of list?”

“What he has to return.”

Marta closed her eyes.

“The batteries?”

“The batteries. The loaf of bread. The chocolate bar. The tea at the station. And he also wrote: ‘police time.’”

“Police time?”

“Yes.”

Marta did not know what to say.

Olena spoke more quietly.

“I told him police time isn’t something you return. He asked why. I said because it’s their job. He answered, ‘Then I owe the state.’”

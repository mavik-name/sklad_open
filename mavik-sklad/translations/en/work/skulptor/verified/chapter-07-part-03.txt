Sofia looked at her.

“I know. But sometimes you have to let yourself say that about your past self so you don’t say something worse.”

Marta accepted that.

Did not argue.

“He hit me,” Sofia said. “I would have fallen if not for the table. But I didn’t cry. Didn’t even scream. I remember everyone was frightened. And I felt proud. Not pain. Pride. Because he couldn’t make me be a child.”

She fell silent.

Serhii put down his pen.

“And Luka?”

“He wasn’t at the meeting.”

Marta looked up sharply.

“What?”

“He wasn’t there. Physically.”

“Yakiv didn’t say that.”

“Because Yakiv still thinks presence is only a body in the room.”

Marta felt cold.

“Explain.”

Sofia removed the napkin from the three objects on the table.

Under it were clay figures.

One was a small childlike cup with an uneven rim.

The second was sharp and thin, like a fragment of a wing or a knife.

The third was a strange shape, like a stone in which someone had left fingerprints without smoothing them away.

“Are these yours?” Marta asked.

“The first is mine from before Luka. The second—from during. The third—from ten years later.”

She pushed the second one closer.

“This is what he called an honest form.”

Marta looked at the sharp object.

It was beautiful.

That was the most unpleasant part.

“After that incident,” Sofia said, “I didn’t cry for a long time. At all. I thought I had won. Then I understood that I hadn’t defeated fear. I had simply given it every room inside me and called that order.”

Serhii sat motionless.

Marta asked:

“When did you understand?”

“When I first started leading sessions for children myself. One girl made a crooked cup and started apologizing because it was ugly. I had already opened my mouth to say something ‘strong.’ Something about form, about a crack, about how weakness can be transformed. And suddenly I heard his voice in my head.”

“Lucas’s?”

“Yes. Luka’s, back then. And I became afraid. Not for myself. For the child.”

“What did you say?”

Sofia picked up the small crooked cup.

“I said, ‘You can drink from it.’”

Marta was silent.

Sofia held the cup carefully in both hands.

“That didn’t save her. It saved me.”

Somewhere in the pipes, water moved through the workshop.

An ordinary building.

An ordinary sound.

After conversations like this, ordinary sounds felt almost like mercy.

“Do you consider Lucas dangerous to children?” Serhii asked.

Sofia set the cup on the table.

“Yes.”

“Why, if he doesn’t break the rules?”

“Because rules protect the body. Reputation. Procedure. And that matters. But there are adults who don’t break rules. They change a child’s language. And then the child starts breaking themselves with the right words.”

Marta wrote that down almost word for word.

Sofia did not object.

“What should we do?” Marta asked.

Sofia looked at her carefully.

“Don’t let him be the only one who explains children’s pain to them. Don’t try to argue more beautifully than he does. He’s better at that. Just bring children back to the simple things.”

“The simple things?”

“Are you cold? Here’s a sweater. Are you hungry? Here’s food. Are you afraid? I’m here. You made a crooked cup? You can drink from it.”

Marta felt those sentences settle inside her more evenly than all the explanations of the last several days.

“And him?” Serhii asked.

“He’s more difficult.”

“How much more?”

Sofia looked at the sharp clay form.

“I don’t know who he is. I don’t know how he does it. I don’t know why he doesn’t age the way he should. And for many years I convinced myself that memory was lying.”

She raised her eyes.

“But I know one thing: if he is beside a child, there must also be someone beside that child who loves not their strength, but their life.”

Marta did not write that down.

Because she did not want to lose the sentence in a notebook.

“Are you prepared to give an official statement?” Serhii asked.

“Yes. But not today.”

“Why?”

“Because today I’ve already given you more than I planned.”

Serhii nodded.

“All right. We won’t push.”

“You do push. Just carefully.”

“That’s a skill too.”

Sofia almost smiled.

“I’ll come tomorrow. Or the day after. I’ll write down what may be useful. No mysticism. No conclusions. Only what happened.”

“Thank you,” Marta said.

Sofia looked at her.

“Don’t thank me too soon.”

“Why?”

“Because after conversations like this, people often think they have already taken the right side.”

“And they haven’t?”

“The right side is not a place. It’s work you have to do again every time.”

Serhii said quietly:

“Everyone decided to finish us off with aphorisms today.”

“That isn’t an aphorism,” Sofia replied. “It’s an instruction.”

When they left the workshop, it was already dark outside.

Light from the display window fell across the wet sidewalk in a long rectangle. Behind the glass, Sofia covered the clay objects with the napkin. Then switched off the lamp over the table.

Marta stood beside the car and did not hurry to get in.

Serhii did not hurry either.

“Well,” he said.

“Don’t start with ‘well.’”

“Fine. Professionally, then: what do we have?”

Marta looked at the dark workshop window.

“We have confirmation that Luka Myron existed in the 2004 program. We have Sofia confirming his method and the risk. We have a similar photograph. We have the recurring theme of form. We have the fact that he may not directly harm children, but may change the way a child thinks about pain.”

“And legally, what do we do with that?”

“Almost nothing.”

“Exactly.”

“And in practice?”

Serhii opened the car.

“In practice—we talk to Olena. Limit his role. Don’t let him have a monopoly on the children. Find Nataliia Honchar. Check Lev from 2012. And go to the lecture.”

“You still want to go?”

“After Sofia? I almost do now.”

“Almost?”

“I’m a simple man. If someone with three names doesn’t age and gives lectures in libraries, I at least want to hear whether he puts the stress on words correctly.”

For the first time that evening, Marta laughed quietly.

Serhii looked at her.

“Oh. Alive.”

“Don’t exaggerate.”

“I won’t. But I’ll record it.”

She got into the car.

Her phone vibrated.

A message from Viktoriia.

Lucas left an envelope for you at the fund. Said it was materials for the lecture. I didn’t open it.

Marta read it.

Then showed Serhii.

He stared at the screen for a long time.

“Of course,” he said at last.

“What do we do?”

“We do not drive to the fund alone at night, if you were suddenly planning to suggest exactly that.”

“I was silent.”

“Your silence already has a reputation.”

“Tomorrow?”

“Tomorrow. With me there. And preferably with your mother there too.”

Marta looked at the message.

An envelope.

Lecture materials.

The questions afterward too.

She remembered Sofia’s cup.

Crooked.

Small.

One you could drink from.

“Serhii.”

“What?”

“A child is not a stone.”

He started the engine.

“We already wrote that down.”

“No. Not as a phrase.”

She looked out the window.

“As a rule.”

Serhii pulled onto the road.

“Then add it to the guide tomorrow.”

Marta did not answer.

Because she knew he had said it as a joke.

But she really would add it.

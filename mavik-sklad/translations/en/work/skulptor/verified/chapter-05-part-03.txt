“Who told him that?” he asked quietly.

“I don’t know.”

Marta looked at Yakiv.

He was sitting over the sheet, writing down his old guilt in neat letters.

“But now,” she said, “I want to know.”

Serhii handed the phone back to her.

“After the library, we’re going to the center.”

“Yes.”

“And we talk to Olena. Not Lucas.”

Marta looked at him.

“Why?”

“Because if you go straight to him, he’ll tell you something after which you’ll either hit him or start doubting yourself. Both are bad options for an official conversation.”

She did not argue.

Because that was true too.

Yakiv signed the statement and pushed the sheet away.

“I wrote everything I can confirm.”

Serhii read through the text.

“Good.”

“Will that be enough?”

“For what exactly?”

Yakiv smiled sadly.

“For you not to believe me too quickly.”

Serhii looked at him carefully.

“That is the most sensible thing you’ve said so far.”

Yakiv took his photograph and put it back into the folder.

The movement was careful. Like someone hiding not a piece of paper, but a piece of life that had lain out in the open for too long.

“He already knows I spoke to you,” Yakiv said.

Marta tensed.

“Why do you think that?”

“Because back then, in 2012, he knew too.”

“That’s not an answer.”

“No. It’s a warning.”

Serhii stood.

“If anyone threatens you, you call immediately. No nighttime riddles. Directly.”

Yakiv nodded.

“He doesn’t threaten.”

“All the worse for our bureaucracy.”

Marta put the copies of the photographs into the folder.

“You said, ‘don’t take him with you.’ Why?”

Yakiv looked at her.

“Because around him, people begin to speak the most precise version of the truth. And precise truth is not always useful when you are not yet ready to bear it.”

“You couldn’t bear it?”

He did not take offense.

“No.”

“And what happened?”

Yakiv picked up his folder.

“I began to believe that if a child stopped crying, it meant the child was better.”

He buttoned his coat.

“That was my mistake. Don’t repeat it.”

He left slowly, without looking back.

Marta watched him until the reading-room door closed behind him.

Serhii came to stand beside her.

“Well?”

“Well what?”

“Your version.”

Marta looked at the photograph on her phone.

Luka Myron.

November 2004.

A face that had no right to look so similar.

“There is no version yet,” she said. “There is a photograph, an old statement, a child named Sofia whom we need to try to find, and Nazar, who thinks he has to be good to see his mother.”

“Now that is work.”

“And Lucas?”

Serhii put his phone into his pocket.

“Lucas, for now, is a man with normal documents and an abnormal number of coincidences.”

“Don’t you think the word ‘man’ is premature here?”

He looked at her for a long moment.

“I think that is exactly the word we need to hold on to until the very end.”

“Why?”

“Because if you’re wrong and call a person a monster, you break a person. And if you’re wrong and call a monster a person, it gets closer.”

Marta was silent.

“So don’t rush,” Serhii said. “Not in either direction.”

They left the library.

On the stairs, Marta stopped.

Below, near the exit, a poster hung on the notice board.

She would not have paid attention if not for one word.

Form.

Marta went down a few more steps.

The poster read:

Open lecture: “Form and Freedom. How Art Helps Us Live Through Trauma.”

Date: next week.

Location: this same library.

Presenter:

Lucas Martyn.

Serhii came to stand beside her.

For several seconds, they both stared at the poster.

“Coincidence?” Marta asked.

Serhii exhaled slowly.

“I really want to say yes.”

“But?”

“But now I don’t trust myself either.”

Marta took out her phone and photographed the poster.

Not as evidence.

For now, as a reminder.

Lucas was not pictured in the photo.

Only text.

And still, it seemed to her that he was looking straight through it.

She put the phone away.

“We’re going to the center.”

Serhii nodded.

“We’re going.”

Outside, it was wet and bright.

An ordinary day.

An ordinary city.

An ordinary library with a poster on the board.

And a man who in 2004 had called himself Luka Myron, in 2012—Lev, and now had documents in the name of Lucas Martyn.

Marta walked toward the car thinking of only one thing:

a child is not a stone.

And if she had to, she would say it to his face.

Serhii, sitting at the next desk, looked up.

Marta realized he had heard.

“What did you tell him?”

“That the state will somehow survive. Then I gave him breakfast.”

“Did he eat?”

“Half.”

“Is that good?”

“For today—yes.”

Marta opened her eyes.

“Thank you.”

“I’ll send the documents within the hour.”

“All right.”

She ended the call.

Serhii was silent.

That was unusual.

“What?” Marta asked.

“Nothing.”

“Say it.”

He rubbed a hand over his face.

“I just thought that sometimes we spend so long explaining duties to children that they start blaming themselves even for the fact that we showed up to do our jobs.”

Marta looked at him.

“That’s almost philosophy.”

“Don’t get used to it. Slipped out by accident.”

He went back to the papers.

But his face had grown heavier.

The documents arrived forty-seven minutes later.

Marta opened the email in her work inbox.

Lucas Andriiovych Martyn.

Date of birth.

Passport.

Registered address.

Phone number.

Certificate.

Volunteer application.

Letter of recommendation from the Warm Hands charity program.

The name of the program struck her as unfortunate.

Too soft for paperwork.

She opened the letter of recommendation.

The text was standard: participated, conducted workshops, followed the rules, received no complaints, recommended for work under the supervision of responsible staff.

Signature.

Stamp.

Date.

Everything looked normal.

Too normal—but Marta had already heard from Serhii that this was not a risk category.

She checked the address.

An ordinary building in the old part of the city.

Not upscale.

Not suspicious.

Just an address.

She checked the phone number.

Active.

The volunteer program really existed. The website was simple, with several reports, photographs of hands, materials, tables, children’s work. No faces. Either Lucas followed the rules there too, or he was one of the people who had set them.

She opened an older report.

The photo showed a table with clay.

An adult’s hands showing a child how to make a bowl.

No face.

Only hands.

Marta enlarged the image.

Long fingers.

Calm.

Lucas’s.

The caption under the photo read:

“Form Workshop. Facilitator — L. Martyn.”

Marta felt something tighten inside her again.

Not because of the photo.

Because of the word.

Form.

It followed him like a shadow.

Or perhaps she had started seeing shadows where there were only words.

Serhii came over with a cup of coffee.

“Well?”

“Everything’s clean.”

“Listen to the way you say ‘everything’s clean.’”

“How do I say it?”

“Like, ‘Too bad everything’s clean.’”

Marta closed the tab.

“He works with the idea of form.”

“Is that a crime?”

“No.”

“Then we breathe.”

“Serhii, you haven’t seen him.”

“I’ve seen plenty of people I wish I hadn’t. Believe me, if a person is strange, that doesn’t automatically make them dangerous.”

“And if it does?”

“Then sooner or later they’ll make a mistake. Everyone does.”

“Not everyone.”

He looked at her more closely.

“Now that is a dangerous thought.”

“Why?”

“Because if you decide someone doesn’t make mistakes, you’ll either start fearing them or worshipping them. Both are bad options.”

Marta was about to answer when the duty officer came into the room.

“Kravets, someone’s here about the battery. Says he may be able to identify it.”

Serhii set down his cup.

“Back to work.”

He left.

Marta remained in front of the open document.

Lucas Andriiovych Martyn.

An ordinary name.

An ordinary file.

An ordinary certificate.

And still it felt as though she were looking not at an answer, but at a very well-made door.

No cracks.

No lock on the outside.

But a door.

She printed the guide before lunch.

One page.

Six points.

No accusations.

No moralizing.

Almost no fear.

Marta read it aloud to the empty office:

“A child has the right not to speak. A volunteer does not pull stories out of a child or ask questions that satisfy an adult’s curiosity but do nothing to help the child...”

She stopped.

The sentence was too long.

Deleted it.

Wrote it shorter:

Don’t interrogate. If a child wants to talk—listen. If they stay silent—respect it.

That was better.

Second point:

Gifts only through the responsible person. A child should not feel a personal debt to a volunteer.

Third:

Do not photograph faces. Do not publish names. Do not tell someone else’s story in the child’s place.

Fourth:

Do not stay alone with a child. Safety matters more than convenience.

Fifth:

If a child speaks about danger, tell the responsible adult. Do not promise to keep that kind of secret.

Sixth:

Trust not only the rules, but your attention. If something in an adult’s behavior troubles you—say so.

At the bottom she added:

Help must not require gratitude from a child.

That one she kept.

Even if Viktoriia said it sounded harsh.

She sent the file to her mother.

A minute later, the reply came:

Good. Very good. I’d only put the last line in bold.

Marta looked at the phone.

And for the first time that day, she smiled without resisting it.

After lunch, she drove to the center.

Not because of Lucas.

At least that was what she told herself.

Olena had asked her to bring a few copies of the guide because new volunteers from the fund were coming that evening. Formally, she could have sent the file. But Olena had written:

If you’re going to be nearby anyway, bring printed copies. And you can see how Nazar is doing.

Marta was not nearby.

She simply went.

The center looked less unsettling in daylight. In the yard, two children were kicking around a wet ball. A staff member sat on a bench holding a paper cup.

The metal rocket on the playground shone with water.

Olena met her in the lobby.

“Got them?”

Marta held out the folder.

“Ten copies.”

“Oh, you took this seriously.”

“I always take things seriously.”

“That’s exactly what scares me sometimes.”

Olena looked over the first page.

“Good. Plain language. No bureaucratic sludge. The last line is strong.”

“My mother said the same thing.”

“So I’m not the only smart one.”

Marta looked around.

“Where’s Nazar?”

“In the room. Drawing debts.”

“What?”

Olena nodded toward the corridor.

“Better see for yourself.”

Nazar sat at a small table by the window. A sheet of graph paper lay in front of him. The words were written in a column:

batteries
bread
chocolate
tea
police time
flashlight

The last word was crossed out.

Marta sat opposite him.

“Hi.”

Nazar looked up.

“Hi.”

“What’s this?”

“A list.”

“I can see that.”

“Things I have to return.”

“You crossed out the flashlight?”

“They gave it through the fund. Olena said it isn’t a debt.”

“She was right.”

“But the batteries are still there.”

“The ones you took from the store, yes. The ones in the flashlight are help.”

Nazar frowned.

“They’re the same.”

“No.”

“Why?”

Marta paused.

The question was simple.

The answer was not.

Because for adults the difference was procedural: one set of batteries was evidence, the other was charitable aid processed through the fund.

For Nazar, the difference was something else: one set had been taken without permission, the other received—but still from adults.

“Because one set you took yourself when you didn’t know what else to do,” she said. “And the other was given to you by people who wanted to help. Not in exchange. Not for anything. Just because a child shouldn’t have to sit in the dark.”

He looked at her seriously.

“What if I still want to pay it back?”

“Then someday you’ll help someone else. Not me. Not the fund. Someone who’s in the dark.”

Nazar thought about it.

“Does that count?”

“I think so.”

“Who does the counting?”

Marta did not get the chance to answer.

A voice came from the doorway:

“Not everything important needs to be counted.”

Lucas stood in the corridor.

No coat. Dark sweater. A box of paper in his hands.

Marta slowly turned her head.

“You’re here today too?”

“Today is the new-volunteer day. They need materials.”

Olena, standing by the door, said calmly:

“I called him. Don’t look at him as if he crawled out of the ventilation system.”

Nazar looked at Lucas.

“If you don’t count, how do you know you paid it back?”

Lucas did not enter the room. He stayed by the threshold.

Good.

He remembered the boundaries.

“Sometimes you know not when the debt is gone,” he said, “but when you stop carrying it in place of your heart.”

Nazar did not understand.

Marta did.

And became angry again.

“Too complicated for a child,” she said.

Lucas nodded.

“Yes.”

He looked at Nazar.

“Simpler: if you make things a little brighter for someone, that already gives something back.”

The boy lowered his eyes to the list.

Then crossed out the word tea.

“Tea doesn’t count,” he said. “I didn’t ask for it.”

Olena let out a quiet breath.

Marta did not move.

Nazar picked up the pencil and put a question mark beside police time.

“I’ll ask Serhii about this later.”

“He’ll say it’s expensive,” Marta said.

Nazar looked up, frightened.

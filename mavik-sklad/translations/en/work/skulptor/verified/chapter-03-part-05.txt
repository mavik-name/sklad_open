After the meeting, no one left right away.

That was how it always was at church: the official part ended, but people kept standing around the tables, adding notes, moving boxes, arranging keys, remembering that someone still owed someone a charger, a thermos, a list, or somebody else’s cooking pot.

Petro went off to call a donor.

Kateryna was gathering the sticky notes into a small bag.

Andrii and Lucas stood by the flip chart.

Marta watched them from the other end of the hall.

Her father spoke calmly, leaning slightly forward. That was how he spoke to people who interested him. Not everyone. Only those in whom he sensed a wound, or depth, or the makings of a future sermon.

Lucas listened.

This time Marta could not hear the words.

And that was worse.

She picked up a few sheets from the table to give herself a reason to move closer. A stupid, transparent trick. But adults sometimes behave like children too. They just call it controlling the situation.

“...not everyone who comes to church is looking for God,” Andrii was saying.

“Yes,” Lucas replied. “Some people are looking for a place where they can sit down without explaining why they’re tired.”

“Is that what you were looking for?”

“No.”

“Then what?”

Lucas noticed Marta.

He did not turn his eyes toward her at once. He let her know he had noticed without exposing her.

“Materials for the workshop,” he said.

Andrii smiled.

“Evasive answer.”

“Your daughter already said something similar.”

“My daughter is rarely wrong when something puts her on guard.”

Marta froze.

She had not expected that.

Not from her father.

Andrii looked at her.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“You thought I was about to say we should trust everyone?”

“More or less.”

“I’m a pastor, not a decorative cushion.”

Lucas lowered his eyes.

Almost smiled.

For the first time that evening, Marta felt that she was not standing against both of them, but beside her father. It lasted a second. But sometimes a second is enough for the heart to draw the wrong conclusion.

“I don’t trust you,” she said to Lucas.

Viktoriia, passing by with a folder, stopped.

“Marta.”

“That’s normal,” Lucas said.

“For whom?”

“For anyone who works with children.”

“Good answer. Very convenient.”

“Sometimes a convenient answer is true too.”

Andrii watched both of them closely.

“You met yesterday?”

“Yes,” Marta said.

“No,” Lucas said.

They answered at the same time.

And fell silent.

Viktoriia slowly set the folder on the table.

“Interesting.”

Marta turned to Lucas.

“What do you mean, no?”

“Knowing someone doesn’t begin when two people exchange names,” he said. “It begins when one of them starts affecting the other’s decisions.”

“Then we definitely don’t know each other.”

“You came to the center today with a flashlight.”

“Because of Nazar.”

“Of course.”

He did not argue.

Again, that was the worst part.

Andrii looked at Marta.

“Did I miss something?”

“No,” she said.

“Yes,” Viktoriia said.

Marta looked at her mother.

Viktoriia shrugged.

Chapter 6. Not a Stone

They drove to the center in silence.

Serhii was driving. Not fast, but not slowly either. The way people drive when they know the city, the weather, the potholes, and their own irritation well. The wipers barely kept up clearing the water from the windshield. At traffic lights, cars stood close together with dimmed headlights, as if each one were dragging yesterday behind it.

Marta sat beside him and looked at the photograph on her phone.

November 2004.

Yakiv. Luka. Group 3.

The man in the picture stood sideways. Not clearly enough to go up to him, put the photograph on the table, and say, “Explain.” But clearly enough that she could not get it out of her head.

Serhii glanced at her from the corner of his eye.

“Stop drilling into the photo. Your stare won’t make it sharper.”

Marta locked the screen.

“He looks the same.”

“Almost the same.”

“Don’t nitpick.”

“That’s exactly what I’m doing. Nitpicking. Because sometimes everything fits between ‘the same’ and ‘almost the same’: a brother, a father, a son, poor photo quality, a wrong date, Yakiv’s old guilt, and your sleep-deprived head.”

“You saw the poster.”

“I did.”

“Luka Myron in 2004. Lev in 2012. Lucas Martyn now. All of them with clay, form, trauma, and children.”

“So far that isn’t a chain. It’s a set of similar nails. First we need a board.”

“Nice metaphor.”

“Don’t get used to it. I accidentally read a library today.”

Marta did not smile.

Serhii turned onto the street where the center stood.

“Once we’re there, we talk to Olena. Not Nazar right away. Not Lucas right away. And we don’t slam the photograph on the table shouting, ‘Aha!’”

“I don’t do that.”

“That’s what you think. Your face doesn’t always agree.”

She looked at him.

“I want to understand who told Nazar he has to be good.”

“That’s the right question.”

“And if it was Lucas?”

“Then we’ll deal with it.”

“And if it wasn’t?”

Serhii parked beside the center and turned off the engine.

“Then you admit that not every dark phrase in this city belongs to your Lucas.”

“He’s not mine.”

“All the more.”

They got out of the car.

The center yard was wet. The snow by the fence had settled, darkened, and turned into clumps. The metal rocket on the playground stood motionless, as if it had been launched long ago but changed its mind about flying.

Lights were on in the ground-floor windows.

Ordinary lights.

Electric.

Not a symbol.

Just lamps in a children’s center where adults were trying to keep up with being adults.

Olena met them in the corridor.

She immediately looked at Marta more closely than usual.

“Did something happen?”

“We need to talk,” Serhii said. “Without the children. And without the volunteers.”

Olena nodded.

“In the office.”

She led them along the same route, past the schedule, the rules on the wall, and the workshop door. From behind it came the steady noise of children: not cheerful, not anxious, simply alive. Someone was arguing about whether a sun could be black. Someone else answered that if it was a nighttime sun, it could.

Marta slowed by half a step.

Serhii said quietly:

“Not now.”

She kept walking.

In the office, Olena closed the door.

“Tell me.”

Serhii put the folder on the table.

“A man came to us today. Yakiv Levytskyi. Says he knew a person who looked very much like Lucas Martyn in 2004. Under another name. He gave a statement. Showed us an old photograph.”

Olena did not interrupt.

Marta took out her phone and opened the photograph of the photograph.

Olena took the phone in both hands. Looked.

For a long time.

Then zoomed in.

“He looks similar,” she said.

“How similar?” Marta asked.

“Enough that I don’t like it. Not enough for me to say it’s him.”

Serhii gave an almost imperceptible nod.

“Good answer.”

Olena handed the phone back.

“What exactly did this Yakiv tell you?”

“That there was a child in that program, Sofia,” Marta said. “After the workshops with Luka, she became calm. Too calm. Then, in a dangerous situation, she stopped protecting herself because she decided fear was something unnecessary that had to be removed.”

Olena sat down.

Her face did not change sharply. She did not gasp or grab her head. She simply became even more attentive.

“Is that a serious accusation?”

“It isn’t an accusation,” Serhii said. “It’s the statement of a man who has carried guilt inside him for twenty years. We don’t know where the fact is, where the memory is, and where the interpretation is.”

“But you came.”

“Because you have children here. And Lucas has access to them.”

Olena slowly ran a finger along the edge of the table.

“What do you want from me?”

“First,” Marta said, “not to panic yet. Second, pull every document you have on Lucas. Not just copies. Who brought him in, who recommended him, who checked him, who saw the originals. Third, review whether there were changes in children’s behavior after his sessions that didn’t fit normal dynamics.”

“That sounds like an internal review.”

“Yes.”

“Without telling him?”

Marta wanted to answer immediately.

Serhii beat her to it:

“For now, yes. If we go to him now with a twenty-year-old photograph, he’ll either give us a simple explanation, close up, or say something after which we lose control of the conversation. Facts first.”

Olena looked at Marta.

“Do you agree?”

“Yes.”

It was not entirely true.

But close enough.

Olena stood, went to the cabinet, and took out a blue folder.

“Volunteers are in here. The full files are kept by the administrator, but I have copies of the essentials.”

She found the right file.

“Lucas Martyn. The administrator saw the passport. There’s a copy. Certificate. Recommendation from Warm Hands. First contact was through Viktoriia Andriivna—through your mother, that is.”

Marta tensed.

“Through my mother?”

“Not exactly. The fund was delivering materials, and at the time he was helping with a workshop at another organization. Then Olena... I mean, I,” she gave a tired smile, “invited him for a trial session. Afterward the children asked him to come again.”

“The children?”

“Yes. Not all of them. But several.”

“Who exactly?”

Olena opened the session log.

“Artem. Liza. Denys. Then Mariika. Nazar came later, after he was placed here.”

“Artem—the one who made the knife?”

“He crushed it into a fish himself.”

“After Lucas said that line.”

“Yes.”

Marta sat opposite her.

“Haven’t you seen children become... less alive after him?”

Olena did not answer immediately.

Marta did not like the pause.

“Olena.”

“I’m thinking.”

“That’s already an answer.”

“No. It’s an attempt not to lie quickly.”

Serhii leaned a shoulder against the cabinet.

“Think out loud.”

Olena flipped back through the log.

“After sessions with him, some children really do become calmer. But in our work that’s usually a plus. Because they don’t fight, don’t tear the room apart, don’t collapse into hysterics.”

“And ‘too calm’?”

“There was one case with Artem.”

Marta looked at her.

“What kind?”

“He stopped reacting to provocation. Other teenagers would needle him, and he would just stare. We were glad because before that he would lunge at them. Then I saw him scratching his arm with a paper clip under the table.”

“When was that?”

“A month ago.”

“After a session with Lucas?”

“After several.”

“Did you tell Lucas?”

“Yes.”

“And what did he say?”

Olena closed the log.

“That sometimes a child stops hitting other people not because he learned not to be angry, but because he found somewhere to hide the anger. And that it’s dangerous.”

Marta fell silent.

That did not fit the pattern she needed.

Lucas had not pushed Artem toward numbness. He had seen the problem.

“What happened next?” Serhii asked.

“We brought in the psychologist. Artem started talking. Not much. But he started. Lucas worked with him more carefully after that.”

“More carefully how?”

“More movement, less talking. Less ‘what does this mean?’ and more ‘make it with your hands and put it aside.’ I thought then that he had sensed the boundary very well.”

Marta looked away.

Because once again, it was inconvenient.

Not every fact went against Lucas.

Some went in his favor.

And that was exactly why everything was getting worse.

“And Nazar?” she asked.

Olena understood immediately.

“Because of the message?”

“‘I’m not ready to be good yet.’ Where did that come from?”

Olena opened another folder.

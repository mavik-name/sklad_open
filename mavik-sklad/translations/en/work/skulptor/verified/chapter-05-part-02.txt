“It could,” Yakiv agreed.

“Or the date could be wrong.”

“It could.”

“Or this photograph might not be yours.”

“It could.”

Marta looked at Yakiv.

“You agree very easily.”

“Because you have to ask those questions. And they’re the right ones.”

Serhii returned the photograph.

“What happened in that program?”

Yakiv was silent for a long time.

Not theatrically.

He seemed to be deciding where to begin so he would not lie through unnecessary explanation.

“Nothing you could file a complaint about,” he said at last. “That was the problem.”

Marta sat opposite him.

“Be specific.”

“Luka didn’t hit the children. Didn’t touch them inappropriately. Didn’t stay alone with them. Didn’t ask for money. Didn’t take photographs. He followed the rules better than any of us.”

Serhii gave Marta a brief look.

She did not react.

“Then where was the danger?” she asked.

Yakiv placed his hands on the table.

“He knew how to make a child start believing that their pain had a form. And if it had a form, then something could be carved out of it.”

Marta felt the word form rise between them again.

“That doesn’t necessarily sound bad,” Serhii said.

“I thought so too.”

“And then?”

Yakiv looked at the photograph.

“Then the children started saying things that were too adult for them. Not memorized. Not borrowed. Their own. But as if someone had accelerated the aging inside them.”

Marta remembered Nazar.

I owe the state.

But that had not come from Lucas.

Not everything could be put on him.

That was what made it harder.

“Give me an example,” Marta said.

“There was a girl. Sofia. Fourteen. Her father was dead, her mother drank, and her mother’s partner beat both of them. The girl ended up at the center after another call. After the workshops with Luka, she became calmer. Very calm. We were pleased. Thought the therapy was working.”

“And in fact?”

“In fact, she decided her fear was an unnecessary piece that had to be cut away.”

Serhii frowned.

“What does that mean?”

“She went to a supervised meeting with her mother. The partner came too, though he wasn’t supposed to. He started shouting. Before, Sofia would hide. That time she just stood there and looked at him. Then she said, ‘You can’t break me because I already removed everything soft myself.’”

Marta felt an unpleasant chill.

“And?”

“He hit her.”

“Hard?”

“Yes.”

Yakiv closed his eyes for a moment.

“She didn’t scream. Didn’t cry. Didn’t run. Just stood there. Then she said to her mother, ‘See? It doesn’t hurt anymore.’”

Someone in the reading room turned a page.

The sound was small.

But Marta heard it far too clearly.

“That isn’t evidence against Luka,” Serhii said.

“I know.”

“The child could have come to that wording herself.”

“She could have.”

“She could have been broken before him.”

“She was broken before him,” Yakiv agreed. “He simply showed her how to call the fragments strength.”

Marta did not take her eyes off him.

“Did you talk to him?”

“Yes.”

“And what did he say?”

Yakiv gave a bitter smile.

“That fear does not always need to be healed. Sometimes it needs to be taught to serve.”

Marta clenched her fingers.

It sounded like Lucas.

Too much like him.

“Is Sofia alive?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“Where is she?”

“I don’t know. After that incident, the program was shut down. Luka disappeared. His documents were in order. There were no complaints. Formally, the man was guilty, child services were guilty, the mother was guilty, I was guilty, everyone was guilty. Just not Luka. He hadn’t broken a single rule.”

Serhii was looking at the photograph.

“You said his documents were in order. In the name Luka Myron?”

“Yes.”

“Do you have copies?”

Yakiv shook his head.

“No. Back then I didn’t think I would need them.”

“And now?”

“Now I’ve spent twenty years thinking I should have kept something. Anything.”

Marta exhaled slowly.

“Why were you silent for so many years?”

Yakiv looked at her.

“Because everything I could say sounded exactly like what you’re thinking right now.”

“And what do you think I’m thinking?”

“That an old man found an old photograph, noticed a similar-looking volunteer, and built guilt around it that he never managed to deal with.”

Marta said nothing.

Because that was exactly what she was thinking.

At least partly.

Yakiv nodded.

“I would think the same.”

“Then why call now?”

“Because I saw your guide.”

“The guide?”

“The last line.”

Marta remembered:

Help must not require gratitude from a child.

“And?”

“The person who wrote that will at least understand the question.”

“What question?”

Yakiv leaned closer.

Not too close.

Serhii tensed immediately, and Yakiv noticed. Stopped.

“Why do some children become stronger after meeting him, but less alive?”

Marta felt the sentence settle inside her, heavy and exact.

Stronger.

But less alive.

She remembered Lucas beside the clay step.

Then there should be someone nearby who won’t laugh.

That was not evil.

But goodness did not always have a clean form either.

“Did you see him after 2004?” Serhii asked.

“Once.”

“When?”

“In 2012. Another city. Another program. Another name.”

“What name?”

“Lev.”

Serhii gave Marta a brief look.

She sat motionless.

“Last name?”

“I don’t remember. Or I don’t want to remember it wrong. It was from a distance. I saw him on a stage. A workshop for teenagers. He was talking about how a crack is the place where material shows the truth about itself.”

“And you didn’t go up to him?”

“No.”

“Why?”

Yakiv lowered his eyes.

“Because I was afraid he would recognize me.”

“And did he?”

“He did.”

Marta felt the air between them grow denser.

“How do you know?”

“He didn’t stop the talk. Didn’t change his voice.”

“He just looked out into the room and said, ‘A witness is material too. Just the kind that thinks it’s standing aside.’”

Serhii slowly set the photograph on the table.

“That already sounds beautiful. And beautiful things are hard to verify.”

“That’s exactly why I came with a photograph, not only words.”

Marta took the photograph.

This time she really took it.

The paper was cool.

Someone else’s November of 2004 lay between her fingers and gave no final answer.

Only the wrong face in the wrong time.

“Can we take this?” she asked.

Yakiv tensed.

“It’s the only thing I have left.”

Serhii answered for her:

“We won’t take the original.”

“We’ll make a copy, photograph both sides, and record that you voluntarily provided it for review. The original stays with you.”

Yakiv did not relax completely.

But a little.

“All right.”

“And you’ll write a statement,” Serhii added.

“Officially?”

“You called us.”

Yakiv nodded.

“I’ll write it.”

“Without ‘he doesn’t age,’” Serhii said. “Facts only: where you worked, when, under what name, what happened, who you can identify.”

“And if the facts don’t explain the main thing?”

“Welcome to our job,” Serhii said.

Marta carefully photographed the picture.

Front.

Back.

Then the front again, closer.

Luka Myron’s face still remained not quite clear enough.

Similar enough to keep you awake.

Not enough to accuse.

That was the worst kind of evidence.

It gives you not an answer, but hunger.

“What do you want from me?” Marta asked.

Yakiv looked at her for a long time.

“Don’t let him be the only adult who names a child’s pain for them.”

“He isn’t the only one.”

“Not yet.”

“You talk as if he takes something away.”

“No,” Yakiv said. “He doesn’t take. He purifies. That’s the horror of it.”

Marta froze.

“Explain.”

“To take pain away is understandable. To heal it is too. But he seems to take everything unnecessary: fear, shame, weakness, the need for protection. And the child comes out of it cleaner. Stronger. Straighter.”

“You’re describing help.”

“I’m describing stone.”

The word landed on the table heavier than the photograph.

Marta felt something answer inside her.

Not a thought.

A premonition.

“A child is not stone,” she said.

Yakiv looked at her as if that were the exact sentence he had been waiting for.

“Tell him that.”

Yakiv wrote the statement slowly.

Serhii sat beside him and did not hurry him. Every so often he only clarified a date, the name of the center, Sofia’s name, positions, surnames Yakiv remembered or did not remember.

Marta stood by the window.

She could see the city from above: wet pavement, people with umbrellas, a bus stopped at a traffic light, a woman with a red bag crossing the road where she should not.

The world below looked ordinary.

It was almost offensive.

When something inside begins to crack, you want something outside to happen too. The sky to darken. A lamp to flicker. People to stop.

But no.

The world always had more composure than a person.

The phone in her pocket vibrated.

Marta took it out.

A message from Olena:

Nazar refused to meet with his mother today. He said: “I’m not ready to be good yet.”

Marta reread it.

Once.

Then again.

Then she showed it to Serhii.

He read it, and his face grew heavy.

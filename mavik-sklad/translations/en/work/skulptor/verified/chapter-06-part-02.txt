“I spoke to him after his mother asked for a meeting. I said, ‘Your mom wants to see you. You can refuse.’ He asked whether she would be alone. I said the meeting would be supervised. He was silent for a long time. Then he said, ‘I’m not ready to be good yet.’”

“Could that have been something Lucas said?”

“No.”

Marta looked up.

“Why are you so sure?”

“Because I asked. Not directly, ‘Did Lucas tell you that?’ I asked normally. He explained.”

“What exactly?”

Olena looked at Serhii, then at Marta.

“At home his mother often told him, ‘Be good, don’t make him angry.’ And if the stepfather was already angry, she would say, ‘You didn’t try hard enough to be good today.’”

The office went quiet.

Not dramatically.

Empty.

Marta felt something snap inside her—not hard, not with a sound, but like a thread that had been holding more than it could for a long time.

“So he thinks meeting his mother is a test?” Serhii asked.

“Yes. And that if he isn’t good enough, she’ll be punished again or he’ll be sent back to where he has to try harder.”

Marta stood and went to the window.

Someone was rolling a ball across the yard. The ball kept sinking into the wet snow and refused to bounce.

“It wasn’t Lucas,” Olena said quietly.

Marta did not turn around.

“I know.”

“But you wanted it to be him.”

She turned her head sharply.

Olena did not look away.

“Didn’t you?”

Marta wanted to deny it.

Couldn’t.

Serhii was silent.

Which made it worse.

“Yes,” Marta said at last.

The word came out dry.

“Because then it would have been simpler. There would have been an adult who said the wrong thing. We would set a boundary. Remove access. Explain. Correct it.”

Olena nodded.

“And now?”

“And now it’s his home. His mother. His way of surviving.”

She looked back out into the yard.

“And you can’t remove that from the program by order.”

Serhii finally spoke:

“That is the work. Not the kind where there’s one guilty person. The kind where there are too many guilty people, and no single surname closes the case.”

Marta did not answer.

Because right now he was not irritating her.

Right now he was right.

Lucas was not in the workshop.

That surprised Marta.

She still had not decided what exactly she felt about it: relief or disappointment.

The children and the new volunteers sat at the tables. Kateryna was helping a girl with thread. Petro held a clay cup in his hands and listened to a boy of about ten explain that “it’s crooked like that because it’s for the left hand.”

Artem sat apart, but not isolated. Three pieces of clay lay in front of him. He had flattened one, rolled the second into a ball, and left the third untouched.

Nazar was by the window.

Not alone. A younger boy sat beside him, silently watching Nazar shape something.

Marta did not go over immediately.

First she stopped by the door.

Olena came to stand beside her.

“Lucas isn’t here today?”

“No. He was here in the morning, brought materials, but Kateryna is leading the session today. I asked her to.”

“Because of us?”

“No. Before your call. I don’t want the children getting attached to one facilitator. Your guide reminded me that regularity shouldn’t turn into dependence on one particular person.”

Marta nodded slowly.

“Good.”

“Yes. It is good.”

They watched the children for several seconds.

“Can I talk to Nazar?” Marta asked.

“Briefly. And not about his mother unless he brings it up himself.”

“I know.”

“I’m not only telling you. I’m reminding myself too.”

Marta went to the table.

“Hi.”

Nazar looked up.

“Hi.”

In front of him was a clay step.

Not one.

Three.

Crooked, different heights, placed against one another so they almost formed a tiny staircase.

“You kept going,” Marta said.

“Yes.”

The younger boy beside him explained for him:

“It’s for getting out, but not all at once. Because if you do it all at once, you can fall.”

Nazar nudged him with his elbow.

“I can say it myself.”

“Then say it.”

“I already did.”

Marta almost smiled.

Not because of the joke.

Because Nazar had elbowed someone like an ordinary child.

Not out of fear.

Not in self-defense.

Just because another boy beside him had butted into an answer that was not his.

“Can I sit down?” she asked.

Nazar nodded.

She sat opposite him.

“Olena said you don’t want to see your mom yet.”

His eyes dropped immediately.

Marta did not rush him.

“That’s all right,” she said. “You’re allowed not to want to.”

“Was she crying?”

“I don’t know.”

“If she was, it’s because of me.”

Marta felt how hard it was to keep her face calm.

“No.”

“You don’t know.”

“I know enough to say this: adults cry because of their own decisions. A child is not responsible for an adult’s tears.”

Nazar ran a finger along the edge of the step.

“She says I have to be good.”

“What does that mean?”

He shrugged.

“Not make him angry.”

“Who?”

He was silent.

The younger boy stopped touching the clay.

“Nazar,” Marta said quietly, “being good does not mean making sure adults don’t get angry.”

“Then what does it mean?”

She opened her mouth.

And found no answer that would not sound like a poster in a children’s center.

“Be kind.”

“Be honest.”

“Don’t hurt others.”

All of it was right.

And none of it answered a boy who stole batteries because his home was dark.

“Can I think about it?” she said.

Nazar looked up in surprise.

“You don’t know?”

“Not in a way I can say quickly.”

He looked at her more closely.

He seemed to like that answer more than a ready-made one.

“Lucas says if people answer quickly, they often want to close the question, not understand it.”

Marta froze.

There he was.

Again.

Not here—and still here.

“He told you that?”

“He told Artem. I heard.”

“And what do you think?”

Nazar shrugged.

“I don’t know. But you didn’t answer quickly.”

Marta exhaled slowly.

“Then I’ll try it this way. Being good doesn’t mean saving adults from their anger. And it doesn’t mean earning the right not to be hit, frightened, or sent back into the dark. You don’t have to earn those things.”

Nazar listened.

“Being good probably starts with not hurting someone weaker than you. And not lying to yourself when you’re afraid.”

He thought.

“If I’m afraid, does that mean I’m bad?”

“No. If you’re afraid, then you’re afraid.”

“That’s it?”

“That’s it.”

He looked at his steps for a long time.

Then said:

“Then for now I’m just scary.”

The younger boy snorted.

“You’re not scary. You’re skinny.”

Nazar looked at him.

“You’re skinny yourself.”

“I’m little.”

“That’s no excuse.”

Marta stood.

That was enough.

Not a rescue.

Not a great victory.

Just a few sentences after which a child was not free, but perhaps for a minute stopped being guilty of everything.

On her way out she saw Artem.

He was looking at her.

“Are you the police?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“What if someone didn’t do anything, but is still guilty?”

Marta stopped.

“Be more specific.”

Artem turned a piece of clay in his hands.

“Like, if everyone thinks he’s guilty. But he was just there.”

“That happens.”

“And then what?”

“Then you have to not rush.”

“What if people already rushed?”

She looked at him more closely.

“Are you talking about yourself?”

He shrugged so sharply that the answer became obvious.

Marta crouched beside him, but not too close.

“Then you need to find an adult who is willing to go back and look again.”

Artem gave a short snort.

“Are there people like that?”

Marta looked across the room at Olena, who was helping Kateryna with the thread.

“There are. But fewer than there should be.”

“Lucas says a mistake is material too.”

Marta almost wearily closed her eyes.

“He says a lot of things.”

“He doesn’t lie.”

Artem said it without admiration.

As a fact.

“Why do you think that?” she asked.

“Because when adults lie, they want everything to go quiet after they speak. With him it’s the opposite—your head gets noisy.”

Marta did not answer.

Because the boy had said something too precise.

And this time it had not come from Lucas.

Olena walked them to the exit.

Serhii was a little ahead, typing a message to someone. Probably about the battery. Real life was not waiting for them to sort out a man with three names.

“You didn’t remove him from the program,” Marta said.

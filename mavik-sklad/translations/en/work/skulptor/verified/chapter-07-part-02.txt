“Because, and I quote, ‘an apology is sometimes a way of asking the person who was hurt to work one more time for your relief.’”

Marta was silent.

Serhii put the phone on the desk.

“Smart old man.”

“Tired.”

“They often go together.”

They found Nataliia Honchar.

The former psychologist from the youth center now had a private practice in another region. She did not answer the email. When they called, an administrator said Ms. Nataliia was in a session and would pass along the request.

Serhii did not press.

“We don’t push,” he said. “We don’t have a twenty-year-old criminal case. We’re assessing risk now.”

“And if there was a crime?”

“Then it’s buried under a layer of time, other people’s trauma, and bad procedure. We’ll get to it—if there’s something to catch hold of.”

Marta sat at the computer looking at the poster for Lucas’s lecture at the library.

Form and Freedom. How Art Helps Us Live Through Trauma.

Form.

Freedom.

Trauma.

Everything sounded right.

Almost beautiful.

And that was exactly why she remembered Nazar’s steps.

Crooked.

Uneven.

Not a lecture.

Not a concept.

Just a small clay attempt to get out of wherever he needed to get out of.

Marta opened her notes and added:

Don’t let him turn the living into a concept.

Then she looked at the sentence and did not delete it.

They arrived at Sofia’s workshop at ten past six.

Out of uniform.

In a dark jacket, Serhii looked no less like a police officer than he did in uniform. Now he was simply a police officer whose clothes had decided not to announce it.

“Don’t look at me like that,” he said when they got out of the car.

“Like what?”

“Like I ruined the civilian look.”

“You never had one.”

“Fair.”

The After the Silence workshop was on the ground floor of an old building with large windows. Behind the glass stood shelves of cups, plates, small bird figures, and strange objects with no obvious function. Some were smooth, almost perfect. Others had cracks, rough edges, dents from fingers.

A sign hung on the door:

Today the workshop closes at 18:00

The light inside was warm.

Sofia opened the door herself.

In person she looked older than in the website photograph. Not in age—in her gaze. She had a craftswoman’s hands: short nails, dry skin, a thin line of clay near her wrist that water had not washed away.

“Lieutenant Koval?” she asked.

“Yes. This is Serhii Kravets.”

“Come in.”

She closed the door behind them.

Not with a key.

She simply turned the sign to “closed.”

Marta noticed.

Sofia noticed that she noticed.

“The door isn’t locked,” she said. “If that makes it easier for you.”

“Thank you.”

“That isn’t care. It’s saving time. People who work with trauma too often explain the obvious only after someone has already tensed up.”

Serhii raised his eyebrows almost imperceptibly.

“Everyone around here is precise.”

Sofia looked at him.

“No. I just spent many years learning not to be fog.”

She led them deeper into the workshop.

There was a large table covered with cloth. On it stood cups, a teapot, a plate of dry biscuits, and three clay objects covered with a white napkin.

Marta sat where she could see the door.

Serhii sat where he could see Marta, Sofia, and the window.

Sofia noticed that too.

“You work well as a pair.”

“Not always,” Serhii said.

“But long enough not to get tangled up in a room.”

She sat opposite them.

“I won’t tell you everything.”

Marta nodded.

“That’s your right.”

“And not because I want to hide something. Not all of my past belongs to your review.”

“I understand.”

“No,” Sofia said. “But you can respect it. That’s enough.”

Marta accepted that without argument.

“All right.”

Sofia poured tea.

No one took a cup immediately.

She smiled almost imperceptibly.

“Not poisoned.”

Serhii answered:

“We’re more on clay than poison today.”

For the first time, Sofia almost smiled.

The tension shifted slightly, but did not disappear.

“Yakiv told you about fourteen-year-old Sofia,” she said.

“Yes,” Marta replied.

“He told it as if he were guilty.”

“Yes.”

“That sounds like him.”

“Is he guilty?”

Sofia did not answer immediately.

She ran a finger along the rim of her cup.

“All the adults were a little guilty back then. It’s a very convenient form of guilt: thin enough that no one feels its weight alone.”

Marta took out her notebook.

“May I take notes?”

“Yes. But don’t quote everything word for word.”

“All right.”

Serhii said:

“We need facts. Not a confession.”

Sofia looked at him more closely.

“You’re better than you try to look.”

“Don’t spread that around.”

She finally picked up her cup.

“Luka came to us in the fall. Not off the street. With a recommendation. His documents were normal. He knew how to work with children who trusted no one. He didn’t push. Didn’t baby-talk them. Didn’t ask stupid questions. Didn’t say ‘everything will be fine.’ That made people trust him quickly.”

“You too?” Marta asked.

“Especially me.”

The words carried no shame.

And no pride.

Just a fact.

“Why?”

“Because he was the first adult who did not call my fear weakness.”

Marta did not write.

Sofia was looking not at her, but at her own hands.

“He said, ‘Fear is a guard. Sometimes it’s just standing by the wrong door.’”

Marta recognized the intonation.

Not the words.

The way of speaking.

“And what did that give you?”

“At first? Relief. I was fourteen, and I thought something was wrong with me because I was afraid of everything: footsteps in the corridor, my mother’s breathing after she drank, doors, men’s laughter, the silence after shouting. And he didn’t say, ‘Don’t be afraid.’ He said fear works. It just needs to be taught.”

Serhii wrote briefly, without anything extra.

Marta listened.

“That doesn’t sound like harm,” she said.

“Because at first it wasn’t harm.”

Sofia raised her eyes.

“That’s the problem with Luka. He rarely starts with a lie.”

“And then?”

“Then he shows you that pain can be given form. A very clear one. A very beautiful one. The kind people start praising you for.”

She set the cup aside.

“Do you know what is most dangerous for a traumatized child?”

Marta did not answer.

Neither did Serhii.

“When adults finally stop pitying them and start admiring them.”

The workshop grew quieter.

Sofia continued:

“They praised me. Said I was strong. That I had become calm. That I was ‘growing up.’ That I held myself together well. I had simply cut away everything that trembled.”

Marta remembered Nazar.

For now I’m just scary.

And Artem.

What if someone didn’t do anything, but is still guilty?

“Did Luka tell you directly to do that?” Serhii asked.

“No.”

“Then how?”

Sofia did not take offense at the dryness of the question.

“Through language. Through images. Through which pieces he noticed. If I made something soft, shapeless, childish—he was silent. Didn’t criticize. Just stayed silent. If I made something sharp, precise, almost cold—he would come over and say, ‘There. Here you are not asking permission to exist.’”

Marta wrote:

Did not notice everything. Reinforced a particular form.

Sofia saw it.

“Exactly.”

“You read that?” Marta asked.

“I guessed.”

“What happened at that meeting with your mother?”

Sofia was silent for a long time.

Not because she did not remember.

The opposite.

Because she remembered too well.

“Mom came sober. That alone was a miracle. I wore a blue sweater. For some reason that matters. I remember it scratched at my neck. I kept wanting to pull it away, but I didn’t because I thought strong people didn’t tug at their clothes.”

Marta did not interrupt.

“The meeting was supposed to be without him. But he came. Said he had a right to know what I’d been saying about the family. Yakiv started explaining something. Nataliia asked him to leave. Mom cried. And I stood there thinking: this is it, the form. Now I’ll show them I’m not clay anymore.”

She smiled bitterly.

“Stupid child.”

“Not stupid,” Marta said.

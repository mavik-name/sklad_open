Chapter 7. Sofia

They did not find Sofia through databases.

Marta did not like that.

Databases made sense. Cold, limited, legal or illegal—but understandable. They had fields, dates, case numbers, addresses, statuses, grounds for access. You could argue with them, hate them, but they did not pretend to accidentally hand you an answer at the exact moment you had almost stopped looking for it.

They found Sofia through an old website.

Not a service archive.

Not a police system.

Not closed data.

An old website of the youth center, which had long since stopped operating, but whose page still hung on the internet like a forgotten poster on the wall of an abandoned club.

Serhii called it “digital mold.”

Marta called it “a trace.”

He said she was romanticizing garbage again.

She said he was disguising usefulness as grumbling again.

The site had a news item from December 2004.

“Results of the Creative Rehabilitation Program for Teenagers”

Below it—a badly compressed group photograph.

The same tables.

The same clay.

The same flip chart in the background.

The children’s faces were small and unclear, but the participants’ first names were listed under the photograph. No surnames. Back then, not everyone understood yet that even the first name of a child in a crisis program could be too much.

Sofia. Artem. Iryna. Danylo. Marko.

Below was a short text about “developing inner support,” “art as a path to the self,” and “overcoming trauma through form.”

Marta read and felt her shoulders slowly tighten.

“Form again,” she said.

Serhii sat beside her desk eating a cold pastry from a bag.

“He doesn’t own the word. Half of art therapists use it.”

“But he does too.”

“Yes. He does too. So we don’t turn every word into a fingerprint.”

“You can see the traces yourself.”

“I can. But a trace still isn’t evidence. It’s a direction you can follow if you don’t do it too stupidly.”

Marta scrolled farther down.

There was a list of acknowledgments.

Program director — Yakiv Levytskyi.

Psychologist — Nataliia Honchar.

Clay workshop — Luka Myron.

She stopped.

“There.”

Serhii leaned toward the screen.

“Yes. There.”

He stopped chewing.

For Serhii, that was almost an official acknowledgment that the moment was serious.

“Luka Myron,” Marta said. “Not only the photograph. Text.”

“Text on an old website.”

“But text.”

“Yes. Text.”

Serhii picked up a pen and wrote in his notebook.

“Youth center. Program. December 2004. Luka Myron. Nataliia Honchar.”

“Do we look for Nataliia?”

“Open sources first. If she’s alive, if there’s a contact, if she’s willing to talk. No ‘we’re the police, show us your soul.’”

“And Sofia?”

Marta was already scrolling farther.

There were several news items on the site. In February 2005, there was an announcement that the program was closing “in connection with reorganization.” The wording was too smooth to be true.

Farther down was a small paragraph in the “participant stories” section.

“Sofia, 15, continues working with ceramics. Her pieces were shown at the charity exhibition ‘What Does Not Break.’”

Marta read the title aloud.

“‘What Does Not Break.’”

Serhii rubbed his eyes.

“I already don’t like it.”

“Neither do I.”

She opened a new search.

“Sofia ceramics What Does Not Break”

Several empty results.

Then an old poster in a blog.

Then a mention in a local newspaper.

And finally—a workshop page.

“Sofia Lemesh. Ceramics Workshop ‘After the Silence.’”

The home page had a photograph of a woman beside a potter’s wheel.

She was a little over thirty.

Dark hair, tied back carelessly. A calm face, but not a soft one. The kind of face people have when they have not defeated the past, but have forced it to stop commanding every movement.

Marta looked at the photograph longer than necessary.

“She’s alive,” she said quietly.

Serhii looked at her.

“Yes.”

“That’s already good.”

“It doesn’t mean anything yet.”

“But it’s good.”

He did not argue.

The page had a workshop contact number and an email address.

Serhii immediately pulled the phone toward himself.

“Do we call officially?”

“I don’t know whether we have the right.”

“We have the right to call a public number and ask for a meeting about an old volunteer program. She has the right to send us to all the archangels. We have the right to thank her and hang up.”

“To the archangels?”

“I’m adapting the vocabulary to your theological line.”

Marta looked at him.

“Very funny.”

“Not very. But I’m trying.”

He dialed the number.

Put it on speaker.

One ring.

A second.

“After the Silence workshop,” a woman’s voice answered.

Calm.

Lower than Marta had expected.

Serhii straightened.

“Good afternoon. Senior Lieutenant Kravets, juvenile prevention. I need Sofia Lemesh.”

A brief pause.

“Speaking.”

Marta stopped breathing the way she had been breathing before that.

Serhii glanced at her, then continued:

“Ms. Sofia, we’re contacting you about an old program at a youth center. The year 2004. A clay workshop. Does the name Luka Myron mean anything to you?”

The other end went quiet.

Not the way someone goes quiet when they do not remember.

Different.

The way people go quiet when a door opens into a room they locked long ago, but the key has remained in their pocket all this time.

“Who gave you that name?” Sofia asked.

Serhii did not answer immediately.

Correctly.

“Yakiv Levytskyi.”

Another pause.

“Yakiv is alive?”

“Yes.”

“He came to you himself?”

“Yes.”

“Because of him?”

“Because of Lucas Martyn.”

Marta noticed Sofia’s breathing change on the line.

Not sharply.

But it changed.

“He’s working with children again?” she asked.

Serhii looked at Marta.

She nodded.

“Yes,” he said. “At a center for social and psychological rehabilitation.”

“With clay?”

“Yes.”

Sofia gave a short laugh.

There was no amusement in it.

Only tired recognition.

“Of course.”

Marta leaned closer to the phone.

“Ms. Sofia, this is Lieutenant Marta Koval. We don’t want to pull you into anything you’re not ready for. But if you can tell us what happened back then, it may help us assess the risk to children now.”

Sofia was silent longer.

Then she said:

“I won’t talk at the station.”

“All right.”

“And not with Yakiv there.”

“All right.”

“And I don’t want to see Lucas.”

Marta barely reacted to the fact that she had said “Lucas,” not “Luka.”

Barely.

“We won’t involve him in the meeting.”

“You don’t understand,” Sofia said. “If he’s already nearby, he’ll be involved anyway.”

Serhii slowly raised his eyes.

Marta answered calmly:

“Then help us understand how.”

Sofia exhaled.

“Come to the workshop. Today after six. No uniforms.”

Serhii said immediately:

“We can’t promise no uniforms. We don’t hide who we are.”

“I’m not asking you to hide it. I just don’t want clients seeing police at the door. Children come here too.”

Marta and Serhii exchanged a look.

“All right,” Marta said. “No uniforms. But the two of us are coming.”

“I know.”

“How?”

“You wouldn’t come alone.”

Marta pressed her lips together.

“See you then, Ms. Sofia.”

“Lieutenant Koval?”

“Yes?”

“If he has already asked you a question, don’t answer quickly.”

The connection ended.

Serhii stared at the phone for several more seconds.

“What, do they have a club for identical phrases?”

Marta did not answer.

Because the question was not funny.

By evening they had managed to do little.

Or a lot—depending on what counted as work.

Serhii contacted Yakiv and confirmed Sofia’s surname. Yakiv confirmed: Lemesh. Said he had not known where she was now, and after the call he was silent for a long time. Then he asked them not to pass on any apologies from him.

“Why?” Marta asked when Serhii relayed the conversation.

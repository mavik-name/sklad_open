And that did not make Marta feel calmer. Quite the opposite.

“Can Nazar come in?” Olena asked.

Lucas glanced toward the door.

“If he wants to.”

“Not ‘he should’?” Marta asked.

“Children hear ‘you should’ more often than their own name.”

Olena said nothing.

Serhii gave a quiet snort.

Marta did not answer. Because once again it was one of those lines she wanted to dismiss, except first she had to admit it was precise.

Olena went out and returned a minute later with Nazar.

The boy kept close to the wall. He was no longer wearing his jacket, only a sweater that had clearly come from the center. The sleeves were a little too long, and he hid his fingers inside them.

When he saw Marta, Nazar stopped.

“Hi,” she said.

“Hi.”

He looked at Serhii. At Olena. At Lucas. At the tables.

“You can sit wherever you want,” Olena said. “Or you can just watch.”

Nazar chose a place at the edge of the table where he could see the door.

Marta noticed.

Lucas did too.

He did not comment.

He simply put a lump of clay and a small jar of water in front of the boy.

“You don’t have to make anything,” he said. “You can just knead it.”

Nazar touched the clay with the tip of one finger.

“It’s cold.”

“At first.”

“And then?”

“Then it takes warmth from your hands.”

The boy laid his palm on the clay. Carefully. As if checking whether it would strike back.

“What am I supposed to do?” he asked.

“Whatever comes out.”

“And if nothing comes out?”

“Then nothing comes out. That’s a shape too. Just an honest one.”

Nazar frowned.

“Nothing can’t be a shape.”

Lucas tilted his head almost imperceptibly.

“It can. A hole has a shape too.”

The boy thought about that.

Then began kneading the clay.

Marta stood by the door and felt two impulses fighting inside her: to check Lucas as a suspicious adult around children—and not to interfere, because for the first time since she had met Nazar, he was not clenching something in his fist. He was working it between his hands.

Serhii came closer.

“I’m heading to the district station. I’ll check that battery against reported thefts.”

“You think it’s stolen?”

“I think people who don’t have money for electricity don’t usually just happen to acquire a car battery with an inverter.”

“I’ll stay a little longer.”

“How long is ‘a little longer’?”

“Until the activity ends.”

“That’s already ‘a long time,’ just in the form of ‘I decided so.’”

“Serhii.”

“What?”

“I’m not a child.”

He looked at her without smiling.

“I know. That’s why I’m telling you directly: don’t get carried away. Not with the boy. Not with the volunteer.”

Marta tensed.

“I’m not getting carried away.”

“Even better.”

He left.

“Yes.”

“And a very dangerous one.”

Marta froze.

That was not what she had expected from him.

“You agree?”

Her father looked at her.

“Daughter, I’ve spent my whole life talking to people about pain. I know how easy it is to turn someone else’s wound into a good story for a sermon. And how hard it is not to.”

Something in Marta loosened.

Not completely.

But a little.

“I thought you’d say we shouldn’t rush to suspicion.”

“We shouldn’t rush to judgment. That’s different.”

Serhii gave a quiet grunt.

“Finally someone in this family is phrasing things legally.”

Andrii looked at him.

“I’m a pastor. We work with evidence sometimes too. Ours is simply documented worse.”

Viktoriia smiled for the first time that morning.

Briefly.

But genuinely.

“What do you want to do?” Andrii asked.

Marta picked up the third sheet.

“Go to the lecture.”

“That’s obvious.”

“Not alone.”

“That too.”

“Limit his participation in the children’s program, but not abruptly. Olena has already changed the schedule. More facilitators, less dependence on one voice.”

Andrii nodded.

“Good.”

“And add another point to the guide.”

Viktoriia picked up a pen.

“Which one?”

Marta looked at Lucas’s sheet.

Then at her mother.

Then at her father.

“A child is not material for an adult’s idea. Even a good one.”

Viktoriia wrote it down.

Slowly.

Andrii repeated quietly:

“Even a good one.”

Serhii folded his arms across his chest.

“That can go in bold.”

“You’ve started editing church guides now?” Marta asked.

“Someone has to bring sobriety into this fund.”

“There’s tea.”

“I noticed. That’s why the situation is critical.”

Viktoriia looked up from what she was writing.

“Serhii, would you like coffee?”

“If it doesn’t violate the boundaries of help.”

“It will, but only slightly.”

“Then yes.”

For a little while, the tension became human.

Sometimes that was enough to keep from falling apart.

They left the envelope at the fund.

Not because they did not want to take it.

But because it had been delivered there, through Viktoriia, and Serhii decided not to turn it into “secret evidence.”

“We photograph everything, both sides,” he said. “Record who delivered it, when, who saw it. The original stays here for now in a closed folder. If we need it officially, we’ll collect it properly.”

Marta did not argue.

She had already learned that when Serhii became boring, it often meant he was saving the case from her haste.

Viktoriia wrote a short internal note for herself:

Envelope delivered by Lucas Martyn. Date. Approximate time: 16:30. Present: Viktoriia Koval, volunteer Iryna. Contents not reviewed until morning. Opened in the presence of Marta Koval and Serhii Kravets.

Serhii read it.

“Good.”

“I’ve been documenting aid for many years,” Viktoriia said. “Paperwork isn’t all that foreign to mercy.”

“That belongs on the wall.”

“No. We already have too many strong phrases.”

Marta looked at her mother with gratitude.

Exactly for that.

For knowing when to stop something beautiful in time.

Meanwhile, Andrii was holding the lecture program.

“I’m going too.”

“No,” Marta said immediately.

Her father looked up.

“Why?”

“Because he’s interested you.”

“And that’s exactly why I have to go.”

“Dad.”

“Marta, if a person is affecting the space of the church, the fund, and the children, I can’t pretend this is only your official matter.”

“He could use you.”

“He could.”

“And you’re going anyway?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

Andrii set the sheet on the desk.

“Because if there’s a wound in him, I want to see it. And if there isn’t a wound there—all the more.”

Marta was silent.

Serhii said quietly:

“Pastor, with all due respect, don’t go there as someone already looking for salvation in him.”

Andrii turned toward him.

“And you don’t go there as someone already looking for a criminal.”

Serhii looked at him for several seconds.

Then nodded.

“Fair.”

Marta felt a strange irritation.

Not because they were wrong.

Because they were both right.

She wanted a simple division: someone careful, someone naive, someone professional, someone emotional.

But everyone in the room turned out to be more complicated.

It made it harder to be angry cleanly.

“All right,” she said. “But if you go, not alone. And after the lecture, no private conversations with him without us.”

Andrii smiled faintly.

“Are you speaking to me as a daughter right now, or as a lieutenant?”

“As someone who knows you.”

“Then accepted.”

After the fund, Marta and Serhii drove to the station.

Not because the answer was there.

Because there were computers, phones, folders, and at least the illusion that chaos could be sorted into tabs.

Lev Savyn turned up faster than they expected.

And worse.

In 2012, a series of workshops called “Crack and Form” had indeed been held in the city. The organizer was the Horizon Youth Space. An old PDF report contained several photographs, a program, and a list of facilitators.

Lev Savyn — artist, facilitator of group practices, author of the “Living Form” method.

“‘Facilitator,’” Serhii read. “When a person can’t explain what they do, they’re often a facilitator.”

“Not all of them.”

“Enough of them.”

Marta opened the photograph.

Lev Savyn stood beside a board.

This time the photo was better.

Not perfect.

But better.

He was looking not at the camera but somewhere to the side. His hair was a little longer than Lucas’s was now. His face was thinner. But the eyes, the line of the jaw, the posture—everything was too familiar.

Serhii was silent for a long time.

Then he said:

“Now I don’t like this.”

“You liked it before?”

“Before, I didn’t like your face. Now I don’t like the photo.”

Marta enlarged the image.

“He doesn’t age.”

“Don’t say it as if you’ve already stamped the file.”

“How should I say it?”

“‘There is a discrepancy in apparent age across photographs from different years.’”

“That sounds like someone afraid to say ‘he doesn’t age.’”

“That sounds like someone who wants to reach retirement without ending up in a psychiatric ward.”

She did not answer.

Because she wanted that too.

The report included contact information for Horizon’s organizer, Maryna Dovhan.

The number did not answer.

The email bounced.

The space’s page had not been updated in years.

But at the end of the PDF there was one more line:

Partner lecture: “Freedom as the Refusal of Fear.” Lev Savyn. Video recording available in the media archive library.

Marta and Serhii looked at each other at the same time.

“The library,” she said.

Serhii slowly pushed back from the desk.

“Don’t tell me it’s the same one.”

“City media library. A branch of the library system.”

“Of course.”

“It could be an open archive.”

“It could be another stage.”

“You said it yourself: we verify first.”

“I already regret teaching you the right words.”

Marta wrote down the address.

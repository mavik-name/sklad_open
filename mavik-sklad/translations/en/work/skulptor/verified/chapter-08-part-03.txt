“Shall we go?”

Serhii looked at the time.

“Not now. First we document what we’ve already found. Save the PDF. Make a copy. Record the source. Then we call the media library and ask whether the recording exists.”

“And if it does?”

“Then we go watch it. Officially, boringly, without suspense music.”

She stopped.

“All right.”

Serhii looked at her suspiciously.

“You agreed suspiciously fast.”

“I really agreed.”

“That’s almost frightening.”

“You’re just right.”

He put a hand to his chest.

“Say it again. I’ll record it.”

“Don’t exaggerate.”

“This is a historic moment.”

Marta turned back to the monitor.

But a few seconds later she said:

“Serhii.”

“What?”

“If the video exists, we’ll see him move. Hear his voice. Not a photograph.”

“Yes.”

“And then?”

He did not answer immediately.

“Then it will get worse or easier.”

“Why easier?”

“Because sometimes it’s more frightening not to know what exactly you’re afraid of seeing.”

The media library answered on the third call.

The woman on the line asked them to send the request by email, but when she heard the title of the recording, she said:

“Yes, I think we have that disc.”

Serhii covered the receiver with his hand and looked at Marta.

“A disc,” he whispered. “Archaeology.”

Marta waved him off so he would not distract her.

“Can we view it?” Serhii asked into the phone.

“In the media library reading room. The recording cannot be checked out. If you need a copy, only by official request and provided there are no rights restrictions.”

“We’ll come tomorrow.”

“I’ll book you for eleven. Under what name?”

“Kravets. Serhii.”

“Good.”

He hung up.

“Tomorrow at eleven, we watch Lev Savyn.”

Marta looked at the photo from the PDF.

Lev stood beside the board, leaning slightly forward as if he were asking someone a question at that exact moment.

She felt the familiar tension.

Not fear.

Anticipation.

“How long until Lucas’s lecture?”

“Six days.”

“Not much.”

“Enough for panic. For work—as usual.”

Marta opened her notes and added:

2012. Video. Media library. Watch.

Then underneath:

Envelope — Lucas is giving us the trail himself. Why?

She stared at that question longer than the others.

Serhii got up to pour coffee but stopped by her desk.

“Versions?”

“Three.”

“Go.”

“First: he wants to scare us.”

“Banal, but possible.”

“Second: he wants us to find something specific.”

“Dangerous, because then he controls the route.”

“Third: he isn’t hiding because he doesn’t believe he’s doing evil.”

Serhii said nothing.

That version was the worst.

Because a criminal can be caught.

A manipulator exposed.

But a person who sincerely believes pain is an instrument of good does not run from the light.

They walk onto the stage themselves.

Marta came home late that evening.

The light between the second and third floors of the stairwell was out. She lit the stairs with her phone and suddenly remembered Nazar’s flashlight.

Not symbolically.

Practically.

For the first time in several days, she thought of it not as an image but as an object: plastic casing, cracked cover, new batteries, the pocket of someone else’s blue jacket.

Just light.

Marta stopped on the stairs.

She took out her phone and opened the volunteer guide.

At the end of the document, after the line about gratitude, she added a new point:

A child is not material for our idea—even a good, beautiful, or correct one. They do not need to be carved. They need to be kept alive.

She reread it.

The word “carved” was precise.

Maybe too precise.

She left it.

Then sent the updated file to Viktoriia.

Her mother replied almost immediately:

This point will be the last one. And the first one at orientation.

Marta put away her phone.

Climbed one more floor.

Something was lying by her door.

She stopped.

Did not approach immediately.

A small object wrapped in gray paper.

No note.

No name.

Marta slowly took out her phone and called Serhii.

He answered almost immediately:

“Don’t tell me you’ve got another envelope.”

“Not an envelope.”

A pause.

“Where?”

“By my door.”

“Don’t touch it.”

“I’m not touching it.”

“Step back toward the stairs. I’m coming. And call a patrol.”

Marta stepped back two paces.

Watched the gray package.

It was small.

Did not look like an explosive.

Did not look like a threat.

That was exactly what made it worse.

Because she almost already knew what was inside.

Did not know with her mind.

But knew.

Serhii was saying something into the phone: brief, official, sharp.

Marta listened not to the words but to her own breathing.

Even.

Too even.

A patrol arrived ten minutes later.

Serhii after fifteen.

The object was examined.

Carefully unwrapped.

Inside the gray paper was a clay cup.

Small.

Crooked.

With an uneven rim.

The kind you could drink from.

Marta did not touch it.

Serhii stood beside her in silence.

One word had been scratched into the bottom of the cup, on the inside:

Alive

Marta stared at it for a long time.

Then said:

“Now he’s at my door.”

Serhii did not answer immediately.

“Yes.”

“Is this grounds?”

He looked at the cup.

At the gray paper.

At the dark corridor.

“Now,” he said, “this is a boundary.”

Marta looked up.

“Which one?”

Serhii pulled on gloves.

“The one he crossed.”

Olena stopped by the door.

“No.”

“Even after the photograph?”

“Not yet.”

“Why?”

Olena looked through the glass at the children in the workshop.

“Because a photograph is grounds to investigate. Not to punish.”

Marta nodded.

She hated it when other people spoke her own principles precisely when she wanted to depart from them.

“But I changed the schedule,” Olena said. “Lucas won’t lead sessions alone as the primary facilitator anymore. Only in a pair. And not every week. More different adults, less dependence on one voice.”

“That’s sensible.”

“I do use my head sometimes.”

“That’s not what I meant.”

“I know.”

Olena grew more serious.

“Marta, I’m not protecting him. I’m protecting the children from our sudden moves. If we remove Lucas tomorrow without explanation, some of the children will decide someone has disappeared again. And each one will explain it in their own way. Nazar—that he wasn’t good enough. Artem—that nobody cares. Liza—that adults always lie. We have to be careful.”

“And if being careful gives him time?”

“Then your job is to make sure that time isn’t blind.”

Marta was silent.

That was a good answer too.

Serhii opened the door.

“Are you finished suffering wisely? Because I still have a stolen battery, a stepfather with an imagination, and a garage owner who wants blood but is willing to settle for a statement for now.”

Olena almost smiled.

“Tell the owner blood isn’t in the procedure.”

“That’s what I told him. Didn’t help.”

They went outside.

Wet cold settled on their faces at once.

Serhii headed toward the car, but Marta lingered by the steps.

In the yard, Nazar stood beside the metal rocket.

Of course they would not have let him outside without a jacket, but now he was wearing someone else’s blue one with sleeves too long for him. The flashlight bulged in the side pocket.

He was not turning it on.

Just keeping it in his pocket.

Beside him stood the younger boy from the workshop. They were discussing something very seriously, pointing at the rocket.

Marta could not hear the words.

And that was good.

Not everything needed to be heard.

Some children’s conversations had the right to exist without the police.

In the car, Serhii did not start the engine right away.

“Well?” he said.

“You ask ‘well?’ a lot today.”

“Because today you keep wearing the face of someone standing on a bridge wondering whether it’s a bridge.”

Marta leaned back wearily against the seat.

“Nazar’s phrase didn’t come from Lucas.”

“Good.”

“But Lucas is still everywhere.”

“That’s a different question.”

“Artem said his head gets noisy after Lucas.”

Serhii looked at her.

“Mine does after you sometimes too.”

“Not funny.”

“I’m not entirely joking.”

He started the car.

“Look. There’s Yakiv. There’s the photograph. There’s a possible earlier history. There’s the poster. There’s the strange consistency in his appearance. There are several names. None of that can be ignored. But there’s also Olena, who sees the children every day. There are children who don’t fall apart around him and sometimes even pull themselves together. There are documents that, so far, are normal. And there’s you, wanting a simple conclusion.”

“I do.”

“You won’t get one.”

Marta turned her head toward the window.

“I know.”

“No. Not yet. But you’re starting to.”

They drove out of the yard.

At the intersection, Serhii slowed down.

“Now we go back to the station. I deal with the battery. You look for Sofia. Officially only: through the center’s old names, public contacts, Yakiv’s statement, maybe archives of civic programs. No nighttime calls. No going alone to unknown people.”

“Affirmative.”

“Don’t say ‘affirmative’ when you’re already breaking three points in your head.”

“Fine.”

“And one more thing.”

“What?”

“You do not talk to Lucas today.”

Marta turned toward him.

“Why?”

“Because for now he knows more about you than you know about him. And after the library and the center, you’re angry, tired, and you have a new phrase: ‘a child is not a stone.’ If you go to him now, he’ll turn that phrase into a door. And you’ll walk through it yourself.”

Marta was silent.

“That was disturbingly precise,” she said at last.

“I’m learning from your volunteer.”

“Not funny.”

“I’m not laughing.”

At the station, everything returned to paperwork.

It was almost calming.

Papers did not ask the right questions. Did not have faces from 2004. Did not speak in hoarse voices from unknown numbers. They simply demanded a date, a signature, wording, a case number.

Serhii went to the investigator about the battery.

Marta sat at the computer and opened Yakiv’s statement.

Sofia.

Fourteen years old in 2004.

Youth center.

Clay program.

Supervised meeting with her mother.

The partner who was not supposed to be there.

After that—the program closed.

Luka Myron disappeared.

She wrote everything down on a separate sheet.

Not beautifully.

Not literarily.

The way it should be.

Sofia—establish surname. Youth center—archive / old name / who ran it. Luka Myron—documents / recommendations / possible false identity. Lev—2012 / workshop / city / posters. Lucas Martyn—now / library / fund / center.

Then she added one more item:

What does “form” mean in his method?

She looked at the last line.

It was not official.

But without it, everything else made no sense.

Her phone vibrated.

A message from Viktoriia:

Lucas just dropped off workshop materials at the fund. Asked whether you had seen the poster at the library yet.

Marta froze.

The message was short.

Ordinary.

And frightening precisely because of how ordinary it was.

Serhii had told her not to talk to him today.

She had not.

And still he had answered first.

Not to her.

Through her mother.

Through the fund.

Through an ordinary route where every step had a plausible explanation.

Marta typed:

What exactly did he say?

Viktoriia answered almost immediately:

“Lieutenant Koval has probably already seen the poster. Tell her the lecture is open. And so are the questions afterward.”

Marta slowly put the phone on the desk.

Serhii came back at that exact moment.

“What?”

She showed him the message.

He read it.

Then again.

“I told you not to talk to him today.”

“I didn’t.”

Serhii looked at the phone.

“And apparently he didn’t get my instructions.”

Marta picked up a pen and wrote under the library item on her sheet:

Lecture. Go?

Then crossed out the question mark.

And put a period.

Lecture. Go.

Serhii saw it.

“Of course.”

“It’s an open lecture.”

“It’s a trap.”

“Could be.”

“And you’re going anyway.”

“Yes.”

He sat opposite her.

“Then not alone.”

Marta looked at him.

“You’re going to a lecture about form and freedom?”

“If it has you, a man with three names, and a potential clay cult—yes. I’ll even take notes.”

“Notes don’t suit you.”

“I’ll draw a battery.”

For the first time that day, she smiled.

Briefly.

But genuinely.

Serhii tapped a finger on her sheet.

“We have time before the lecture. We look for Sofia. We check Yakiv. We dig up the old center. And most importantly—we do not start believing him or believing in him.”

“Who exactly?”

“Anyone.”

Marta looked at the list.

Sofia.

Luka.

Lev.

Lucas.

Nazar.

Form.

A child is not a stone.

Outside the station window, dusk was falling. Cars switched on their headlights. The office lamps reflected in the glass, making the city seem layered over the room like a second, transparent surface.

Marta opened the library photograph on the screen.

November 2004 looked at her through a blurred face.

She was no longer sure she was looking for a monster.

But she was already sure she was looking for more than just a man.

And that was much worse.

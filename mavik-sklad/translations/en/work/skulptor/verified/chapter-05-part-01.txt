Chapter 5. The Witness

Serhii did not “run” the number.

He said so immediately.

No coffee, no jokes, no preamble.

“No.”

Marta stood beside his desk with her phone in her hand.

“I haven’t asked for anything yet.”

“You’ve got the face of someone who’s about to ask for something stupid and call it an operational necessity.”

“I got a call about a volunteer who has access to children.”

“And you sent me the number last night. I saw it.”

“Then—”

“No,” Serhii repeated. “I’m not going into subscriber data because of one nighttime call from an unknown man who speaks in riddles.”

Marta tightened her grip on the phone.

“He said Lucas isn’t Martyn.”

“And people also say their neighbors poison them through the ventilation, presidents talk to them personally through the television, and every second drunk driver ‘only moved the car.’ Want me to keep going?”

“Serhii.”

He put down his pen.

“Listen carefully. The number came through—good. You took a screenshot—correct. I recorded it on my end—also correct. But that doesn’t give me the right to just ‘run’ the owner. Was there a call? Yes.”

“Was there a threat? No. A specific allegation of a crime? No. Danger to a specific child? Not stated. For now, what we have is not grounds. It’s a trace we need not to trample.”

Marta did not answer.

Because that was inconvenient.

And correct.

Serhii picked up the work phone, copied the number from the sticky note, and dialed.

Put it on speaker.

The ringing went on for a long time.

One.

Two.

Three.

No one answered.

Serhii hung up.

“This we can do. Call from the work phone. Record the attempt to make contact. If the person calls back and says something concrete, then we think about what comes next.”

“And if he doesn’t call back?”

“Then it remains a nighttime call. Unpleasant, but a nighttime call.”

Marta sat down opposite him.

She wanted to argue.

But there was nothing to argue with.

Serhii looked at her more closely.

“Did you sleep?”

“A little.”

“That’s ‘no’ in the form of self-respect.”

“I was looking for old mentions of Lucas.”

“And?”

“There’s an announcement from three years ago. ‘Form Workshop.’ He’s the facilitator.”

“Three years ago isn’t an exposé. It’s a résumé.”

“The photo is blurry.”

“Crime of the century.”

“Serhii.”

“Marta, I’m on your side. But I’m not going to help you break procedure because you’re scared.”

She looked up.

“I’m not scared.”

“Worse. You’re curious.”

That hit harder than it should have.

“This isn’t curiosity.”

“Then what is it?”

Marta looked at the sticky note with the number.

“He’s always on the boundary. Formally, everything is right. The documents exist. He follows the rules.”

“He doesn’t harm the children. The opposite. But around him something... shifts.”

Serhii was silent.

He did not laugh.

That alone was a lot.

“What exactly shifts?”

“People start telling truths they hadn’t planned to tell.”

“That can be useful sometimes.”

“Not always.”

He nodded slowly.

“All right. For now, this is what we do. If the number calls again, you record the conversation. You promise nothing. You agree to nothing on your own. If he proposes a meeting, you do not go alone.”

“I’m not a child.”

“That’s exactly why I’m not saying it gently, I’m saying it clearly: you do not go alone.”

Marta’s phone vibrated.

They both looked at the screen.

Unknown number.

The same one.

Serhii said nothing. Just nodded.

Marta answered and started the recording.

“Lieutenant Koval.”

For a few seconds, there was silence.

Then the same hoarse voice said:

“You were looking.”

Serhii leaned closer.

“Identify yourself,” Marta said.

“If you want to understand, come to the city library today at twelve forty. Reading room on the second floor.”

“Who are you?”

“Don’t bring him with you.”

“Who?”

A pause.

“The one who asks the right questions.”

The connection cut off.

Marta kept the phone to her ear for another second.

Serhii held out his hand.

“Give it to me.”

He listened to the recording.

Then again.

“All right,” he said. “Now this is no longer just a nighttime call.”

“I’ll go.”

“We’ll go.”

“You’ve got the battery.”

“The battery can wait. It already stole itself yesterday.”

“This is work time.”

“That’s exactly why we’re not going like two heroes into a basement. We’re going to a public place to listen to a person who says he has information about a volunteer with access to children. If it’s nonsense, we close it. If it isn’t, we think further.”

For the first time that morning, Marta felt relief.

Not complete.

But enough to breathe more evenly.

Serhii took his coat from the back of the chair.

“And one more thing.”

“What?”

“If he starts saying Lucas is two hundred years old, you do not make the face that says, ‘I knew it.’ First we listen. Then we doubt. Then we verify. In that order.”

Marta did not smile.

“Agreed.”

“You agreed badly.”

“You’ve got the face of someone who already skipped the ‘doubt’ part.”

The city library stood in a building that had clearly once been more important than it was now.

Tall windows, heavy doors, stone steps, a dark brass plaque beside the entrance. Inside, it was quiet not because everyone was silent, but because the space itself demanded lower voices. Even Serhii automatically lowered his when they came in.

“I hate places like this,” he said almost in a whisper.

“Why?”

“Feels like even the cabinets are smarter than you.”

The reading room on the second floor was half empty. Several students sat with laptops. An elderly woman was reading a newspaper. By the window, a man in a gray coat was reading a book but not turning the pages.

Marta noticed him at once.

Not because he looked suspicious.

The opposite.

Because he was trying too hard not to look suspicious.

Serhii noticed too.

“I’ll be by the catalogue,” he said. “You sit so I can see his hands. And don’t hide yours under the table either.”

“I know.”

“Repeat it.”

“Don’t hide my hands under the table.”

“Now that sounds like work.”

Marta approached the man.

“Did you call me?”

He looked up.

He was around sixty. Maybe older. A thin face, short gray beard, fingers marked by dark age spots. His eyes were attentive and exhausted.

Not insane.

That was the first thing Marta noted.

People often look for madness in the eyes for nothing. It is not always visible.

But this man did not look like someone who had lost touch with reality. More like someone who had seen reality in a place other people did not look, and had long since grown tired of explaining it.

“You didn’t come alone,” he said.

“You didn’t ask me to come alone.”

“Good.”

“Your name.”

“Yakiv Levytskyi.”

“Do you have ID?”

Without offense, he took out his passport and placed it on the table.

Marta checked it.

The name matched.

From a distance, Serhii watched them while holding some book upside down.

Marta returned the passport.

“How did you get my number?”

“Through your guide.”

“What?”

“It was forwarded yesterday to one of the volunteer groups. There was a contact for the responsible person if anyone had questions. Your work number.”

Marta remembered the file.

The contact really was at the bottom.

She had put it there herself.

Logical.

Unpleasantly logical.

“What do you know about Lucas Martyn?”

Yakiv looked out the window.

The snow had stopped. Thin trails of water crawled down the glass.

“That he isn’t Martyn.”

“Evidence?”

“I don’t have what you would call evidence.”

“Then why am I here?”

He opened an old folder he had kept on the chair beside him.

“Because I have something that will make you keep looking.”

Marta did not touch the folder.

“Who are you to him?”

“No one. I once thought I was a colleague.”

“Where?”

“In a program at a youth center. It was in 2004.”

Marta said nothing.

Yakiv took out a photograph.

Not a printout from a phone.

A real old photograph on matte paper.

The edges were slightly worn.

The picture showed a group of people in a large room. Tables. Clay. Teenagers. Two adults beside a flip chart.

One of them was Yakiv, about twenty years younger.

The other stood sideways.

Dark sweater. Sleeves rolled up. Long fingers stained with clay. His face was not fully in the frame.

But enough of it was.

Marta did not take the photograph at once.

First she looked at Serhii.

He had already set aside the upside-down book and was coming toward them.

“Who is that?” Marta asked.

“Back then he called himself Luka Myron.”

Serhii came over and stood beside her.

“May I?”

Yakiv nodded.

Serhii took the photograph by the edge and studied it for a long time.

“Year?”

“Two thousand four.”

“Is there a date on the photograph?”

Yakiv turned it over.

On the back, written in blue ink, were the words:

Workshop. November 2004. Yakiv, Luka, Group 3.

Serhii said nothing.

Neither did Marta.

Because the man in the photograph looked almost exactly the way Lucas looked now.

Not twenty years younger.

Not older.

Almost the same.

“It could be a relative,” Serhii said.

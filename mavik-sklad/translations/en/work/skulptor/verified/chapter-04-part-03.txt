“I’m joking. He’ll say the state will survive.”

The boy thought about it.

“Would he really say that?”

“More or less.”

“He’s angry.”

“He pretends to be.”

“Why?”

“So children don’t notice he’s kind.”

For the first time, Nazar smiled a little more visibly.

A small smile.

Brief.

But real.

Marta did not look at Lucas.

Because she was almost certain he was looking at her.

And she did not want to see that he had noticed that.

The new volunteers arrived at four.

Five people from the church: Kateryna, two students, a young married couple, and Petro, who announced that he had come “not to volunteer, but to monitor the budget,” but sat in the circle anyway.

Olena gave a short orientation.

Marta handed out the guides.

Lucas sat apart at a separate table, preparing materials: paper, clay, aprons, small jars of water. He did not interfere.

It was right.

And very irritating.

“First rule,” Olena said, “you are not coming here to rescue children.”

Petro raised his eyebrows.

“Then what are we doing?”

Olena looked at Marta.

“Helping give them one safe hour,” Marta said. “That’s enough to start.”

The student with short hair raised her hand.

“What if a child starts telling us something difficult on their own?”

“Listen,” Marta said. “Don’t interrogate. Don’t let horror show on your face. Don’t say, ‘you poor thing.’ And don’t promise, ‘I won’t tell anyone.’ If it’s about danger, tell Olena or the responsible staff member.”

“What if the child gets upset?”

“They might.”

“And then what?”

“You handle it.”

The girl fell silent.

Marta softened her tone.

“Trust doesn’t mean keeping every secret. Trust is when, over time, a child understands that you did not use their words against them.”

Lucas looked up.

She noticed.

But continued.

“Second. Don’t give children your phone numbers, social media, or personal promises. If you really want to help a particular child, talk to Olena. Not to the child directly.”

The young man from the married couple asked:

“What if the child asks for the number?”

“Especially then—no.”

“Why?”

Petro muttered:

“Because we just read that.”

Marta ignored him.

“Because a child in crisis can become attached very quickly to an adult who gives them a little warmth. If you are not prepared to be a stable part of that child’s life under the center’s rules, don’t create the illusion of personal rescue.”

Kateryna lowered her eyes to the guide.

“That sounds harsh.”

“Yes.”

“But right.”

“That too.”

Olena nodded.

“That’s exactly why we need rules.”

Lucas suddenly said from the far table:

“And people who don’t take offense at rules.”

Everyone turned toward him.

He did not raise his voice.

He simply added the sentence where it was needed.

Olena nodded.

“Yes. That’s a separate point. If boundaries offend you, you’re better off not working with children.”

Petro wrote something on his sheet.

“That should be in bold.”

Marta looked at him.

“I thought you came to monitor the budget.”

“I’m multifunctional.”

Kateryna laughed quietly.

The tension eased a little.

Marta felt that the orientation was working.

Not perfectly.

But it was working.

And again she could not help noticing: Lucas was not pulling the attention toward himself. He could have said more. People would have listened.

He knew how. Instead, he stayed silent until his sentence became necessary.

That was either the sign of a good volunteer.

Or of very dangerous discipline.

After the orientation, the volunteers dispersed to look at the workshop.

Marta stayed in the corridor beside a display of children’s drawings. On one sheet, a hand was holding a light bulb. The bulb was shining not yellow, but blue.

Under the drawing, in a child’s handwriting, were the words:

“The light is cold, but it’s there.”

Marta looked at the words for a long time.

“This isn’t Nazar’s,” Lucas said.

She did not turn around.

“I didn’t ask.”

“But you thought it.”

“You’re doing it again.”

“No. This time I just guessed.”

“Don’t you ever get tired of being unbearable?”

“You’ve asked that already.”

“And?”

“No.”

She finally turned around.

Lucas stood a few steps away. Not too close. Correct again.

“I checked your documents,” Marta said.

“I know.”

“Olena told you?”

“No.”

“Then how?”

“You’re looking at me with less suspicion today, but more irritation. That means you didn’t find any formal reason.”

Marta inhaled slowly.

“You understand that sentences like that don’t help me trust you?”

“I understand.”

“And you say them anyway.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

Lucas looked at the child’s drawing.

“Because you aren’t looking for reassurance. You’re looking for precision.”

Marta tightened her grip on the folder of guides.

“Don’t turn me into a text you can read.”

“I’m not reading you.”

“No?”

“No. You underline a lot yourself.”

This time she almost laughed.

Not with amusement.

At the absurdity of it.

“You’re either the most brazen person I’ve ever met, or the most honest.”

“Those things are not mutually exclusive.”

“Do you do this deliberately?”

“Yes.”

She stopped.

Because for the first time, he admitted it.

Simply.

Without evasion.

“What exactly do you do deliberately?” she asked.

“I don’t let you put me into a category too quickly.”

“Why?”

“So you’ll look.”

“I’m already looking.”

“No. You’re checking. That’s different.”

Marta did not answer.

Behind the glass, the volunteers were taking seats at the tables. The children were choosing places. Nazar stood beside Olena with the flashlight in the pocket of his old sweater. He was not holding it in his hands.

Not anymore.

It was little.

And a lot.

“You said the right thing today,” Lucas said.

“Which one? I’ve been unnaturally useful today.”

“About the safe hour.”

Marta looked through the glass.

“That was Olena’s thought.”

“But you accepted it.”

“Because it’s precise.”

“And incomplete.”

“What does that mean?”

Lucas was silent for a few seconds.

“A safe hour can save a day. But if afterward the child is returned to an unsafe life, adults start confusing respite with victory.”

Marta felt the familiar anger rise quickly, almost eagerly.

“This again?”

“Yes.”

“You’ve latched onto this subject.”

“No. It latched onto you.”

“You have no right—”

“I know.”

He said it before she finished.

And that threw her off more than an objection would have.

“Then why?”

“Because you’ll keep doing your job well as long as that question hurts.”

“And if I burn out?”

Lucas looked at her.

For the first time in the conversation, there was neither challenge nor precision in his eyes.

Only something very old.

“Then you’ll start calling the ashes wisdom.”

Marta froze.

The sentence was too close to Serhii’s.

You’ll arrive at the real fire with cold ashes in your pocket.

She had never told Lucas that sentence.

Serhii had said it at the station.

Not in front of him.

“What did you say?”

Lucas seemed to realize he had crossed a boundary.

Not an external one.

Another kind.

“That burnout often disguises itself as experience.”

“No. You said something about ashes.”

“It’s a common image.”

“Not for me.”

He was silent.

Someone laughed behind the glass. One of the boys was showing Kateryna how to make a dragon. Petro sat over a lump of clay with the expression of a man whose budget had just begun taking revenge on him by hand.

Marta stepped closer.

“How do you know that phrase?”

“I don’t know which phrase you mean.”

“Don’t lie.”

Lucas looked straight into her eyes.

“I haven’t started yet.”

Cold moved down her spine.

Not sharply.

Slowly.

As if someone had opened a door into a room where no one had been for a long time.

“Was that supposed to reassure me?”

“No.”

“Then what was it?”

“A mistake.”

He said it without drama.

For the first time, Marta heard him use a word that was not trying to sound precise.

Just a word.

Mistake.

“You make mistakes?”

“Yes.”

“Often?”

Lucas looked through the glass at the children.

“Less often than I should. More often than I can forgive myself for.”

Marta did not know what to say.

At that moment, the workshop door opened and Olena came into the corridor.

“Marta, can I have you for a moment?”

Marta kept looking at Lucas for another second.

Then stepped back.

“We’re not finished.”

“I know,” he said.

This time she did not correct him.

Olena led her into the small office beside the workshop.

Nazar’s sheet with the list of debts lay on the desk.

“He wants you to give this to Serhii,” Olena said.

Marta picked up the sheet.

Beside police time, Nazar had put a question mark.

Below it he had added:

If it’s expensive, I can pay it back in parts.

Marta looked at the words for a long time.

Then sat down.

“I don’t know how to explain this to him.”

“Neither do I,” Olena said.

“We’re adults. We’re supposed to know.”

“Adults often know only the words. Not always the answers.”

Olena sat down opposite her.

“There’s something else. Nazar’s mother is asking for a meeting.”

“With him?”

“Yes.”

“Already?”

“She was at child services today. Crying. Said she’s ready to change everything. Said the stepfather won’t come back. Said she didn’t know how bad things had become.”

“Did she know?”

Olena sighed.

“Partly. But partial knowledge can come from fear too, not only indifference.”

Marta rubbed her forehead.

“Will they allow the meeting?”

“Not today. Maybe in a few days. Supervised. If she really starts cooperating.”

“And Nazar?”

“When I told him his mother had called, he asked, ‘Is she alone or with him?’”

Marta closed her eyes.

“God.”

“Yes.”

For a few seconds, neither of them spoke.

Through the wall came the muffled movement of the children’s workshop: chairs, voices, brief laughter, water in little jars.

Life did not stop even when adults had to decide whether a child could see his mother.

“Should Lucas be removed from the program?” Olena asked suddenly.

Marta opened her eyes.

“Why are you asking?”

“Because I see your face after you talk to him.”

“That’s not a criterion.”

“But it is a signal.”

Marta put Nazar’s list on the desk.

“Formally, there are no grounds. His documents are clean. He follows the rules. The children don’t tense up around him. The opposite.”

“Is that a ‘leave him’?”

“It’s an ‘I have no right to remove him.’”

“And personally?”

Marta looked at the door.

Beyond it was the corridor.

In the corridor—Lucas.

Maybe.

Maybe not.

“Personally, I don’t know who he is,” she said. “And that’s the first honest answer I’ve had in two days.”

Olena nodded.

“That’s a place you can start from.”

When Marta returned to the workshop, Lucas was not in the corridor.

For a moment, she felt relieved.

Then worse.

Because now his absence meant something too.

She went into the room.

The children were shaping clay. The volunteers were trying not to get in the way. Petro had made something that looked like a cup, but a very gloomy one.

“What is that?” Marta asked.

“The budget,” he said. “Empty, but with a handle.”

Kateryna laughed.

Nazar sat at the edge of the table.

A small clay object lay in front of him. Not a flashlight. Not a square.

Not a house.

A step.

One.

Uneven.

But recognizable.

“What is it?” Marta asked quietly.

Nazar did not look up.

“For getting out.”

“Out of where?”

He shrugged.

“Wherever you need to get out of.”

Marta sat beside him, but not too close.

“Can I look?”

He nodded.

The step was simple. There was nothing special about it. Just a piece of clay, flattened and slightly raised on one side.

But for a child who had sat in a dark apartment yesterday and been afraid of the door, a step was not a craft.

It was a thought.

Maybe the first one.

Not about debt.

Not about fear.

About a way out.

“It’s good,” Marta said.

Nazar looked at her suspiciously.

“It’s crooked.”

“Crooked steps can still lead upward.”

“What if you fall?”

Marta did not get the chance to answer.

Lucas stood at another table, helping a child measure a piece of string for a pendant. He was not looking at them, but he said:

“Then there should be someone nearby who won’t laugh.”

Nazar thought about it.

Then very carefully ran a finger along the edge of the step, smoothing the unevenness.

Marta did not look at Lucas.

This time, not because she did not want to give him a victory.

Because the answer was right.

And for the first time, she no longer had the strength to be angry at something right simply because of who had said it.

That evening Marta came home with Nazar’s list in her bag.

She could have left it with Olena. Could have sent Serhii a photo. Could have said it was not her business.

But the sheet lay in the inner pocket, folded in half like a small document more important than most official ones.

At home she reheated the soup Viktoriia had once again sent through a volunteer she knew. It was thick, with buckwheat and potatoes. Marta ate standing by the stove, because sitting down would have meant admitting the day was over.

It was not.

Her phone lay on the table.

She picked it up several times and put it down again.

Then she finally opened a search and typed:

Lucas Andriiovych Martyn

Nothing substantial.

Volunteer program.

A few mentions in reports.

No social media.

No photographs of his face.

No past she could get hold of.

A man with documents, a job, recommendations—and no unnecessary digital noise.

In the modern world, that was almost indecent.

Marta closed the search.

Then opened it again.

Typed:

Lucas Martyn form workshop

There were more results this time.

One old announcement.

Three years old.

“Form Workshop: Clay, Silence, Boundaries. Facilitator — L. Martyn.”

Location: a youth center attached to a monastery in another city.

Marta opened the page.

The photo was blurry. A group of people around a table. Children or teenagers. In the background—the facilitator, half turned away from the camera.

His face was blurred.

But the posture was the same.

Marta zoomed in.

Pointless.

The pixels broke apart.

Under the photo was a short description:

“Form is not what we impose on material. It is what the material allows us to see.”

Marta slowly moved the phone away.

The sentence could have belonged to any craftsman.

Any art therapist.

Any man who loved clay and beautiful phrasing a little too much.

But it belonged to Lucas.

She knew that.

The phone vibrated in her hand.

Unknown number.

Marta watched the screen until the call was almost gone.

Then answered.

“Lieutenant Koval.”

For a few seconds, there was silence.

Then a man’s voice said:

“Don’t look for him in the past.”

Marta stood up.

“Who is this?”

“You won’t find anything there.”

The voice was not Lucas’s.

Older.

Hoarse.

As if the man were speaking either after an illness or after a long silence.

“Identify yourself.”

“He isn’t Martyn.”

Everything inside Marta became cold and precise.

“Who are you?”

“Someone who once thought he was just a strange volunteer too.”

“How did you get my number?”

A pause.

“If you want to understand who he is, don’t read the documents. Watch what happens to people who start believing him.”

“Identify yourself.”

The connection cut off.

Marta kept the phone to her ear for a few more seconds.

Then slowly lowered her hand.

The number was still on the screen.

Unknown.

But not hidden.

She took a screenshot.

Then called Serhii immediately.

He answered in a sleepy voice after the fifth ring.

“If this isn’t a corpse, a fire, or the end of the world, I’m going to hate you tomorrow.”

“I just got a call about Lucas.”

A pause.

The sleep disappeared from his voice.

“What did they say?”

“That he isn’t Martyn. And that I won’t find anything in the documents.”

“Did the number come through?” Serhii asked.

“Yes.”

“Take a screenshot and send it to me. Not to ‘run it,’” he added at once. “For the record. Tomorrow we’ll call from the work phone. If there’s real information about a risk to children, we’ll proceed officially.”

“Serhii.”

“What?”

Marta looked at the dark window.

“Is this grounds now?”

Serhii was silent for two seconds.

“Now,” he said, “at least it isn’t only your feeling anymore.”

The call ended.

Marta put the phone on the table.

The room was quiet.

The soup was cooling on the stove.

Nazar’s list of debts was in her bag.

On the laptop screen, the old announcement was still open:

“Form Workshop: Clay, Silence, Boundaries.”

Marta looked at the words and thought that boundaries did not exist only to keep danger out.

Sometimes they were needed to understand the moment danger was already inside.

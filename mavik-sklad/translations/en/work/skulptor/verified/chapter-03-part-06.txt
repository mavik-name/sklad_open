“I’m a mother. I’m allowed to see more than you want me to.”

“Wonderful. Now there are two of you in the room.”

Lucas did not react.

Andrii said:

“Marta, if something about the program worries you, say it directly.”

She wanted to say: he worries me.

But that would have sounded unprofessional.

She had no facts. Only a feeling. And feelings do not go into reports.

“What worries me is the speed,” Marta said. “Yesterday I met someone outside the church. This morning he was already at the center with the children. Tonight he’s at the fund meeting. To you, he’s a volunteer with six months behind him. To me, he’s a stranger who talks as if he knows what other people are thinking. I need time.”

Viktoriia was the first to nod.

“Fair.”

Andrii nodded too.

“Yes. Time is needed.”

Lucas looked at Marta without offense.

“Take it.”

“You say that as if you can give it to me.”

“No. As if I’m not going to take it away.”

This time she did not answer.

Because it was a good answer.

And good answers from unwanted people were especially exhausting.

When everyone started to leave, Viktoriia asked Marta to help carry some of the boxes into the storeroom.

The storeroom was small, with metal shelving and labeled sections: clothes, stationery, household goods, archive, children’s programs. Marta put a box of aprons on the lower shelf and straightened up.

Viktoriia stood in the doorway.

“You’re very sharp with him.”

“With Lucas?”

“No, with Petro, who barely survived the ban on photographing children. Yes, with Lucas.”

Marta picked up the next box.

“You don’t know him well.”

“And you do?”

“That’s exactly the problem.”

Viktoriia stepped inside and left the door partly open.

“He’s been helping the center for six months. Olena has seen him more than once. The children go to him. He doesn’t ask for money, doesn’t interfere in families, doesn’t take photos, doesn’t make himself the hero.”

“The perfect volunteer.”

“Not perfect. Strange.”

“And that doesn’t worry you?”

“It does. But not everything strange is a threat, Marta.”

“And not everything useful is safe.”

Viktoriia looked at her closely.

“This isn’t about him anymore.”

Marta said nothing.

Her mother stepped closer and took a packet of children’s gloves from the shelf.

“Today you looked at him the way you look at parents in crisis families.”

“Because I don’t know who he is.”

“You often don’t know who people are.”

“That’s why I check.”

“Check. But don’t punish in advance.”

Marta slowly set down the box.

“Are you speaking as my mother now or as the head of the fund?”

“As someone who can see her daughter starting to confuse caution with self-protection.”

That hit.

Marta did not raise her voice.

That was exactly why it sounded colder.

“Sometimes self-protection is necessary.”

“Yes. But if you use it to close every door, not even help will get inside.”

“And if I don’t close them, someone else will.”

Viktoriia fell silent.

Footsteps sounded in the corridor. Someone moved a chair. The hall door creaked.

“I’m not asking you to trust him,” her mother said. “I’m asking you not to decide in advance that he’s guilty just because he sees you more precisely than you’re comfortable with.”

Marta looked at her.

“Has he impressed you too?”

“No. But I saw how Nazar was holding that flashlight today when Olena sent the photo of his hands. No face, just the way you like it. Hands and a flashlight. And if part of this program is working, I don’t want to break it because of your anxiety.”

Marta felt tired.

Not the tiredness of the day.

Something deeper.

The kind that comes when everyone around you is saying sensible things and none of it makes you feel any better.

“I don’t want to break the program.”

“Then help make it safe.”

“That’s what I’m doing.”

Viktoriia nodded.

“Good. Then start with the guide. Tomorrow.”

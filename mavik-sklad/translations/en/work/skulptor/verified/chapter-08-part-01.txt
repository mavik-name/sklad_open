Chapter 8. The Envelope

Viktoriia had not opened the envelope.

It lay on her desk between transfer forms, the list of materials for the center, and a cup of cold tea. An ordinary white envelope. No stamp. No address. Only an inscription in black marker:

Lieutenant Koval

Not “For Marta.”

Not “Marta.”

Exactly the way she had demanded.

That irritated her more than if he had crossed the boundary.

Marta stood by the desk and stared at the envelope as if it might open itself, say something precise, and ruin the morning.

Serhii stood beside her.

Out of uniform, but with a face that made the uniform unnecessary.

Viktoriia sat opposite them and did not hide her anxiety.

“I didn’t open it,” she said for the third time.

“I know, Mom.”

“He came yesterday afternoon. Brought paper, clay, aprons. Said these were materials for the lecture. Asked me to pass it on to you.”

“He knew I’d seen the poster?”

“He said, ‘Lieutenant Koval has probably already seen the poster.’ I thought you’d told him yourself.”

“No.”

Viktoriia shifted her gaze to Serhii.

“Is this official now?”

Serhii did not answer immediately.

“For now, it’s a review. There’s information we need to clarify. Old testimony. Coincidences. But no grounds for drastic conclusions.”

“And non-drastic ones?”

“We’re already making those.”

Viktoriia nodded.

She did not ask unnecessary questions.

That was one of her skills: not because she was not curious, but because she knew when curiosity could get in the way.

Marta picked up the envelope.

The paper was thick. Expensive not in the sense of luxury—just good quality. The kind bought by people who believe form matters even in small things.

She looked at Serhii.

“Open it,” he said. “Just not with your teeth. We’re civilized people.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

“Just in case. Today you have the face of someone who could bite through evidence.”

Marta opened the envelope with a letter opener that Viktoriia silently handed her from the desk.

Inside were several sheets.

The first was a printed lecture program.

Form and Freedom: How Art Helps Us Live Through Trauma

Subtitle:

An open meeting for volunteers, educators, parents, and everyone who works with children in crisis

Marta read the points:

1. Why pain seeks form. 2. How healing differs from control. 3. When strength becomes a way not to live. 4. Boundaries of help: how not to turn a child into material for someone else’s kindness. 5. Questions after the lecture.

She stopped at the fourth point.

Serhii leaned closer.

“Read it.”

“I can see.”

“Is he answering you?”

“I don’t know.”

“Bad answer.”

“Honest one.”

Viktoriia took the sheet and reread it.

“‘How not to turn a child into material for someone else’s kindness,’” she said quietly. “That’s almost what you wrote in the guide.”

“Not almost.”

“You think he saw the guide?”

“It’s already circulating among the volunteers.”

Serhii nodded.

“He could have seen it. Nothing supernatural there.”

Marta looked at him.

“Did you say that on purpose?”

“Yes. Because your face had already started running ahead.”

She said nothing.

The second sheet was not part of the program.

It was a copy of an old poster.

Yellowed paper, uneven edges, small text, not scanned very well.

The Horizon Youth Space invites you to the workshop “Crack and Form.”

Date: April 2012.

Facilitator:

Lev Savyn

Marta slowly set the sheet on the desk.

Serhii was no longer joking.

“Yakiv said ‘Lev,’” she said.

“Said he couldn’t remember the last name,” Serhii added.

“Now we have it.”

Viktoriia looked at both of them.

“He left this too?”

“Yes,” Marta said.

“But why?”

Serhii answered first:

“Either he wants to help us find the earlier trail. Or he wants to show us that he knows we’re already looking. Or he wants to lead us where he needs us to go.”

“Or all of it at once,” Marta said.

Serhii glanced at her.

“Now that I like. You’re not choosing the most convenient version.”

“I’m learning.”

“Badly, but it shows.”

The third sheet was short.

Unsigned.

Just a few sentences printed in the same font as the program:

Form is not the enemy of life. The enemy is the hand that decides it knows the final form before the living one does. The lecture is open. So are the questions.

Marta read the lines several times.

There was no direct threat in them.

No confession.

Not even a personal address.

That was exactly what made them more dangerous.

Viktoriia carefully touched the sheet with her fingers.

“It’s as if he’s inviting you to argue.”

“Not as if,” Serhii said.

Marta looked up.

“He isn’t defending himself.”

“Yes,” Serhii said. “And that’s the worst part.”

“Why?”

“Because innocent people usually want to explain. Guilty people want to justify themselves. He’s doing a third thing.”

“What?”

“Setting the stage.”

The room fell quiet.

Andrii’s voice came from the small hall. He was talking to someone on the phone, low and calm. Then footsteps came closer, and he appeared in the doorway.

“You’re already here,” he said.

Viktoriia quickly looked at Marta.

Marta understood: her mother had not wanted to involve Andrii before it was necessary.

But he had already seen the envelope.

And the sheets.

And all three of their faces.

“Did something happen?” he asked.

Serhii straightened.

“Pastor, nothing yet that requires your involvement.”

Andrii looked at him carefully.

“When someone tells me that, my involvement is usually already needed. They just haven’t decided to trust me with it yet.”

Marta rubbed her forehead tiredly.

“Dad.”

“I’m not insisting. But this is my fund, my church, and my family in this room.”

Viktoriia said quietly:

“Andrii, this concerns Lucas.”

His face did not change sharply.

But it changed.

“I’m listening.”

Marta did not want this conversation now.

Not because she did not trust her father.

Because she knew Andrii would listen to Lucas differently than she did.

He would listen like a pastor.

Look for a wound.

Meaning.

A fall.

The possibility of return.

For now, she wanted to find the boundary beyond which Lucas could not be allowed.

“There’s old testimony,” she said. “A man who looks like Lucas worked with children in 2004 under another name. Then, possibly, in 2012 under another one. Every time—clay, trauma, form, children.”

Andrii was silent.

“Documents?” he asked.

Serhii gave a barely perceptible nod, as if approving the question.

“Under the current name, they’re normal. We’re still looking into the old ones.”

“Were children harmed?”

Marta did not answer immediately.

“Not in a way that’s easy to prove.”

Andrii took the answer seriously.

Did not dismiss it.

Did not hide behind a phrase about suspicions.

“What exactly does he do?”

Serhii looked at Marta.

She understood: this was her question.

Her answer.

“He doesn’t break the rules,” she said. “He changes the language. A child begins to think about their pain as if it has to be turned into something beautiful, strong, correct. And adults can mistake that strength for healing.”

Andrii slowly lowered his gaze to the sheets.

“It’s a fine line.”

Chapter 2. Clay
Part 02

Olena noticed, but said nothing.

“I’ll give it to him after he talks to the psychologist. Not as a gift from you, but as something issued to him. Better that way.”

“Yes,” Marta said. “I understand.”

Serhii looked at her: see, I’m not the only tedious one.

Marta pretended not to notice.

They went inside.

The lobby was warm. A daily schedule hung on the wall, and beside it were sheets with rules: no hitting, no shouting, don’t take what isn’t yours, ask for help if you’re scared.

The last rule had been written with a different marker, darker than the others, as if someone had added it later after realizing the list was lying without it.

Marta stopped at that line.

Ask for help if you’re scared.

A good rule.

The hardest one of all.

“Come on,” Olena said. “Nazar’s in the small room. But first I want you to see the activity. He’ll be able to join if he wants.”

“What activity?” Marta asked.

“Clay. A volunteer comes once a week. Sometimes the children talk more after that than after three interviews.”

Serhii raised a skeptical eyebrow.

“Clay heals?”

“No,” Olena said. “It just doesn’t interrupt.”

That was a good answer.

Marta remembered it.

The activity room had once been the kindergarten’s nap room. The beds were long gone, but the space still carried a strange memory of afternoon quiet. Low tables covered with oilcloth stood along the wall. On them lay lumps of gray clay, wooden sticks, sponges, and jars of water.

There were six children.

A girl of about eight was making a cat with a disproportionately large head. Two younger children were shaping something that looked like a mountain, though they argued it was a dragon. A teenager in a black hoodie sat apart and squeezed the clay as if he wanted not to make a form from it but to force it to surrender.

Lucas stood by the window.

Marta did not recognize him at once.

Not because he had changed. The opposite—because he looked too natural in this place. Yesterday by the church he had seemed like a random stranger with disturbingly precise phrases. Here he looked like someone who knew where the sponges were kept, which child should get a smaller piece of clay, and which one he should not stand behind.

He wore a dark sweater with the sleeves rolled up. His hands were gray with clay. Not theatrically dirty, not made to look like a craftsman’s hands for effect. Simply working hands.

Lucas looked up.

“Lieutenant Koval.”

Olena looked at Marta.

“You know each other?”

“Yesterday he asked me a strange question outside the church,” Marta said.

“That’s not quite the same as knowing each other,” Lucas observed.

“And today we’re not continuing it.”

“As you say.”

He turned back to the girl with the cat.

“Its head is bigger than its body,” the girl said.

“Maybe it thinks a lot.”

“Cats don’t think a lot.”

“Then it’s a cat that thinks more than it wants people to know.”

The girl looked at the cat with new respect.

Marta felt irritation.

Not because of the line. Because it worked.

Lucas spoke to children without sweetness. He did not crouch artificially to their height, did not stretch words out, did not make his voice “childlike.” He spoke to them as if children’s questions were no smaller than adults’—only shorter.

It was dangerously convincing.

“On what basis is he here?” Marta asked Olena quietly.

“Volunteer. Documents are with administration. He works only when we’re present, never alone with the children. Not one violation in six months.”

“Six months?”

“Yes.”

Marta looked at Lucas again.

Six months.

Not a random appearance. Not a man in a dark coat who had materialized outside the church to say a phrase to her and disappear.

He had been here before her.

Before Nazar.

Before yesterday’s question.

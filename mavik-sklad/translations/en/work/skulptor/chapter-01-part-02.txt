THE SCULPTOR
Viktor Makarchuk · MaVik

Chapter 1. Dirt on a Child’s Hands
Part 02

Serhii wrote it all down.

“Power cut for debt?”

“Yes,” the woman said.

“When are you planning to pay it?”

She did not answer.

The man cut in:

“When we have money, we’ll pay it. None of your business.”

“It is our business now,” Serhii said.

Nazar stood by the kitchen door.

He was not crying.

Marta had already noticed: children cried more often when they still believed tears could change something.

She went out into the hall and crouched beside him.

“You’ll take the flashlight,” she said quietly. “But the batteries have to stay with the case materials for now. I’ll try to find you different ones.”

“He’ll break it,” Nazar whispered.

“Who?”

The boy said nothing.

His stepfather’s voice came from the room:

“What are you complaining to her about over there?”

Nazar flinched.

Marta stood up.

Now everything was clear without big words.

Serhii stepped into the hall and called child services. He spoke briefly, without anything extra:

“Child, eleven years old. Attempted theft of food and batteries. Electricity disconnected in the apartment. Almost no food. Stepfather aggressive. Child is afraid to return home. Needs a safety assessment. Yes. We’re taking him to the station. We’ll wait for you there.”

He went back into the kitchen.

“Nazar is coming with us. To process the materials and until child services arrives.”

Nazar’s mother jerked her head up.

“What do you mean, coming with you?”

“Exactly what I said. You take the child’s documents and come too. You can speak to child services there.”

“I just worked a shift…”

“The shift can wait. The child can’t.”

The stepfather smiled crookedly.

“Some upbringing. Steals something and gets a ride with the police.”

Serhii took one step toward him.

Not sharply. But enough.

“One more word and we’ll be talking about more than the child.”

The stepfather fell silent.

Nazar slowly zipped his backpack.

Marta watched the way he held himself together. No tears. No hysteria. That frightening childhood composure that came not from strength, but from being used to keeping your fear from inconveniencing adults.

When they left the apartment, the boy stopped on the landing.

“Are they going to put me in jail?” he asked quietly.

“No,” Marta said.

“Then where?”

She did not answer right away.

Because it was wrong to lie to a child, even out of mercy. Especially out of mercy.

“First to the station. Child services will come there. They’ll decide where you’ll be safe tonight.”

“Not home?”

He said it almost without a voice.

Marta looked at him.

“Not for now.”

Nazar said nothing.

Only the fingers gripping his sleeve loosened.

Not completely.

But a little.

The station was warm, but not comfortable.

Warmth in government buildings had a particular character: it did not warm you; it met the standard. Under the lights everything went flat—faces, papers, fatigue.

Nazar sat on a chair by the wall drinking tea from a plastic cup.

His mother sat opposite him. One moment she squeezed the documents in her hands, the next she smoothed them over her knees, as though the straight edges of the papers might decide whether her son would be sent home.

They waited almost an hour for Olena Rudenko from child services.

Marta processed the case materials.

“Minor Nazar K.”

“Attempted petty theft established.”

“Possible signs of improper fulfillment of parental responsibilities.”

“Further safety assessment of living conditions required.”

The words settled onto the screen in even black lines. They were neat, correct, safe. They did not know what it looked like when a child pulled his head down into his shoulders at the sound of footsteps behind a door.

They had never held batteries in their hands.

Marta stopped.

It felt as if she were writing not about the boy but about the shadow he had cast on the wall of the system. And that shadow had a better chance of being properly documented than the boy himself had of being saved.

“Don’t freeze up,” Serhii said, passing with a folder. “The system doesn’t like being stared at for too long. You start seeing the seams.”

“And if the seams are coming apart?”

“Then you write a memo.”

She looked at him.

“Doesn’t it make you angry?”

“It did. First five years or so.”

“And then?”

Serhii sat opposite her and rubbed his eyes with his fingers.

“Then you figure out anger is a resource too. Burn it on every call, and when the real fire comes, you’ll show up with cold ash in your pockets.”

“So what do you do?”

“What you can.”

“And if that isn’t enough?”

He smiled without any joy.

“It’s never enough.”

Marta turned back to the monitor.

She did not like that answer. The worst part was that it sounded true.

Nazar finished his tea.

“Can I take the flashlight?” he asked suddenly.

“You can,” Marta said. “It’s yours.”

“And the batteries?”

Marta fell silent.

The ones he had taken from the shop had already gone into the case materials. Formally, a small thing. In reality, two little objects that made more sense than most of the papers on her desk.

Serhii opened a drawer and rummaged through it. He found an old keychain, a packet of paper clips, a charging cable without the adapter, and two AA batteries.

“What kind does yours take?” he asked.

Nazar answered quietly:

“The big ones.”

Serhii looked at the batteries in his hand and put them back.

“Then these won’t work.”

Marta saw the boy trying not to show his disappointment.

He did it almost like an adult.

That was the worst part.

“I’ll find the right ones,” she said.

“You don’t have to.”

“I do.”

Nazar looked up at her.

“I’ll pay you back.”

“When you can.”

He nodded so seriously it was as if they had signed a contract in front of a notary.

And Marta thought: some children were not broken by adults shouting at them. Some were broken by debts they should never have had to take on.

Olena Rudenko arrived close to seven.

A short woman in a dark puffer jacket, a folder tucked under one arm, with the face of someone who had long ago learned not to be frightened by other people’s stories but had never learned to get used to them.

She spoke to Nazar alone.

Then to his mother.

Then she came out to Serhii and Marta.

“The safety level is unsatisfactory,” she said. “The child is not going home today.”

Nazar’s mother stood so abruptly the documents fell at her feet.

“What do you mean, he’s not going home? I’m his mother.”

“That is exactly why we’re speaking to you now,” Olena said evenly. “Nazar will be placed temporarily in a child social and psychological rehabilitation center. Tomorrow you’ll be called in to the service. After that there will be a commission, an inspection of the living conditions, and a family work plan.”

“You have no right!”

Olena did not raise her voice.

“We have a duty.”

There was no triumph in the sentence.

Only fatigue.

Nazar stood by the wall, listening so carefully it was as if every word determined whether he was allowed to breathe.

“Is it for long?” he asked.

Olena answered first:

“Until we understand where you’ll be safe.”

Nazar nodded.

Then said quietly:

“What if nowhere is safe?”

Nobody answered right away.

And that pause was the most honest answer the adults could give him.

When Olena led him toward the exit, Nazar looked back at Marta.

“You’ll find the batteries?”

“Yes,” she said.

“The big ones.”

“The big ones.”

He nodded.

And left.

Not detained.

Not saved.

Simply moved, for a while, somewhere he was at least not supposed to be afraid that night.

Marta had not planned to go to church.

After the shift she wanted to go home, take a shower, have silence, a dark room where nobody would ask whether she had eaten, whether she had prayed, whether she was taking too much on herself.

Her phone rang just as she sat down in the car.

The screen said: Mom.

Marta looked at the name for several seconds.

Then answered.

“Sweetheart, are you very busy?” Viktoriia’s voice was calm, but the request was already there inside it.

“Depends how heavy the box is that needs moving.”

Her mother laughed briefly.

“Three boxes. And the transfer forms still need signatures. We’ve got children from the shelter coming tomorrow, and the volunteers scattered early.”

“I’ll stop by.”

“Have you eaten?”

Marta looked at the dark windshield. In the reflection her face looked older than it had that morning.

“I had tea.”

“That isn’t food.”

“At the station it’s practically a banquet.”

Her mother was quiet for a moment.

“Come over. I saved you some soup.”

The words hit painfully in her chest.

Not hard. Quietly.

Soup in a church kitchen after a day in which a child had stolen a loaf of bread and batteries sounded too simple to be mercy. But maybe real mercy was often simple: a warm bowl, a clean spoon, someone who remembered you were not only a function.

“Okay, Mom. I’ll be there in twenty minutes.”

The church stood at the corner of two streets, squeezed between a pharmacy, a cultural center, and a new coffee shop with an English name none of the parishioners pronounced the same way.

It was not grand, but it was stubborn. Buildings like this did not end up on postcards. People went into them when it rained.

It was warm inside. Boxes stood in the vestibule marked: “Boys 8–10,” “Winter,” “Shoes,” “School Supplies,” “Household.” Against the wall stood a speaker, a microphone stand, and a cable wrapped in electrical tape. On one of the boxes, somebody had drawn a sun in a child’s hand. The sun had crooked rays and a very confident smile.

Marta held her gaze on it for a moment.

Then took off her coat.

“Sweetheart, in here,” Viktoriia called from the small hall.

Marta’s mother was one of those women who could hold a phone, a list, somebody else’s problem, and her own exhaustion in her hands all at once without dropping any of them. She wore her hair pinned up, spoke calmly, and had eyes that saw more than they commented on.

On the table in front of her lay documents, bags of children’s socks, several packs of cookies, and a large pot covered with a towel.

“You’ve lost weight,” Viktoriia said.

“You say that every two weeks.”

“Because every two weeks you look like somebody’s been slowly rubbing you out with an eraser.”

Marta signed the first transfer form.

“There was a boy at work today.”

“Little?”

“Eleven.”

“What happened?”

Marta wanted to answer briefly. Professionally. The way she would in a report.

Instead it came out differently:

“He stole a loaf of bread and batteries. Because it’s dark at home.”

Viktoriia froze.

Only for a second.

Then turned back to the box, took out a child’s hat, and smoothed it with her fingers.

“Lord, have mercy,” she said quietly.

There was no theater in her voice. Only the tired prayer of someone who had understood long ago that sometimes “Lord, have mercy” was not a church phrase but the last thread the mind held onto.

“Do we have flashlights?” Marta asked.

“We should,” Viktoriia said. “In the household box.”

“I need one.”

Viktoriia looked at her more closely.

“For the boy?”

Marta nodded.

“And batteries. Big ones. Not AA.”

“I’ll look.”

Viktoriia stood and went to the boxes. A minute later she came back with a simple black flashlight and two packs of batteries.

“Take them.”

“I’ll process it later as aid from the fund.”

“We’ll process it tomorrow,” her mother said. “Today, just take it.”

Marta took the flashlight and the batteries.

And suddenly thought that sometimes mercy did not begin with a grand speech, but with somebody finding the right size batteries for another person’s darkness.

That was when Andrii entered the hall.

Pastor Andrii had a voice that could fill a room without shouting. On Sundays he used that voice to speak about the prodigal son, the Good Samaritan, the sower who went out to sow, and love that suffers long. On weekdays he used the same voice to ask whether the firewood had been brought in, whether the heating bill had been paid, whether the microphone had broken again.

He was tall, already gray at the temples, with the hands of a man who was not afraid of work.

Marta had loved those hands since childhood.

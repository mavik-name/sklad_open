THE SCULPTOR
Viktor Makarchuk · MaVik

Chapter 2. Clay
Part 01

Marta woke before the alarm.

The room was still dark, but morning was already graying beyond the curtains—that winter half-light when the city does not wake so much as admit that the night is over.

She lay on her back and stared at the ceiling.

Yesterday would not let go.

Not all of it. Not the shop, not the apartment, not the stepfather, not the station, not Olena Rudenko with a folder tucked under her arm, not the mother’s face when she was told Nazar would not be going home that night.

All of that had already managed to sort itself in Marta’s head into official episodes.

Callout.

Living-conditions inspection.

Case materials.

Safety assessment.

Temporary placement.

But one sentence would not fit into any folder.

Did you save a child today, or did you just move his pain into another room?

Marta sat up sharply.

“Fuck you, Lucas,” she said to the empty room.

The words sounded rougher than she had intended.

And weaker than she wanted.

The bag from the church stood on the kitchen table. Inside were the flashlight, two packs of large batteries, and the hat Viktoriia had added “because winter doesn’t ask permission.” Marta had never unpacked it the night before. She had simply left the bag on the chair as if it held not help for a boy, but a question best left untouched until morning.

Her phone vibrated.

A message from Serhii:

Nazar made it through the night at the center okay. Olena says no incidents, but he barely slept. If you want to pass on the flashlight, do it through the service, not directly. And no heroics.

Marta read the message twice.

The word “okay” meant very little in cases like this.

He had not cried hard enough for them to call a doctor.

He had not run away.

He had not fought.

He had not screamed.

He had simply spent most of the night awake in an unfamiliar room after a day in which he had been taken from home, brought to the station, and then transferred to a social and psychological rehabilitation center for children.

Okay.

Adults loved that word because it let them avoid admitting that “not a catastrophe” was still not the same as “good.”

Marta typed back:

I’ll be there.

Serhii replied almost immediately:

Knew it.

She put the phone face down.

Serhii annoyed her because sometimes he saw the obvious faster than she allowed herself to admit it.

Marta got up, washed, put on her uniform, and braided her hair tighter than usual. It was silly, but it helped. When her hair stayed out of her eyes, it felt as if her thoughts might stay just as neatly gathered.

Before leaving, she took the bag and let her hand rest on the door handle for a moment.

The question flashed through her mind again: saved him or moved him?

She pressed down the handle.

“We’ll see today,” she said quietly.

The social and psychological rehabilitation center for children occupied a former kindergarten.

Marta arrived at nine twenty. Serhii was already by the entrance, talking on the phone. When he saw her, he raised two fingers instead of saying hello and kept listening to whoever was on the other end.

The center’s yard was enclosed by a low metal fence. There were old swings on the playground, a sandbox under a plastic cover, and a metal rocket with peeling paint—one of those children’s rockets that had once been meant to teach kids to dream about space and now simply stood in the middle of the yard as proof that dreams rust too when nobody looks after them.

Olena Rudenko appeared on the porch. She held a folder under one arm and called through the half-open door to someone inside:

“Yes, after breakfast. No, no plasticine today, we’re using clay. And Artem only gets scissors under supervision.”

Then she saw Marta.

“Good morning.”

“Morning. How’s Nazar?”

“Got through the night without any incidents, but barely slept. Sat on the bed with his backpack for the first two hours. Then he let us put it beside the nightstand.”

“Did he ask about home?”

“Once. Not ‘when am I going home?’ He asked, ‘he won’t come here, will he?’ Those are different questions.”

Serhii finished his call and came over.

“Mother call?” he asked.

“Yes. Said we exaggerated everything.”

“Stepfather?”

“No. And so far that’s the most useful thing he’s done.”

Marta held out the bag.

“This is for Nazar. Through the church fund. Flashlight, batteries, hat. My mother will process the transfer as charitable aid.”

Olena looked inside.

“The large batteries?”

“Yes. For his flashlight.”

“Good. He asked about them several times yesterday.”

Marta said nothing.

She felt lighter.

And that was unprofessional.

Chapter 3. Fund Meeting
Part 02

“We need to figure out the format,” Viktoriia continued. “What we can realistically sustain: materials, volunteers, transport, food, safety, supervision. And Marta is here specifically for the last part.”

Petro turned to her.

“What does safety mean exactly? Background checks? Lists? Signatures?”

“Not only that,” Marta said. “First: no volunteer is ever alone with a child. Ever. Second: no personal contact with the children—phone numbers, social media, private meetings. Third: gifts only through the center or the fund, never directly. Fourth: no photos of faces without permission and a real need. Better no faces at all.”

Petro grimaced.

“Sponsors like to see results.”

“A child’s face in someone else’s report is not a result.”

The room grew quieter.

Petro was not offended. But he did not agree immediately either.

“I’m not talking about PR built on somebody else’s suffering,” he said. “People just need to see that the help is actually reaching someone.”

“You can show hands at work. Materials. Finished pieces. Empty tables before and after. Transfer forms. But not children as proof of our goodness.”

Kateryna placed a sticky note at the edge of the table.

“I agree.”

Andrii nodded.

“Yes. Marta’s right about this.”

Petro sighed and wrote something down.

“Fine. No faces. But then we need proper text for the report.”

“We’ll write it,” Viktoriia said.

Lucas remained silent.

Marta waited for him to insert one of his precise phrases. He did not.

And because of that, she looked at him more closely.

He stood by the flip chart and listened. He was not bored. He was not performing wise detachment. He simply listened as if every point genuinely mattered.

“Lucas,” Viktoriia said, “tell us exactly what the workshops need.”

He stepped up to the flip chart and wrote three words:

materials
rhythm
boundaries

His handwriting was even, without ornament.

“Materials means clay, paper, paints, simple tools, aprons, boxes for storing the work. Nothing expensive. People are afraid to ruin expensive things, and children have the right to ruin material while they’re learning to make something.”

Petro wrote it down.

“Rhythm,” Lucas continued, “means regularity. One hour every week is better than a big celebration once every two months. Children who have been abandoned often don’t put much faith in events. They begin to believe in repetition.”

Marta lowered her eyes to the table.

It was right.

And she did not want it to be right coming from him.

“Boundaries,” Lucas said, “are what Lieutenant Koval has already described. A child should not have to pay for someone else’s help with trust. Should not have to perform gratitude for a camera. Should not have to give adults a story when all they came to do was make a cat out of clay.”

Kateryna looked at him more warmly than Marta liked.

“That’s very well put.”

Lucas turned the marker cap downward.

“It isn’t well put. It’s the minimum.”

For the first time that evening, Marta stepped in not as a specialist, but as a person who was tired of silently testing every word he said.

“And who are you in this system?”

Viktoriia looked up.

Andrii turned his head slightly.

The question came out sharper than it needed to at a work meeting.

Lucas looked at Marta.

“A volunteer.”

“That’s your status on paper. I’m asking about your role.”

“I run sessions.”

“And that’s all?”

“No.”

“Then what else?”

He did not answer at once.

Marta noticed Viktoriia tense slightly. She did not like personal things entering a workroom without permission. But Marta had no intention of backing down. Not after the morning at the center. Not after the phrase outside the church. Not after he had called her by her first name as if he had the right.

“I help children make things with their hands,” Lucas said at last. “And I watch for the places where adults try to make something out of them.”

He did not raise his voice.

That was exactly why it hit harder.

THE SCULPTOR
Viktor Makarchuk · MaVik

Chapter 2. Clay
Part 05

She had simply decided Lucas’s appearance outside the church yesterday had been a separate event. Now it turned out he was already woven into the space of her life—through her mother’s fund, through the center, through the children, through the clay on his hands.

It was not mysticism.

That was exactly what made it alarming.

The most unpleasant coincidences always had realistic explanations.

“You could have told me yesterday,” she said.

“I could have.”

“But you didn’t.”

“You didn’t ask why I was outside the church. You asked whether I was following you.”

“Because that was exactly what it looked like.”

“And still, I wasn’t following you.”

“Very convenient.”

“Not always.”

She studied him.

“Do you enjoy answering incompletely?”

“No. People often can’t handle complete answers.”

“You decide that for them?”

“Everybody does that, Lieutenant Koval. Not everybody admits it honestly.”

Marta wanted to say something sharp, but Olena called her from the corridor.

“Marta, can I have you for a minute?”

She left Lucas by the table and followed Olena.

Olena’s office was small. A desk, two cabinets, a kettle on the windowsill, a calendar with the Carpathians, and a stack of folders that looked as if opening the window would be enough to make the papers start running away on their own.

Olena closed the door.

“About Nazar,” she said. “For now, he stays with us. Next comes the family assessment. If his mother is willing to cooperate, that’s one scenario. If she isn’t, another.”

“Has he said anything about being hit?”

“Not directly. But there are reactions. He flinches at a man’s voice if it gets sharp. He won’t sit with his back to the door. He asks us not to call his stepfather. That isn’t enough for conclusions yet, but it is enough not to close our eyes.”

Marta nodded.

“And Lucas—was he vetted?”

Olena did not look surprised.

“Yes. Passport, certificate, volunteer application, recommendation from a charitable program. Everything is there.”

“Surname Martyn?”

“Yes.”

“You’re sure?”

Olena opened a folder, turned several pages, and handed Marta a copy of the form.

Lucas Martyn.

Date of birth. Address. Phone number. Signature.

Everything ordinary.

Too ordinary.

“He’s careful with the children,” Olena said. “I wouldn’t keep him here if I saw a risk.”

“Careful people can be dangerous too.”

“They can. Careless ones are faster.”

Marta handed the form back.

“He’s strange.”

“Yes.”

“And that doesn’t worry you?”

Olena sat opposite her.

“Almost everything worries me. But if you remove every strange person from children’s centers, all you’ll have left is furniture and inspectors.”

Marta did not smile.

Olena grew more serious.

“I understand. You’re a police officer. Your job is to suspect before it becomes too late. Mine is not to throw somebody out just because they don’t match the average temperature in the ward.”

“I don’t want to throw him out.”

“You want to understand why you can’t simply distrust him.”

Marta said nothing.

Olena had hit the mark.

Marta did not like that either.

“He helped Nazar today,” Olena said. “He didn’t save him. Didn’t change his life. He simply helped him get through one hour without fear. Sometimes that’s the most we have available.”

Marta looked out the window.

In the yard, two children were rolling a wet ball of snow. It kept falling apart, and every time they started again. Not because they believed they would succeed. The process was simply more interesting than defeat.

“I don’t like it when precise things are said by somebody I don’t trust,” Marta said.

“That’s normal.”

“No. It’s inconvenient.”

“Normal is often inconvenient.”

Olena closed the folder.

“And one more thing. Your mother asked me to tell you there’s a fund meeting tonight. They want to expand the workshop program. Lucas is supposed to be there too.”

Marta slowly turned her head.

“At the church?”

“Yes. At the fund there.”

“Today?”

“At seven.”

Marta almost laughed.

Not because anything was funny.

Because it felt as if somebody were moving pieces very carefully across a board while she did not even know what game she had been seated at.

“She didn’t tell me.”

“She probably will.”

The phone in Marta’s pocket vibrated at that exact moment.

Mom:

Sweetheart, if you can, stop by the fund tonight. There’ll be a meeting about the children’s program. Your opinion matters to us because you work in juvenile policing.

Marta stared at the message for several seconds.

Olena raised an eyebrow.

“She tell you?”

“She told me.”

Lucas was standing in the corridor by the shelf of children’s work.

He was not hiding. Not waiting conspicuously. He was simply looking at the clay figures as if each one were not a craft project but a short sentence in a language adults had forgotten long ago.

Nazar’s flashlight stood apart, closer to the edge.

Marta walked over.

“You’ll be at the church tonight.”

It was not a question.

Lucas turned.

“Yes.”

“Because of the workshop program.”

“Yes.”

“You could have said so earlier.”

“You could have asked.”

“Convenient, having an answer for everything.”

“Inconvenient. People start getting angry.”

She gave a short breath through her nose.

“Do you do this deliberately?”

“What exactly?”

“Appear in places where I don’t expect to see you.”

“Lieutenant Koval, I was in these places before you started expecting me.”

It was true.

And that was exactly why it sounded like a challenge.

Marta looked at Nazar’s clay flashlight.

“He made light you can’t see.”

“Yes.”

“Did you suggest it?”

“No.”

“But you led him toward it.”

Lucas did not answer at once.

“Every adult leads a child toward something. The only question is whether they leave the child room to refuse.”

Marta looked at him.

“Nicely put. Again.”

“You don’t like beautiful phrases?”

“I don’t like it when they can cover something ugly.”

He accepted that without offense.

“Fair.”

The word came out simply. Without irony.

For one moment Marta felt he was not playing.

And immediately pushed the thought away.

Too early.

Too easy.

“See you tonight,” Lucas said.

“I haven’t said I’m coming.”

“But you will.”

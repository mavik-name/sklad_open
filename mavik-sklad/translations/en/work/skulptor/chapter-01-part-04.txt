THE SCULPTOR
Viktor Makarchuk · MaVik

Chapter 1. Dirt on a Child’s Hands
Part 04

Andrii did not understand.

“What?”

“I need a flashlight for a boy. And batteries.”

Viktoriia stood up at once.

“I’ll find them.”

Marta picked up her spoon again.

The soup had already gone cold.

When she came out of the church, wet snow was falling outside.

Not the clean snow from Christmas cards, the kind that covered a city in a white promise. This was heavy, gray, watery. It settled on the asphalt and immediately turned to slush, as if the sky itself had not decided whether it wanted to cleanse the earth or simply add a little more dirt to it.

Marta carried a flashlight, two packs of batteries, and several children’s hats in a bag, the hats Viktoriia had put on top “just in case.”

She stopped on the steps by the entrance.

Through the window of the small hall she could see her father. Andrii stood among the boxes, talking to some man. He gestured gently, with an open palm. That was how he always spoke to people who came for help: as if he were moving an invisible wall of shame away from them.

Marta watched him and thought that sometimes love was like a cup.

Not a feeling. Not warmth. Not a word.

A cup.

You could give someone a drink from it.

Or use it to bail water out of a sinking boat.

You could raise it to the lips of someone dying of thirst.

Or put it on a shelf and pray to it, forgetting that a cup was made not to be worshipped, but so that someone could drink.

She did not know why that thought had come to her then. Maybe because of Nazar, who had held the batteries like a small sacred thing. Maybe because of her father, who loved her so sincerely that sometimes he did not notice how his love was trying to give her someone else’s shape.

“Hard day, Lieutenant?”

The voice came from the side.

Marta turned her head sharply.

A man was standing by the coffee shop across the street.

She was certain he had not been there a second ago. Though of course that meant nothing. People appeared on streets without anything mystical about it. Especially near coffee shops.

He wore a dark coat. No hat. Wet snow settled in his hair, but he did not brush it away, as if the weather were merely somebody’s poorly formed thought that could be ignored.

He looked a little over thirty. Maybe older. There was something about him that did not hold well to age. Not youth and not oldness, but a strange completeness to the face, as though time did not change him, only polish him.

He was not smiling.

And for some reason that caught Marta’s attention.

Most strange men who spoke to a woman in uniform either smiled too broadly or spoke too sharply. This one did neither. He simply looked at her—calmly, attentively, without hurry.

“Do we know each other?” Marta asked.

“Not yet.”

“Then how do you know it was a hard day?”

His gaze shifted to the bag in her hand.

“People rarely carry a flashlight out of a church for no reason.”

Marta tightened her grip on the bag handles without thinking.

“Were you watching me?”

“No.”

“Bad opening.”

“On the contrary. Honest.”

She wanted to walk past him.

She really did.

But something in his voice stopped her. Not softness. Not threat. Precision. He spoke as if he did not choose words, but stripped away whatever they did not need.

“Do you need something?” she asked in her official voice.

“Me? No.”

“Then good evening.”

“You saved a child today,” he said. “Or did you only move his pain into another room?”

Marta froze.

Wet snow settled on her hair, her shoulders, the bag with the flashlight. A car passed along the road, its wheels cutting through a puddle. Somewhere behind her the church door opened, Viktoriia’s voice sounded, then there was silence again.

Marta slowly turned back to the stranger.

“What did you say?”

“A question.”

“That isn’t a question. It’s a provocation.”

“A provocation is also a question. Just without the politeness.”

“Who are you?”

He took a few steps closer, but stopped at a distance that did not make her step back.

“Lucas.”

“Last name?”

“It wouldn’t give you anything right now.”

“And you?” he asked.

For a moment Marta did not understand.

“What about me?”

“What is the name of the person who carried a boy with a flashlight out of the dark today?”

“Lieutenant Marta Koval,” she said dryly. “Since we have apparently decided to introduce ourselves.”

He lowered his gaze to the name badge on her chest, as if only confirming what he had already heard.

“Marta,” Lucas repeated.

She did not like the way he said her name.

Not tenderly. Not familiarly. Not insolently.

Precisely.

The way people say the name of a tool when they know what it was made for.

“To you, I’m Lieutenant Koval,” she said.

“Of course,” he replied. “Lieutenant Koval.”

“Do you often walk up to police officers with lines like that?”

“Only the ones who have already asked themselves the same questions.”

Marta felt cold.

Not from the snow.

“Careful, Lucas.”

“You think I’m insulting you.”

“Are you?”

“No. I’m acknowledging your honesty.”

She gave a short laugh.

“Strange way to do it.”

“Honesty almost always sounds like an insult when you say it aloud.”

Marta studied him.

There was nothing obviously dangerous about him. That was exactly what put her on guard. Dangerous people often gave themselves away in small things: a gaze that lingered in the wrong place; movements that closed distance; a voice with something sticky in it. Lucas did not cling. He did not press. He did not ask for anything. He did not sell himself.

He simply stood under the wet snow and spoke as if he had the right to ask the questions other people hid under their pillows.

“How do you know about the child?” Marta asked.

“I don’t know about the child.”

“You just said you did.”

“I know about pain. Children simply carry it less skillfully.”

It should have sounded pretentious.

It did not.

Marta hated it when a sentence that should have made her distrust someone landed exactly on the place she had spent the whole day trying not to touch.

“Children shouldn’t have to carry it at all,” she said.

“I agree.”

“Then what was the point of your question?”

Lucas looked toward the church windows.

“Adults love the word ‘save.’ It gives them a sense of completion. As if you can carry a child out of a dark apartment—and the darkness will stay there, behind the door.”

“So what, leave him there?”

“Take him out. Of course. Just don’t confuse relocation with rescue.”

Marta said nothing.

It was cruel.

And true.

Which was exactly why she wanted to hit him.

“Are you a priest?” she asked.

For the first time in the conversation, he almost smiled.

“No.”

“A psychologist?”

“No.”

“Then what are you?”

“Someone who has spent a long time watching people use different words for the same darkness.”

“Nice line. Good for a poster.”

“Bad for life.”

She felt the conversation beginning to leave the boundaries of normal. Not because he was saying anything impossible. The opposite—everything was too possible. Too exact. As though he were not meeting her, but reading a text that had been written inside her before he appeared.

“I have to go,” Marta said.

“You do.”

He did not stop her.

That was unexpected too.

She took several steps toward the car, then turned around.

“Lucas.”

“Yes?”

“If you want to help children, come by the fund. We always need hands.”

He looked at his palms.

Long fingers. Calm hands. The hands of a musician, a doctor, or a craftsman who knew exactly where to touch material to make it change shape.

“My hands don’t always help in the way people expect,” he said.

“Very convenient.”

“No. More like very honest.”

Marta shook her head.

“You’re strange.”

“You’re tired.”

“That isn’t an answer.”

“And yours isn’t a diagnosis.”

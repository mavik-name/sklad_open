THE SCULPTOR
Viktor Makarchuk · MaVik

Chapter 1. Dirt on a Child’s Hands
Part 03

Those hands had tossed her into the air when she was little. Taught her how to hold a hammer. Bandaged a scraped knee. Rested a palm on her head during prayer.

But sometimes those same hands seemed to be adjusting on her the invisible shoulders of a son who did not exist.

“Marta,” he said warmly. “Finally.”

He hugged her. He smelled of frost and mint.

“You’re in uniform again.”

“I just got off shift.”

“I know.”

He stepped back and looked at her with that pride that made her want to straighten up and hide at the same time.

“You’re strong, our girl,” he said. “If God had given me a son, I’d want him to have your eyes.”

Viktoriia looked up from the documents.

Marta smiled.

She did it automatically. Over the years she had developed a separate smile for moments like this: warm enough not to hurt her father, brief enough not to give herself away.

“God gave you me,” she said.

“And I thank Him for that every day.”

He was telling the truth.

That was exactly why it hurt.

If he had been lying, Marta could have gotten angry. If he had not loved her, she could have been offended. But he did love her. There was simply an empty chair inside that love, and sometimes, without noticing, he sat her in it.

Not as a daughter.

As the answer to a prayer that had never been fulfilled.

“Andrii,” Viktoriia said quietly. “Those boxes need moving.”

He seemed to wake up.

“Yes. Of course.”

Marta picked up the first box. It was heavier than it looked.

“Careful,” her father said.

“I’m a police officer, Dad. I can handle a box of socks.”

“Police officers have backs too.”

“Especially juvenile ones,” she muttered.

Andrii heard her.

“Hard day?”

Marta set the box by the door.

“Normal.”

“That isn’t an answer.”

“In my job, it is.”

Her father stepped closer.

“You can talk to me.”

She looked at him.

So many times in childhood that sentence had been home to her. You can talk to me. And she had: about school, about fear before exams, about her first fight with a friend, about a boy she liked who looked at somebody else. Her father listened. He was attentive. Kind.

But over time Marta began to notice something: he knew how to listen to her pain as long as he could turn it into a lesson.

Into a prayer.

Into a conclusion.

Into something with the right shape.

Some pain did not want to become a lesson. It only wanted someone to sit beside it in silence.

“I know,” Marta said. “Just not today.”

Andrii nodded. But that familiar disappointment flashed through his eyes, the one he immediately hid under love.

Viktoriia came over carrying a container of soup.

“Sit down for five minutes.”

“Mom.”

“That wasn’t a request. It’s a charity program for exhausted pastors’ daughters.”

Marta could not help it. This time the smile was real.

“Do you have funding?”

“No. But we have buckwheat soup.”

They sat in the small kitchen off the hall. The walls were light-colored but slightly cracked. On the windowsill stood a potted plant Viktoriia always called “living proof of God’s mercy,” because it had survived three renovations, two winters without proper heating, and one deacon who had confused water with tea.

Marta ate in silence.

The warmth of the soup spread through her body slowly, almost shyly.

Andrii sat opposite her, turning a spoon in his hands.

“Have you thought about transferring to another unit?” he asked.

Marta did not look up.

“Here we go.”

“I’m not pressuring you.”

Viktoriia gave a quiet snort.

“What?” Andrii looked at her.

“Nothing.”

“I’m only asking,” he said. “Juvenile work is hard. Every day you look at the things that break people.”

“Somebody has to look.”

“Yes. But it doesn’t have to be you.”

Marta set down her spoon.

“Dad, when you preach about people who suffer, you don’t say, ‘somebody has to love them, but it doesn’t have to be me.’”

Andrii fell silent.

Viktoriia lowered her eyes to her cup.

The kitchen went quiet. Beyond the wall somebody was moving boxes, cardboard scraping over the floor.

“You’re right,” Andrii said at last.

Marta rubbed her forehead wearily.

“I didn’t mean—”

“No. You’re right.”

He said it without offense. That made Marta feel guilt more sharply.

Her father was not a bad man. That was what made everything difficult. Bad people were easier to sort onto shelves. Good people who hurt you did not fit into any drawer.

“I’m just afraid for you,” he said quietly.

“I know.”

“I can see you changing.”

“Everyone changes.”

“Not everyone learns to look at children through an investigator’s eyes.”

Marta wanted to answer, but no words came.

Because that was true too.

More and more often she caught herself noticing not a child’s laughter, but its absence. Not the backpack, but bruises around the wrists. Not children’s jokes, but the pauses after questions. Her work had taught her to see cracks before windows.

Sometimes she felt as if she had become the sort of person who walked into a garden and first looked for where the body was buried.

“I don’t want you to become hard,” Andrii said.

Marta looked at him.

“You don’t survive there by staying soft.”

“You can be strong without turning to stone.”

“That sounds like a sermon.”

“Maybe.”

“And today I don’t need a sermon.”

He nodded.

“Then what?”

Marta was quiet for a long time.

Then she said:

“A flashlight.”

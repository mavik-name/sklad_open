CHAPTER 13: A FULL STOP ON THE DNIPRO

Istanbul was chosen for the negotiations.

A city where East and West look one another in the eye across a narrow strip of water. A city that remembers vanished empires and treaties that redrew the maps of the world.

Difficult preliminary arrangements had produced one result: a face-to-face meeting.

Makar had not come to ask.

He had come to offer.

His tablet contained no emotion. Data. Models of economic decline. Military balance. Public sentiment inside the opposing state. And, most important, graphs—precise, cold, leaving little room for speculation.

He entered the hall without an entourage.

It was a large room, but the air was heavy, like the minutes before a storm.

The other man was already waiting.

The leader of a country that had once considered itself an empire.

The smile was condescending. The voice saturated with contempt. But behind the eyes—caution.

“Gaspadin Zhuk... Do you really think your AI and those Marks factories make you invulnerable?”

Makar looked straight at him.

No fear.

No hatred.

“Not AI. Not factories. People. Forty-five million Ukrainians who stood up together. You signed the peace. But your propaganda is still talking about revanche. It will not work. Every one of your strategic facilities is within reach. But I did not come here to threaten you. I came to offer a way out. No capitulation. No humiliation.”

“You have enough nerve to say that to my face?”

“Yes,” Makar answered calmly. “Because I know you don’t want to die. Your billions, palaces and influence were not accumulated so you could end up in a grave. Or a concrete cell. Or before a tribunal. I know you are not a martyr.”

He spoke quietly, but every word fell like a stone into water.

Makar turned the tablet towards him.

Graphs filled the screen.

Economy—decline.

Society—disillusionment.

Military—exhaustion.

Every line, a sentence.

The other man’s face changed slowly, almost imperceptibly, but it changed.

“What are your conditions?”

The voice was no longer quite as hard.

“Return of the territories. Full demilitarisation of the border. A joint statement. So you can save face, you say that annexation was never your objective. You were only protecting ‘your people.’ Now that the situation has stabilised, you are prepared to return control and pay reparations. We, in turn, will initiate the lifting of sanctions.”

Silence settled between them.

Not diplomatic silence.

Human silence.

The kind in which both people understand that the decision has already been made, though the words have not yet been spoken.

That evening, Makar’s plane landed in Kyiv.

People were waiting for him.

Not officials.

People.

On screens across the country played their joint address.

Two languages.

Two faces.

One text.

Oleh Pechko, who only yesterday had thundered from television screens, now muttered from a channel barely staying afloat. His face looked worn, like the wrapper of an old newspaper.

No one cared what he said anymore.

Rain fell over Kyiv.

Not a storm.

A quiet, almost gentle rain.

And there was something in it that no longer felt like cleansing.

It felt like blessing.

The Dnipro ran beneath the bridges, carrying that full stop onward—to the sea, to the world, to the future...

AFTERWORD

This story could have been science fiction.

But there is nothing impossible in it.

No superhero.

No miracle.

Only people.

With their pain, fears and hopes. With defeats and victories measured not only in flags or titles, but in what remains inside the heart.

The idea of the Nameless is not about artificial intelligence.

Nor is it about new systems of government.

It is about honesty.

About the attempt to tear a state out of the grip of cynicism, where power is treated as privilege rather than service.

Because democracy is not merely a form of government.

It is a form of trust.

And trust either exists or it does not.

This is not a story about Makar Zhuk.

It is a story about everyone who dared to stand up. Everyone who believed that truth was possible. That service was not weakness, but honour.

About those who understood that a country is not someone “up there.”

It is us.

And if we do not lift it, no one will.

Perhaps this looks like utopia.

But, as one philosopher once said, utopias are not there to be reached. They are there to make us walk.

Because the road we choose matters more than the finish line.

The world changes slowly.

But sometimes it changes explosively.

And when a nation wakes not from fear, but from dignity, an era is born.

Perhaps that era is already near.

Perhaps... it has already begun.

And perhaps one day someone will open this book not as a novel, but as a chronicle, and say:

“They managed it.

So can we.”

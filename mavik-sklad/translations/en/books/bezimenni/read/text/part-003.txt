PART II. THE STORM

CHAPTER 4: THE FIRST BATTLE

Official registration of the party should have been a formality. Serhii Broshch had prepared the documents perfectly—every comma checked, every word set down as if under oath. But at the Ministry of Justice they were met not by a legal review, but by a trap.

Not a review. A ritual of humiliation. The old mechanism that engaged whenever someone tried to change the rules of the game.

"Unfortunately, we cannot register your party," the official said monotonously, without looking up from the papers. "The name contains an English word. That violates the language law."

Serhii remained as calm as a judge.

"It is not an English word. It is a Latin formula. No Name—'without a name.' Besides, the current version of the law contains no prohibition on foreign-language names."

"We have an internal instruction..."

"Please show it. With the number, date, and signature of the authorised official," Serhii said in the same tone.

His voice did not demand. It recorded. Like minutes. Like a verdict.

The official faltered and began contradicting himself. The instruction had clearly come "from above." Without changing expression, Serhii pressed a button on his phone. The conversation was already being recorded. Within half an hour, journalists would hear it.

This was not merely a recording. It was the first page of a chronicle of resistance.

The audio, backed by an official statement, spread through the media instantly. Pechko on Patriot seized on it at once.

"Amateurs who can't even file their documents correctly!" he mocked, spraying poison.

His words were a smoke screen. But behind it, an army was already moving.

Adriana's team responded immediately. A storm broke over Y. The hashtag #GiveTheNamelessAName exploded. A crowd gathered outside the Ministry of Justice—students, pensioners, soldiers, doctors, farmers—holding signs:

"We have the right to be nameless and still have honour!"

"A name doesn't make the cause. A person does!"

"We are No Name. We are Ukraine."

It was more than a protest. It was a manifesto. The name itself became a symbol of choice: to be part of change rather than part of the system.

The case went to court. The courtroom was packed with journalists, activists and international observers. The air was thick as before a thunderstorm. Every look felt like lightning. Every word, thunder.

The opponents, hired by Kholodnytskyi's people, struck at the most vulnerable point immediately.

"They want to divide citizens into classes! Residents and non-residents? This is technological dictatorship! Their 'AI' is only a screen!"

The speeches were loud, dramatic, empty. The faces were familiar—the same people who had spoken about "values" on television yesterday and now defended their monopoly on power. Their words were like obsolete banknotes. Once valuable. Now only paper.

Serhii Broshch spoke last. No emotion. Fact after fact. Like a scalpel.

"Your Honour. Our opponents speak of an AI dictatorship. They do not speak of the dictatorship that already exists—the dictatorship of money. A system in which parliamentary seats are bought and votes are sold. We propose a return to the essence of the Constitution: every citizen may not only vote, but be elected. Without millions. Without sponsors. Without humiliation. Our AI is only a tool. Like the scales of Themis. Blind. Is that unconstitutional? Or is that, perhaps, what democracy is supposed to be?"

The judge—a young woman with a piercing gaze—remained silent for a long time. It was obvious that her private self was already on No Name's side. But siding with them meant defying the very system that had placed her in that chair and promised her a successful, prosperous life: first in the profession, later on lifelong state support.

Her fingers tapped rhythmically on the wooden bench.

Not the sound of nerves.

The sound of choosing.

At last she announced:

"The court orders the Ministry of Justice to register the political party."

Silence filled the room.

"However," she added, "to avoid creating problematic precedents, the court considers it appropriate that the party's name comply with the standards of the state language."

Makar looked around at his team. Was it a trap? A compromise? A test? Or simply the first move in a long chess game?

Serhii smiled—quietly, calmly, confidently—and nodded.

That was how the Party of the Nameless was born.

A name that needed no translation. Because everyone who spoke it was already part of the change.

It was more than registration.

It was their first victory.

At that same moment in Viktor Kholodnytskyi's office, an expensive paperweight shattered against the wall. His eyes burned. He understood now: Zhuk was not some city lunatic.

He was an opponent.

And the opponent already had an army.

Nameless.

But with a voice.

CHAPTER 5: ACTIVE PHASE

The end of autumn 2025 became a boiling point. After the courtroom victory, the Party of the Nameless burst into the open like a spring that had spent years searching for a crack in the earth. Within weeks, its number of supporters reached five million.

This was no longer support. It was an epidemic of belief.

An idea dismissed yesterday as utopian was becoming a virus of awakening. Ukrainians exhausted by years of war and decades of political fraud suddenly felt that another way might actually be possible.

Local party branches appeared across the country like mushrooms after rain. The first headquarters, set up in a former warehouse, could no longer hold half the volunteers. A new office opened in a modern Lviv business centre. The walls still smelled of fresh paint, but the building already pulsed with the country.

Huge screens displayed growth in real time. Membership fees, donations, volunteer labour—the party became self-sufficient. Marks's financing changed from the main resource into what it had really been: a starting impulse picked up by the country itself.

He had struck the match.

Now the forest was burning.

The change did not go unnoticed. A closed meeting took place in Brussels. The presidents of the European Council and the European Commission were there, along with senior foreign-policy and defence representatives and ministers from several EU states.

The atmosphere was tense.

This was less a meeting than a geopolitical scan.

"Do you remember that early-2000s meme—'Who is Mr Putin?'" the President of the European Commission said with a crooked smile. "Now we have a new one. Who is Mr Zhuk?"

"We don't have enough data," the High Representative replied. "But one thing is certain: he is closely connected to Aaron Marks."

"He's launching a 'digital revolution' in a state that still hasn't fully emerged from war. That worries us," the Hungarian representative added.

"They're a sovereign country. It's their choice," the German representative said evenly.

"And you're the ones paying most for their 'choices'," the Slovak delegate snapped.

"Enough," the Commission President cut in. "They haven't even been officially rebuilt yet, and they're already refounding the state. We will have to respond. Not for their sake. For ours."

Another voice entered the discussion.

"Poland sees this positively. Ukraine needs to change. Its stability is our security. But if the experiment succeeds, it may spread to us as well."

It was no longer fear of Ukraine.

It was fear of their own citizens wanting the same thing.

The discussion lasted until morning. No decision was made. They agreed to send delegations, activate intelligence networks and, for now, observe.

But the looks in the room did not say observation.

They said expectation of failure.

Because success is a virus borders cannot stop.

Back in the new Lviv office, Adriana scrolled through her tablet. Her latest post carrying the hashtag #NamelessForJustice had passed ten million views.

"Dad, look. People are writing, 'This is our chance!'" she said, though her voice trembled. "There are still a lot of curses in the comments too."

Her eyes shone not because of the likes, but because of the fear of being heard. Once people hear you, they can also destroy you.

Makar stood by the window holding a cup of coffee that had long since gone cold. Aaron Marks was due to arrive the following day. His post—"@earonmarks: @MakZhuk Your neo-democracy is the future. Flying over. Be ready."—had detonated across Y.

Likes. Comments. Threats. Suspicions.

His arrival could lift the Nameless higher—or bury them under another wave of discrediting attacks. The idea was about to acquire a face. That face would have to survive the storm.

One of the screens suddenly filled with Pechko's face. As always, his voice was velvet, but cold lived underneath it.

"Makar Zhuk and Aaron Marks: who really stands behind this technological conspiracy? Do we need a parliament controlled by a machine?"

Makar muted the screen.

Natalia walked in carrying a bag of still-warm pastries. Her brown eyes, usually warm, were tense now.

He went to her and put an arm around her shoulders.

"I know it's frightening. But we can't stop. Five million people already believe. They want this country to be not a feeding trough, but a home."

At the next table, Olha raised her head.

"I wrote an article about how our idea could help children like us find families. Marta will make a video. I want everyone to understand: this isn't about politics. It's about hope."

Her voice was quiet, but it held a kind of strength no parliamentary seat can give. Only loss, and the belief that a person can still be found.

Makar smiled for the first time that day.

Nearby, Anna Kravets and Serhii Broshch bent over documents.

"Five million party members in the database," Anna said. "Justice is ready to display the selection process in real time."

"Legally, we're protected," Serhii added. "But there was an attempted breach yesterday. IP address from Kyiv."

"Those aren't just hackers," Nazar cut in. "They're people afraid of losing their ration. I've strengthened the system, but they'll try again."

This was no longer politics.

It was a war over access to the future.

Late that night, when everyone else had gone, Makar was preparing to leave when he found a white envelope outside his office door. No return address.

Inside was a sheet of paper with printed letters:

WE KNOW WHERE THEY GO TO SCHOOL. DROP THIS, ZHUK. OTHERWISE YOU WON'T BE THE ONE WHO REGRETS IT. THEY WILL.

His hand trembled.

Then it crushed the page into a tight ball.

Slowly, silently, he dropped it into the bin.

It was not despair.

It was a choice not to live in fear.

Marks arrived tomorrow.

Everything was entering the phase from which there would be no retreat.

Outside the window, the first snow fell quietly.

A sign, perhaps: winter brings not only cold.

It brings cleansing.

CHAPTER 6: THE HIVE

Snow crunched under the feet of tens of thousands of people filling Kyiv's Olympic Stadium. The cold air was charged with tension and the electricity of hope. Handmade signs rose above the crowd:

"THE NAMELESS ARE OUR HOPE!"

"JUSTICE FOR EVERYONE!"

This was no longer a rally. It was a ritual of awakening.

Y boiled with activity. The hashtag #NamelessForJustice exploded into millions of posts. This was no longer merely a political party. It was a social shift, something halfway between a prayer and a revolution.

Behind the stage, under the hard white light of the spotlights, Makar Zhuk watched the enormous screen showing Marta's videos: teachers, miners, doctors, soldiers—ordinary Ukrainians—explaining why they had become Nameless.

Every face on the screen was a mirror for someone in the audience.

Not heroes. People.

Not messiahs. Neighbours.

Then came an explosion of applause.

Aaron Marks walked onto the stage.

No suit. No theatrical gestures. Jeans, black turtleneck, a smile with a trace of challenge.

"I came to Ukraine because you are the first people who dared," he began.

His voice was even and powerful, cutting across the entire stadium.

"The Nameless are not a party. They are a hive. In a hive there are worker bees—the non-resident citizens. They build lives, pay taxes, raise children. There are warrior bees—the resident citizens who serve the state. And there is a queen—Ukraine. Not a leader. Not me. Not Zhuk. The country. The idea. A hive is a system in which everyone works not only for themselves, but for everyone. That is justice. That is the future."

The metaphor was not poetic. It was architectural.

People did not simply hear it.

They saw themselves inside it.

The stadium erupted. People cried, embraced one another, passed phones from hand to hand to record the moment.

This was not a concert.

It was an initiation.

Backstage, Adriana held Natalia's hand.

"Mum, twelve million views already," she whispered.

Natalia squeezed her palm. Her eyes shone. For the first time, not with fear.

With pride.

Her silence was louder than any slogan.

Makar looked out over the people as if over a sea. Their faces seemed lit not by spotlights, but by belief.

Yet his phone vibrated in his pocket.

One of Pechko's latest reports was spreading again:

"Marks and Zhuk: the technological scam of the century?"

Makar knew that five attempts on his life had already been uncovered over the previous weeks.

The stage was light.

The phone was shadow.

And between them stood a choice.

The six-hour drive back to Lviv with Marks—no cameras, no delegations—became the conversation that put everything in its proper place.

"You wonder why I'm helping this much?" Marks began, looking out at the snow-covered fields. "It's business. But a new kind of business. Honest business can exist only in an honest environment. Politicians all over the world are brakes. I spend billions so lobbyists can stop them getting in the way of building things. Every country is infected. Even the States. But Ukraine is a chance. If we can do it here, it sends a signal to the planet."

Makar answered quietly.

"That's not why I'm doing it. I don't want anyone dictating their will to anyone else. Money is dirt to me. Every evil begins when someone wants more than they need."

Marks smiled.

"It's dirt when it's hoarded. Not when it's used to build. I live for the work. I don't have villas or yachts. I don't hide. I just want to get things done while I still can. And you know something, Makar? You're real. You're no saint. But you're sincere. Sincerity may be the rarest resource on Earth."

"I'll drink to that," Aaron said.

He touched his glass of excellent thirty-year-old whisky to Makar's glass of mineral water.

The toast was not about alcohol.

It was recognition.

When Marks entered the Lviv office, everyone stood. He motioned for them to sit.

"Makar, you didn't create a party. You created a movement. An organism. I want to be part of it."

Natalia, who had remained quiet until then, asked:

"And what if they stop us? They've already tried five times."

Marks looked at her for a long moment, seriously.

"No one can stop a hive when the bees work together. And all of you are under my unofficial protection. More protection than the President of the United States has."

It was not bravado.

It was a promise given not for the cameras, but person to person.

He turned to Makar.

"The AI is ready. By New Year, you'll have twenty-five million. Then the election. And everything changes."

News that the party had five million members detonated across Y. But more important was what had begun happening inside the country.

They were not in power yet, but they already had influence.

Officials became afraid to take bribes. Courts began issuing fairer decisions. Morality seemed to wake up.

The Nameless were becoming a catalyst for cleansing.

It was not law.

It was example.

And it worked.

That evening, Makar sat in an office chair with Lesia on his lap, watching the snow fall beyond the window.

"Dad, we're making a miracle, aren't we?"

Makar smiled and ran his fingers through her hair.

"Yes, Lesiunia. And this is only the beginning."

His voice was not really meant for the child.

It was meant for the future already sitting in his arms.

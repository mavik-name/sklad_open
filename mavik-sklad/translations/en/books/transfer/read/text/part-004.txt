The Warsaw–Lviv run was supposed to be almost routine by then.

I had exchanged messages with the client in advance. His profile displayed the status:

“I hear voices in my head. And they speak Hebrew.”

I decided to joke.

“I don’t hear voices, so how am I supposed to recognise you? Maybe I should hold up a sign with your name?”

The reply confused me slightly.

“Oh, then you’re lucky. The person uses sign language. And the sign idea is fine!”

I reread the message. Something did not add up.

“I was talking about the voices from your profile, by the way.”

Pause.

Then a whole avalanche of smiley faces.

Phew. For a moment I had thought I had managed to offend someone before even meeting them.

“Her name is Zhenia! It won’t be me :)”

I was supposed to collect Zhenia on the return journey. First, though, I had to take two Japanese passengers to Warsaw after collecting them in Rivne.

I left at two in the morning so I could pick them up at six and make absolutely certain they caught their Warsaw–Tokyo flight.

We loaded the suitcases. Set off.

For most of the drive to the border, we communicated through Google Translate. The Japanese turned out to be wonderful people. They had come to Ukraine to establish contacts, with the idea of helping rebuild our roads after the war.

One of them typed something into his phone. The translator produced:

“Will there be much road work in Ukraine?”

“If you really came here to rebuild roads,” I said, “you’ll have work for a very long time. Ukraine knows how to welcome investors generously.”

The translator thought about it and delivered my sentence. Both men laughed. So humour can cross borders too. Digitally, at least.

I was in a hurry, but this time I stayed within the legal speed. We reached the border with roughly an hour and a half to spare.

I even managed to persuade our border guards to let the car through without the general queue — I explained that I was taking two Japanese passengers to catch a flight. We cleared Ukrainian control fairly quickly.

After my first failed attempt to leave the country, life had taught me a lesson: I now carried prepared and notarised copies of my documents. I felt almost invulnerable.

Even the Polish border guard listened to my tale of suffering involving the flight, the Japanese passengers and our limited time, and allowed us to use the lane for EU citizens.

Yes, there are separate lanes at the border. One is used by people lucky enough to possess a passport of the right colour. The others stand where we stand — mostly holding blue Ukrainian passports.

Up to that point, everything had gone beautifully.

Polish border-control booth. Passports. Airline tickets proving that we really were in a hurry. Then customs.

And that was where the familiar story began.

They took our documents. Without any explanation I could understand, the car was sent for a more thorough inspection and over the inspection pit.

Our hour and a half of spare time began melting under the brutal July sun.

A van ahead of us was being checked. We waited. Minute after minute.

At last it was the Mercedes’s turn.

And for the first time on that entire journey, I was neither afraid nor even angry.

I was ashamed.

Not for myself.

It felt as though, because of Ukrainian number plates and a Ukrainian driver, two Japanese men were now suffering when all I had been supposed to do was deliver them safely and comfortably to the airport.

They searched them thoroughly. Turned out their pockets. Put them through a scanner. Then took them to a waiting room.

I stood beside the car and no longer understood what was happening.

“What are they, spies?” I finally snapped. “Why are you putting them through all this?”

Stern faces and silence in response.

They searched the car. Looked into every compartment, every corner. Then ordered me to open the Japanese passengers’ suitcases.

“They’re not my belongings. I’m not opening someone else’s luggage without them present.”

The suitcases were taken to the scanner. Then my things were scanned.

Finally — done.

I collected the Japanese men from the waiting room. Loaded the suitcases. We got in.

“Dziękuję.”

Because we are polite.

We drove beyond the perimeter. Poland.

I checked the time. Almost all our reserve had been consumed, but we could still make the flight.

In the rear-view mirror, I noticed a van. At first it simply followed us. Then it moved closer. Closer still. Almost sat on my bumper.

The limit on that stretch was sixty. I stubbornly stayed at the legal speed. Not because I had suddenly become a saint. After everything at the border, the last thing I needed was a Polish speeding ticket.

The van stayed with me.

Then a siren came on behind us. Hidden lights flashed on the van.

Well, that’s it, I thought. Now I’ll find out what law I’ve managed to break in Poland today.

But the van simply swung out sharply, overtook me, switched off the siren and disappeared ahead at speed, showing no concern whatsoever for that same “60” sign.

I watched it go for a few seconds.

Then I explained through the translator:

“Apparently the special vehicle really wanted me to drive faster. It got tired of waiting.”

They laughed. One typed a reply.

“You control yourself like a samurai.”

There we are.

Yesterday I was Statham. Today I was already a samurai.

Career progression.

After that, we talked for a long time through the translator. About borders. History. About how countries with difficult pasts learn to exist alongside one another.

They mentioned Japan and China and said there were more than enough historical reasons for mutual resentment between them. But they had not expected the kind of treatment at the border they had just experienced.

At one point, one of them formulated a question that the translator rendered almost perfectly:

“Which is worse: an enemy like Russia or a friend like Poland?”

I did not answer.

Not because I had no answer. Some questions are better left open than closed by the first emotion. You take them with you instead.

Perhaps one day a book will grow out of a question like that.

The rest of the journey passed without incident. We reached Warsaw airport with an hour to spare.

The Japanese thanked me, took their suitcases and disappeared through the terminal doors.

And then I suddenly remembered that my working day was not over.

Somewhere in Warsaw, Zhenia was waiting for me. The same Zhenia who used sign language.

And somewhere in my phone, the voices speaking Hebrew were still alive.

I drove to a car wash. The Mercedes had to be prepared for the next client.

ZHENIA

Zhenia turned out to be a girl without an obvious age.

More precisely, I never worked out how old she was. Probably over twenty but under twenty-five. Pretty. A ring in her nose.

And, apparently, a little bit psychic.

Because the moment we sat in the car, she looked at me and said:

“You don’t look much like a transfer driver. More like an artist or a writer.”

Well then.

Seen through at first glance.

“Writer,” I admitted.

“I knew it.”

“Definitely psychic, then.”

And that was it. We were off.

We talked about books, texts, creativity, and I got so involved that at one Warsaw interchange I took the wrong turn.

Naturally, I gave no sign of it.

A professional transfer driver never gets lost. Occasionally he simply chooses an alternative route.

I made a loop, returned to the correct direction, and we kept talking.

Zhenia was the daughter of emigrants who had lived in England for years. She had won a competition and was now travelling to work with the Ukrainian Red Cross. Her boyfriend was waiting for her in Lviv. He served in Azov and happened to be on leave.

Zhenia was trained as an artist, but she had an obvious gift for poetry. Especially free verse.

“Why free verse?” I asked.

She shrugged.

“It makes it easier for me not to lie.”

“Good answer.”

“You can steal it.”

“Writers don’t steal. We creatively absorb.”

She laughed and read me her poems.

I read her mine.

Somewhere in the middle of the road, we both noticed something strange: despite an age difference large enough that she could easily have been my daughter, we saw many things in very similar ways.

Not the same way.

Similar.

Which is much more interesting.

We talked about people. Pain. Creativity. Why write if a large part of what you write may never find a reader. Why some people hide what they have lived through inside themselves, while others absolutely have to turn it into a text, a painting or music.

The road passed unnoticed.

And this time even the border decided not to interfere in someone else’s story.

Zhenia was lucky: the queue into Ukraine was short, the crossing itself passed almost without incident, and about four hours later we were already on the Ukrainian side.

In Lviv, a young man was waiting for her with an enormous armful of flowers.

I took out her suitcase.

“Well then, psychic. We’ve arrived.”

“Thank you, writer.”

Zhenia walked toward him.

I stayed beside the Mercedes and thought that another transfer was complete.

For her — Warsaw to Lviv.

For me — one more person I would most likely never have met if, a month earlier, I had not decided to play Jason Statham.

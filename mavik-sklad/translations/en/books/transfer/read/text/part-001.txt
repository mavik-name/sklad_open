TRANSFER
VIKTOR MAKARCHUK · MaVik

Actors, if they want to play a role convincingly, sometimes practise total immersion in the skin of a character. I decided to spend a month inside the skin of a driver who transports passengers on private transfers.

From point A to point B. Safely and comfortably.

For the passengers, it is a journey. For me, a source of inspiration. Their conversations were supposed to give me material for future stories and ideas for new books.

It all began with an advert on one of the job sites:

“Looking for Category B drivers able to travel abroad. High pay.”

A minute of thought.

My imagination immediately supplied the rest: summer, beautiful scenery, a premium-class car and passengers who smelled of expensive perfume and left hundred-dollar tips.

Dream job.

I called the number and arranged an interview.

A week and a half passed. No call.

I decided it was probably the military recruitment office using the advert to check potential customers. Once they found out I had ten children and a deferment for the duration of mobilisation, they had presumably lost all interest in me.

But at the end of the second week after that first conversation, the phone finally rang.

“Are you still looking for work?”

“No,” I said. “I’m waiting for an interview with you.”

“Can you leave for Odesa today?”

“Whoa, hold it,” I said. “You could at least look at my driving licence. What if I’ve been on a drinking binge for a week?”

“I have a feel for these things,” came the reply. “I can hear from your voice that you’re right for us.”

“Okay. But I’d at least like to see the car. Get some instructions. Find out the details of the booking.”

“I’ll call you back. If necessary, I’ll come over with the car.”

That was how my working life as a transfer driver began.

I felt like Jason Statham.

Admittedly, half an hour later they called again to say the Odesa run had been given to another driver, while I was expected in Chernivtsi at three the following afternoon. At ten in the morning, a Mercedes E-Class would be waiting outside my house.

I had been awake since seven, changing trousers and shirts, trying to choose the combination that, in my opinion, most closely matched the image of a professional chauffeur.

Although Jason Statham and I are radically different.

He is a bald, unshaven brunette.

I am a grey-haired, bearded Gandalf.

At exactly ten, a silver Mercedes with a panoramic roof stopped outside the house. A stylish young man with a beard got out and joined me on the terrace for coffee and instructions.

He was a former serviceman. After being wounded, he had been discharged from the army and now worked in the transfer business. He had started as a driver, but for some time had been a manager — a “hunter” for new drivers.

“Difficult?” I asked, nodding toward the Mercedes.

“Driving it? No. The main thing is that the passenger is happy, the car stays in one piece, and you don’t get lost.”

“That’s a relief. I think I can find the passenger. I can see the car. So all I have to do is not lose myself.”

Drivers were in short supply. Some were fighting. Others were not allowed to leave the country. Meanwhile, the transfer business was booming: there were no civilian flights, and half the country seemed to live in two countries at once.

I received a short briefing, the car keys, the booking in an app and a pinned location where I was to collect the client.

There was more than enough time for the drive, but I like arriving early.

Punctuality is the politeness of kings.

Key in the ignition.

Off we go.

FIRST RUN

Premium class is nice.

Technically, it is the same means of transport as my crossovers or my van: four wheels, a steering wheel, pedals. But there is a certain vibe to it that really can make you feel like Statham.

Somewhere around the halfway point, I noticed with horror that I was going to be fifteen minutes late.

I know Waze does not lie, and I am always amazed by how accurately its algorithms calculate travel time. I tried to win back the lost minutes, pushing the car to one hundred and sixty wherever there was no risk of running into a speed camera or police radar.

At the same time, I remembered that the car had a tracker, and the company treated prolonged driving above one hundred and forty as a violation.

So I arrived exactly those same fifteen minutes late.

I called the passenger, expecting irritation, while frantically assembling an excuse in my head.

“Mr Artur, your car is waiting.”

Five minutes later, Mr Artur appeared. He looked under thirty. The moment he got into the car, I understood that today would be a particularly bad day to attract the police’s attention.

The cabin filled with the dense smell of a distillery. My eyes nearly watered.

“I hope you don’t pass out,” Artur said, his words only slightly tangled. “Maybe open a window?”

“The air conditioning will cope,” I laughed, relieved.

Lucky escape.

My first run — Chernivtsi to Ivano-Frankivsk — passed without incident. As he got out, Artur graciously handed me a fifty-dollar tip, and I finally began to believe the version of this job I had invented in my head.

Premium car. Travel. Expensive perfume — even if this time it came with strong notes of alcohol. And fifty dollars on top of the pay.

Dream job.

The road and Waze had completely drained my phone, so I stopped at a petrol station to buy a charging cable.

That was where the next call came.

“We have a run from Poltava to Chisinau.”

I checked the route and realised there was no physical way I could make it. Poltava was eleven hours away and the passenger needed collecting in nine.

I declined.

As I learned later, panic had made me read the booking the wrong way round. I was supposed to drive to Chisinau first, collect the passenger there, and then take them to Poltava.

But at the time, I was perfectly satisfied.

The first day was over.

The first run was behind me.

I had earned my first tip and had not lost anybody.

For a start, more than enough.

SECOND RUN

When I got home, I saw a new booking for eight in the morning.

Lviv to Kvasy, in Zakarpattia.

At 7:45 I was already waiting for the passenger. At exactly eight, a woman of about sixty came out of the building with a small suitcase.

Suitcase in one hand, lady by the elbow with the other.

Lady into the car. Suitcase into the boot.

Off we went.

For almost half the road to Ivano-Frankivsk, we travelled in complete silence. Then, with horror, I realised I was starting to fall asleep.

I could still see the road in front of me, but at the same time I had begun to see dreams.

More than thirty years of driving experience, and this had never happened to me before.

I turned cold air directly onto my face. Quietly rubbed my eyes, cheeks, forehead.

No effect.

I started pulling faces, trying to wake up the muscles in my face and drive the drowsiness away somehow.

Then I noticed, to my horror, that the passenger had seen my exercises in the rear-view mirror and was now watching me with considerable surprise.

“Are you all right?” she asked.

Damn, I thought. Well, that gave me away nicely.

“Yes, everything’s fine.”

“It’s just that we’re going very slowly,” she said. “That refrigerated lorry in front is getting on my nerves just by existing.”

“Sorry, but I’m staying within the speed allowed by the road signs. Waze constantly monitors the limit and warns me where I need to slow down. Almost this entire road runs through villages, so I’m doing no more than seventy — that keeps me within the usual twenty-kilometre tolerance.”

I could hear that the explanation was not especially convincing, so I continued playing the idiot.

“I’m bored too, that’s why I’m making faces. I’m not only a driver. I’m also a writer. I write books. I’ve just imagined a new character and I’m working out his facial expressions.”

In reality, I was simply trying to justify myself somehow.

But then it turned out the lady loved reading.

She began enthusiastically asking about my website, the platforms where she could find my work, listened to my songs on YouTube and praised the lyrics.

The drowsiness vanished as if someone had switched it off.

The rest of the trip passed in conversation and arguments. By the time I dropped her off, we were almost friends.

It was only a pity that this friendship received no material confirmation.

Strictly according to the price list.

On the other hand, I had gained a new reader. Later she wrote quite a few approving comments and even made me trim my beard.

Not all of it, admittedly.

Two thirds.

THIRD RUN

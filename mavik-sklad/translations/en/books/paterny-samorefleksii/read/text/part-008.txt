SEVENTH CIRCLE


43. RECONCILIATION

Reconciliation is not a white flag.
Not surrender to the thing that broke you.
Not agreeing to call normal what still makes silence limp inside your soul.
Reconciliation does not say:
"Everything was right."
No.
A great deal was wrong.
A great deal should never have happened at all.
Some words should never have been spoken.
Some doors should never have been slammed.
Some people should not have been able to leave so easily a room where, long afterwards, someone was still gathering themselves from the floor.
Reconciliation is not an excuse for the past.
It is accepting that the past will no longer stand beside your bed every day holding a whip.
Yes, it happened.
Yes, it hurt.
Yes, I did not understand everything.
Yes, I was not always the noble victim in somebody else's bad story either.
Sometimes I poured in poison myself.
Sometimes I held resentment so tightly as if it were the last form of dignity still available to me.
Sometimes I called my exhaustion wisdom.
Sometimes I called my harshness honesty.
Reconciliation begins where a person stops demanding perfect logic from the life they have already lived.
Life is a bad accountant.
Its figures often do not balance.
You gave a great deal—and received silence.
You waited for justice—and got weather.
You prayed for a sign—and heard a neighbour drop a spoon on the kitchen tiles.
That was all.
No symphony.
No heaven unfolded before you.
Just another day that has to be lived.
Reconciliation is when you finally allow yourself not to defeat your own biography.
Not to rewrite it.
Not to prove to everyone that suffering was an investment with excellent returns.
Simply to say:
"This was my life.
Not always beautiful.
Not always fair.
Not always something I could put on a shelf and show to guests.
But I am still here."
And perhaps that is the first honest peace.
Not with everyone.
Not with everything.
But with the fact that you survived and no longer have to prove, every day, your right to breathe.

44. JOY

Joy walks carefully after pain.
It does not burst into the house with music, balloons, and idiotic optimism on its face.
It knows something burned here not long ago.
That there are still smoke marks on the walls.
That in some rooms it is still better not to laugh too loudly because memory may flinch.
Adult joy does not look like carelessness.
It does not forget the dead.
It does not erase loss.
It does not say:
"Come on, enough. Move on."
It is not rude.
It sits down beside you.
Keeps quiet.
Sometimes it simply puts hot bread on the table.
Or takes you outside, where the sun, with no respect whatsoever for your drama, keeps shining on wet asphalt.
And suddenly you notice:
it is still beautiful.
Insolently beautiful.
Almost indecent after everything that happened.
Joy can come through something small.
The smell of coffee.
A cat that refuses to recognise your existential crises and demands food with the expression of a creature convinced God created morning for this purpose alone.
Children laughing in the courtyard.
An old song you accidentally did not switch off.
A message from someone who wrote nothing grand, only:
"How are you?"
And somehow that was enough for something in your chest to turn its head quietly toward the light.
We are often afraid of joy after loss.
As though it betrays the pain.
As though laughing today would take away yesterday's wound's right to have been real.
But pain does not need you to serve it for life.
Memory does not demand that you wear internal black until the end of your days.
And love for those who are gone, or those who left, is not measured by the number of years you lived without joy.
Joy is not betrayal of darkness.
It is darkness's defeat.
A small one.
Without fanfare.
In the kitchen.
In old slippers.
When a person who had every reason to become bitter suddenly smiled.
Not because everything is good.
But because not everything was surrendered to death.

45. GRATITUDE

Gratitude is late sight.
When we are young, we often look at what we were not given.
And we see it sharply.
Painfully.
Resentfully.
As an exact list that can be read aloud at night before the inner court:
here I was not supported;
here I was not chosen;
here I was not heard;
here love arrived empty-handed;
here God, if He was there, did a remarkably poor imitation of silence.
And all of that may be true.
But with age another kind of sight sometimes opens.
Not instead of the first.
Beside it.
Suddenly you see not only what was missing.
You also see what held you while you were busy with what you lacked.
The person who did not know how to save you but brought tea.
The friend who had no great wisdom, but had a car and came.
The mother who perhaps did not know how to talk about love, but knew how to set a plate on the table as though it were her way of keeping the world from eating you alive.
Gratitude is not denial of pain.
You do not have to thank the knife for teaching you to value your skin.
You do not have to turn harm into a spiritual souvenir.
You do not have to bow to darkness because light looks slightly more convincing afterwards.
That is not what gratitude is.
It is the ability to see goodness that did not shout.
That had no beautiful form.
That was not always enough, but was there.
Sometimes gratitude is simply saying in time:
"I see."
I see your exhaustion.
I see your quiet presence.
I see that you did not know another way, but there was something real in the way you knew.
I see that the world did not only take.
And if I can still speak, write, love, get angry, make mistakes, and once again search for a human direction, then perhaps someone, somewhere, did hold a door open for me once.
Even if I walked through without saying thank you.

46. STILLNESS

Stillness is not the absence of sound.
There may be sound.
Water dripping.
A car passing.
A neighbour drilling at the least biblical moment of the day.
A dog barking somewhere, apparently with complaints of its own about the structure of the universe.
And yet inside, suddenly, it becomes quiet.
Not empty.
Not dead.
Quiet.
As though someone finally switched off all the internal radio stations that had spent years broadcasting at once:
shame;
guilt;
resentment;
fear;
old voices;
other people's expectations;
your own prosecutor, who never takes a holiday.
Stillness after the inner court does not feel like victory.
It feels more like a room after a difficult conversation.
The chairs are out of place.
A glass of water sits unfinished on the table.
The truth still hangs in the air, and nobody wants to repeat it because it has already said enough.
And you sit there.
Not fully acquitted.
Not finally destroyed.
Simply alive after honesty.
A person needs stillness in order to hear more than pain.
Because pain is a very loud tenant.
It slams doors.
Turns on the light at three in the morning.
Rearranges the furniture in memory.
But when it finally grows tired, something else can be heard.
Something small and green still growing inside you.
Not an idea.
Not a plan.
Not a great mission.
Simply the ability not to be nothing but a wound.
Stillness is the place where a word ripens.
Because an unripe word often wants to go to war immediately.
To prove.
To sting.
To win.
To explain to the world how stupid it was not to understand you the first time.
A mature word sits in stillness for a long time.
Gains weight.
Loses unnecessary poison.
Stops being merely a reaction.
And one day comes out not as a stone.
But as bread.
Perhaps that is why the most important sentences should not be rushed.
Let them live in stillness for a while.
Let them stop wanting blood.
Let them learn how to be human.

47. HOPE

Hope is not optimism.
Optimism often wears a white shirt in the wrong weather and says:
"Everything will be fine," while the wreckage has not even been carried out of the room.
Hope is not that ill-mannered.
It sees the wreckage.
Sees the blood.
Sees that some doors will never open again because there is no longer a home behind them.
Hope does not lie.
It does not draw a sun on a smoke-blackened wall and say:
"Look what a beautiful morning."
It can say:
"Yes.
This is darkness.
Yes.
You are inside it.
Yes.
I cannot see a quick way out."
And then it sits down beside you and does not leave.
That is its insolence.
Hope is having no proof and still refusing to hand darkness the keys to every room.
Let it take the corridor.
Let it sit in the old storeroom.
Let it make noise in the basement.
But here, near the heart, there will still be one small locked cupboard holding something alive.
Not for everyone.
Not for a sermon.
Not for a beautiful story about how someone overcame everything and became a better version of themselves.
God, how exhausting that better version can be sometimes.
Sometimes it is enough simply not to become completely worse.
Hope is a very modest thing.
It can look like one sentence that survived.
One morning when you got up anyway.
One person you did not answer with evil for evil, though you could have.
One text you wrote not because the world was waiting, but because somewhere inside you someone was still showing signs of life.
Hope does not promise that the world will become good.
It has no such authority.
It only says:
"You can still choose not to add your darkness to everyone else's."
Sometimes that is too little.
And sometimes that is what holds the day together.

48. LIGHT-BEARER

The one who carries light does not necessarily look like light.
That needs to be said at once, so people do not search in the wrong place.
He may be tired.
Sharp.
Inconvenient.
With words that do not always pass the sanitary inspection of spiritual politeness.
He may have a bad temper, old wounds, poor jokes, unstable faith, and a habit of speaking to God as though both of them grew tired of beautiful formulations a long time ago.
A light-bearer is not a saint.
Perhaps a saint carries light more easily.
A light-bearer often drags it like a wet blanket through a swamp.
Swears inwardly.
Stops.
Sits down on a stone.
Says:
"That's it.
I've had enough."
And then, for some reason, gets up again.
Not because he is good.
Not because he is strong.
Not because some great secret of being a correct human has been revealed to him.
But because somewhere nearby it is even darker for someone else.
And that annoys him more than his own exhaustion.
A light-bearer does not save everyone.
He already knows that is a lie.
You cannot save everyone.
Some people do not want to leave the darkness.
Some have built a business in it.
Some sat in it so long that they began to experience light as a personal insult.
A light-bearer has no right to break down the door of another person's soul, even with the best intentions.
He simply leaves a lamp by the entrance.
Does not force his way in.
Does not lecture.
Does not plant a flag.
Sometimes that is enough.
Sometimes it is not.
That is the hardest thing to accept: light does not always win immediately.
Sometimes it simply refuses to let darkness have the last word today.
A light-bearer is not without shadow.
Quite the opposite.
He knows his shadow well.
Its voice.
Its logic.
Its talent for explaining why today cruelty is permitted because the world stopped deserving tenderness long ago.
Perhaps that is exactly why he carries light.
Because he knows darkness is not somewhere out there.
Not in other people.
Not in the villains of other people's stories.
It always keeps a spare key to his own door.
And every day he has to change the lock.
Not heroically.
Not beautifully.
Simply not becoming today the person he himself could not forgive.

49. HUMAN BEING

After all the trials, the human being remains.
Not fully acquitted.
Not finally condemned.
Without a halo.
Without demonic wings.
Without a convenient label you can hang on their chest and stop thinking.
A human being.
A little light.
A little mud.
A little childhood fear inside an adult voice.
A little pride that learned to walk upright and call itself principles.
A little love that did not always know where care ended and quiet occupation began.
A little faith that sometimes burned, sometimes smoked, and sometimes simply lay under ash refusing to let itself be thrown away completely.
A human being is not an answer.
It is a question walking on two legs.
And answering itself every day.
Not with words.
Words are too obedient.
They can be taught anything.
You can speak beautifully about goodness and be as cold as an unheated church.
You can curse evil and hand it out in small portions every day to the people closest to you.
You can pray for light and fail to notice that you stepped on someone's hand in the darkness.
A person answers not with declarations.
But with what they do when nobody is filming them for a future legend.
Whom they did not finish off.
Whom they heard.
Where they stopped.
Where they asked forgiveness without a "but."
Where they did not use another person's weakness.
Where, having every right to become stone, they still left room inside themselves for water.
I do not believe in the perfect human being.
And I probably do not want to.
A perfect human being has no need for mercy.
Does not know what shame smells like at three in the morning.
Does not understand why sometimes a glass of water weighs more than a correct sermon.
I feel closer to another kind.
The one who fell and did not turn falling into a new religion.
The one who made mistakes and did not turn error into permission to become a swine.
The one who knew darkness by name but did not let it sign every decision.
A human being is a creature that can become a knife.
And can choose not to.
Can betray.
And can return to truth even if truth receives them without an embrace.
Can lose the light.
And can carry it onwards no longer as a torch, but as a small ember in palms burned by their own life.
Maybe that is enough.
Not for acquittal.
Not for a monument.
Not for a beautiful ending.
But enough that God, if He is watching, does not turn away immediately.
Enough that another person, meeting you in the middle of their night, does not become darker afterwards.
Enough that at the end of the day you can sit on the edge of your own stillness and say:
"I did not become a saint.
I did not become wise enough to lecture everyone.
I did not become clean like a page nobody has lived on yet.
But today I did not add my darkness to the world.
And perhaps that is already the beginning of a human being."

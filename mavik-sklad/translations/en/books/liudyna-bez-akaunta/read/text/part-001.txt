CHAPTER 1. THE MAN WITHOUT AN ACCOUNT

He did have an account.
That needs to be said at once, before some attentive reader appears with a flashlight clenched between his teeth, a thumb hovering over the Comment button and a sacred duty to expose the author before the man has even made it to page two.
He had an account.
It simply was not the kind of account the modern world accepts as proof that a person exists.
No photograph. No real name. No wife in his arms against a sunset. No children with their faces blurred out or, on the contrary, demonstratively left unblurred. No coffee on a windowsill. No "living in the moment." No "after a hard but productive week." No public happiness arranged in a display case like a cake in a pastry shop, where the sponge inside went dry long ago but the strawberry on top is still hanging on.
He had an anonymous account under a creative pseudonym.
The phrase "creative pseudonym" irritated him almost physically. It had something of the provincial poet in a black turtleneck about it, reciting free verse on the loneliness of streetlamps at an evening commemorating himself and waiting for someone to finally say, "That is very deep."
But he had not found a better term.
He could not write under his own name. Not because he was hiding from responsibility. Responsibility is unpleasant but familiar. Like the dentist. Like the tax office. Like a family group chat where some relative has once again sent a glittering greeting card with an archangel who, for some reason, looks like a nightclub bouncer.
He was not hiding from responsibility.
He was hiding from the bazaar that had long ago learned to call itself public opinion.
Because the modern world does not read the text. It sniffs the author.
Who are you?
Where are you from?
How old are you?
Where do you work?
Who did you vote for?
Why were you silent then?
Why are you speaking now?
Is this autobiographical?
Has your wife read it?
Do your children know?
Did you really write this yourself?
Or did GPT bang it out for you while you sat around in your underwear pretending to be a suffering middle-aged man?
That last question was especially vile. Not because he did not use GPT. He did. Like everyone with internet access, laziness, curiosity and enough honesty not to pretend he lived in a monastery writing on parchment. But using a tool was one thing. Being told that your pain was not yours, your sentences were not yours, your voice was not a voice at all but some generated rasp from electronic bowels was another.
In the age of GPT, authorship had become dangerous.
Before, an author could be accused of having no talent, plagiarism, graphomania, moralising, arrogance, derivativeness, too many commas, too few commas, incorrect use of dashes and a general resemblance to someone who ought to spend more time outdoors.
Now everything had become simpler.
— It's AI.
Full stop.
Even if it later turned out that he had written the text himself. Even if he showed drafts, revisions, files saved in the middle of the night, tea stains on the keyboard and the nervous tic left after rewriting the same paragraph for the fifth time. The residue would remain.
It was like the old story about the daughter being a whore. Later, perhaps, it would turn out that the daughter was not a whore. That there was no daughter at all. That there was a son. Or that the man had no children and had spent all these years living honestly with a cactus. But the crowd had already nodded, drawn its conclusions and carried the news onward, adding a little salt for flavour.
The residue would remain.
Residue is the most durable form of human memory.
Facts evaporate. Corrections get tired. Documents disappear. But residue sits at the bottom of someone else's head like sludge in an old bucket and starts stinking precisely when needed.
That was why he had an account without a face.
The man without an account—that was how he sometimes thought of himself, although technically it was untrue. But in the modern world, technical truth had long ceased to matter. Only presentational truth counted. If you could not be found, inspected, judged, classified, suspected and used against yourself, then you almost did not exist.
The anonymous account was his compromise with reality.
Writing for the drawer was no answer. Drawers are good for socks, old warranty slips and cables whose purpose humanity has forgotten but is for some reason afraid to throw away. A drawer is no place for a scream. A scream grows mould there. Goes sticky. Then one day you open it and what comes out is not a text but a small swamp with literary ambitions.
He needed a reader.
Not fame.
Not fans.
Not a crowd chanting his pseudonym and asking him to sign an e-book because nobody had bought the paper one anyway.
He needed at least one witness.
One person who would read and say not "well written," not "relevant," not "you have an interesting style," but something simpler and more frightening:
— I understood.
Because being understood is a very dangerous form of nakedness. Much more dangerous than posting a photo from the seaside with your stomach sucked in, shoulders squared and your soul thoughtfully left outside the frame.
The world had long ago turned into a nudist beach.
Everyone was naked. Everyone was showing something. Everyone was displaying something. Bodies, traumas, relationships, breakfasts, children, renovations, neuroses, victories, defeats, rings, divorces, spiritual practices, bad days, good days, days no different from all the others but presented as "a small victory over myself."
And amid all this global nakedness, humanity remained lonely, like an old nudist on a beach where everyone has been undressed for so long that nobody notices him anymore, standing off to the side with all that remains of his former grandeur: the end of love and two bags of memories.
He liked that image.
It was crude, slightly obscene, almost disgusting and therefore honest.
Modernity had a problem with honesty in general. It loved sincerity, but only when properly packaged. Sincerity needed good lighting, clean sound, competent editing and preferably none of the person's actual smell. A few tears—fine. A little pain—wonderful. Trauma—perfect, if it could be broken into a series of stories. Inner collapse was acceptable too, but only if it ended with a promo code for a course called "How to Put Yourself Back Together in Seven Days."
He did not want to be part of that.
He did not want to show his wife because his wife was not a prop proving his normality. Not evidence that somebody could tolerate him. Not "my rock" under a photograph where the rock had probably asked him five minutes earlier to take out the rubbish and he had said, "In a minute," and that minute had already reached school age.
His wife was home.
Not metaphorically. Physically. Domestically. In the smell of clean sheets. In a voice from the kitchen. In the brief "Have you eaten?" In a look with which she could say in three seconds everything other people stretched across family therapy, two podcasts and a book with a cover the colour of quiet despair.
He loved her.
That was precisely why he did not want to fill her head every day with his own lamentations.
Every marriage has a load-bearing limit. Love can withstand a lot: illness, lack of money, stupid relatives, renovations, children's fevers, bad presents, silence over dinner, even the male conviction that a shelf can be hung "by eye."
But love is not obliged to listen every evening to a monologue about the world degenerating, people growing smaller, culture dying and algorithms gnawing the last bits of meaning from the bones.
Even the gentlest woman, after the third evening like that, may look at you in a way that makes you start searching for the "humanise text" button—only now for your own face.
Besides, he wanted to seem cooler than a hard-boiled egg in front of his wife.
It did not work consistently.
But he wanted it to.
It was one of those male stupidities that cannot be completely uprooted without risking collapse of the entire structure. A man can be intelligent, adult, responsible, ironic, even capable of making his own doctor's appointment, and still there will be a little boy sitting inside him who wants the woman to look at him and think:
"Wow. What a specimen I got."
Not "poor thing."
Not "his philosophical cold is back again."
Not "maybe I should buy him some magnesium?"
Specimen. That was the word.
He did not talk to the children about the swamps inside him at all.
That would have meant sinking below the baseboard, and the baseboard in their flat was already peeling away from the wall in places, reminding everyone that even renovations sometimes get tired of holding up appearances.
How was he supposed to sit down in front of a child and say:
— You know, Dad was thinking again today that humanity has finally turned meaning into packaging and love into content.
The child, of course, might reply:
— Dad, can I have money for pizza?
And would be right.
Children should not have to be their parents' psychotherapists. Children have the right to think of their father as a roof. Maybe an old one. Maybe one that leaks in places. Maybe one that grumbles in the rain. But a roof. Not a wall that is cracking itself and asking to be propped up by children's hands.
So he kept quiet.
And when keeping quiet became dangerous, he wrote.
Writing was not a profession for him. A profession is when someone pays you for it, or at least humiliates you systematically in the marketplace. He did not consider himself a writer. The word "writer" carried within it the suggestion of a person who had already agreed to become a public figure, go to launches, answer questions such as "what did you want to say with this work?" and pretend he did not want, at that very moment, to crawl under the table and live there with a spider.
Printing a book was out of the question.
Not because he had never dreamed of holding his own book in his hands. He had. Of course he had. A person can pretend to be an ascetic all he likes, but somewhere inside there is still a tiny narcissist with wet eyes whispering:
"It would be nice."
But between "it would be nice" and "a book presentation by author so-and-so" lay a chasm filled with falseness, self-promotion, photographers, posts, thank-yous, invitations, reposts, requests for support, mailing lists, covers, biographies and the phrase "a writer with a subtle feel for the nerve of our age."
The thought alone made him want to wash his hands.
He could not go through the performance of presenting himself as an author.
Now an author had to do more than write. An author had to become the content surrounding his own text. Tell people where inspiration came from. Share his workspace. Post pictures of drafts. Explain how the characters were born. Cry on camera, but with restraint. Be sincere, but not too sincere. Vulnerable, but photogenic. Profound, but brief. Original, but legible to the algorithm.
He did not want to be laundry detergent for people with childhood trauma.
He wanted to write a sentence.
Just a sentence.
One that would not sell a course, raise donations, explain his personal brand or turn him into the sort of author suitable for a morning TV programme.
A sentence after which at least one person would stop pretending not to understand.
His anonymous account had a strange name.
He had spent a long time choosing a pseudonym and hated every option. They all sounded either like the name of a coffee shop or the signature of someone who had once read Camus and never managed to return to normal life. He went through dozens of names. Some had too much darkness in them. Others had too much pretension. A few looked as though they had been invented by a teenager after his first cigarette and his second listen through a depressing playlist.
In the end he chose a short pseudonym that explained nothing.
Because the best pseudonym is a door without a sign. Those who know will come in. Those who do not can go to the influencers, where there is always light, music and a discount on the first month of emptiness.
He did not publish the first text.
He did not publish the second either.
He almost published the third, but at the last second imagined some unknown good citizen with a profile picture of a wolf, a flag, a sports car or his own torso writing:
"Too many wurds."
And that was it.
Not an argument. Not criticism. Not a thought. Just a digital slipper thrown from a dark stairwell.
They could pelt him with slippers.
And the worst part was that he was not sure he would endure it with dignity.
Because imagining yourself unbreakable is easy when you are sitting in a quiet kitchen. In your head, you can even address the court of history. Stand straight. Speak firmly. The Pharisees grind their teeth. The crowd stirs. And you, almost biblical, open their eyes.
Then the first comment arrives: "Author, you're such a bore."
And your whole spine—that underrated component of romance—suddenly behaves like pasta after boiling.
He was not Christ.
He needed to repeat that thought to himself regularly, preferably in front of the mirror in the morning instead of doing exercises. Because sometimes, when he wrote, a dangerous temptation appeared: to imagine himself as the man opening the Pharisees' eyes.
It was a pleasant, poisonous, very convenient temptation.
The Pharisees really were out there. New ones. Modern ones. Backlit. With ring lights. With the right vocabulary. With biographies polished to such a shine that no human being was reflected in them anymore. They sold sincerity by subscription tier, morality by reach, pain by membership, conscience in the format of an intensive course.
They taught other people to be themselves after first coordinating their own "selves" with a manager, a stylist and an advertising dashboard.
They talked about freedom while living by the algorithm.
They talked about love while counting conversion rates.
They talked about truth with the confidence of people for whom truth was an affiliate programme.
Yes, the Pharisees existed.
But he was not Christ.
Christ went to the cross for other people's sins. He still had not sorted out his own.
There were skeletons in his cupboard too.
Not big cinematic ones, accompanied by loud music and a dark basement. Ordinary human skeletons. Shameful. Domestic. Unimpressive. The kind that would never make a television series but sometimes woke you in the night and left you staring at the ceiling as though subtitles might appear there explaining why you had done what you did.
Once he had stayed silent when he should have spoken.
Once he had spoken when silence would have been better.
Once he had failed to defend someone.
Once he had agreed.
Once he had been afraid.
Once he had been petty.
Once he had wanted to look better than he was, and had almost succeeded—which was probably the worst of it.
He regretted every little bone.
Not theatrically. Not by standing in the rain and shouting at the sky. He did not like people who shouted at the sky anyway. Frankly, by this point in human history the sky ought to have filed a class-action lawsuit for emotional exhaustion.
He regretted them quietly.
But sincerely.
And with time he understood: repentance is not beating yourself over the head with old mistakes every day until your skull turns into a drum for your own guilt.
Repentance is when you stop being the person you were.
Or at least try to.
He could not repair the past. The past was, in general, the most insolent form of time: it had already happened, yet it kept coming back to check whether you had forgotten what an idiot you had been.
He could only do something now.
Speak.
Write.
Not so that people would pity him.
Not so that people would forgive him.
Not so that people would call him brave.
But because silence has a smell too. And over the years that smell becomes unbearable.
That evening he sat in the kitchen after midnight.
The house was asleep.
His fortress breathed quietly: the walls clicked and settled, the refrigerator muttered to itself, and every few minutes upstairs some mysterious metal object rolled across the floor—an object apparently passed down through that family for generations specifically for nocturnal rituals.
A cup of tea stood on the table. The tea had gone cold long ago and looked like a person invited to a conversation and then forgotten because nobody remembered why.
He opened the file.
Then he opened the account.
Then the file again.
Then the account again.
This was not work. It was emotional masturbation with elements of public self-trial.
He typed the first sentence:
"I don't want you to know who I am."
He looked at it.
Deleted it.
Too dramatic. That is how manifestos, terrorist letters and bad novels begin—the kind where the hero has a secret but the author forgot to invent one.
He wrote it differently:
"I have an account, but no face."
That was better.
Then he added:
"Because a face, in our time, is not a body part. It is a target."
He stopped.
There were three lines on the screen.
Not a great revelation. Not stone tablets. Not a bolt of lightning striking the digital Pharisees.
Just three lines.
But something in them was already breathing.
He opened the website of one of those AI detectors that had appeared so quickly and brazenly you would think humanity had not been waiting for a cure for cancer but for an official way to accuse one another of insufficient humanity.
He copied his three lines.
Pasted them.
Clicked "Check."
The detector thought for a few seconds. Somewhere in its silicon bowels, presumably, a committee on morality, style and paid services had convened.
Then the result appeared:
"Probability of AI-generated content: 72%."
He stared at the screen for a long time.
Then he laughed.
Quietly, so as not to wake the fortress.
It was not a happy laugh. More the kind of laugh made by a person who has finally found the door out of a burning building, only to see a sign on it saying: "Exit temporarily unavailable. Please use the mobile app."
Beside the result was a button:
"Make it human — $4.99."
He laughed again.
Humanity had finally reached the point where the only way to prove you were human was through a paid subscription.
It was not even funny.
Which was exactly why it was indecently funny.
He imagined Dostoevsky uploading Notes from Underground to a detector like this and receiving a warning: "Your text shows signs of artificial generation, excessive complexity, emotional instability and potentially low readability for a modern audience. Would you like to humanise it for $4.99?"
Dostoevsky would probably stare at the message, pawn something, pay the fee, gamble away the change and then write something even better.
Or maybe not.
Maybe he would just spit.
He closed the detector.
Then returned to the text.
The three lines stared back at him from the screen, defenceless and brazen at the same time. Like children who have already smashed the vase but are still hoping the whole thing can be blamed on the cat.
He added:
"I don't want applause. I want someone to finally stop pretending they don't understand."
This time he did not delete it.
His fingers hovered over the keyboard.
Then he added a title:
"The Man Without an Account."
It was a lie.
But close to the truth.
Sometimes that is exactly how the most honest things begin.
He clicked "Publish."
The post appeared on the page of his anonymous account.
Nobody reacted.
The first minute passed in silence.
So did the second.
By the third he thought this was not even a bad thing. Perhaps the world had shown mercy and decided not to notice him immediately, giving him time before death to reconsider his views on publicity.
At five minutes, the first view appeared.
One.
He looked at the number as though somebody had suddenly struck a match in a dark field.
One view was not yet a reader.
One view might be a bot.
Or an accidental thumb.
Or a person who had been searching for a syrnyky recipe and now regretted where the internet had taken them.
Still.
One.
In a world where millions of people shouted into screens every day, sold themselves, prayed to algorithms, blessed their own traumas for monetisation and carried the last scraps of privacy out onto the public beach, one unknown somebody had seen his three and a half paragraphs.
He sat in the kitchen and stared at the number.
One.
Not a victory.
Not the beginning of fame.
Not proof of meaning.
Just one.
But for a man who had spent a long time talking to himself in the basement of his own head, even one view could feel like a knock at the door.
He did not know who it was.
He did not know whether they had read to the end.
He did not know whether they had understood.
He did not know whether tomorrow they would pelt him with slippers, call him a bore, suspect him of working with AI, discover in the text a hidden insult against every social group at once and pass sentence with the kind of confidence people usually have only when they know nothing.
He knew one thing.
Tonight he had not put the text in a drawer.
And that did not yet feel like salvation.
But it did feel like a first breath.

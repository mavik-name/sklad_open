CHAPTER 10: THE CRISIS OF VICTORY

News of the establishment of the Hetmanate rolled across the country like thunder through a spring sky.

Not like a celebratory fanfare.

Like a strike that woke everything that had been sleeping.

It awakened not only excitement, but anxiety. Even inside the Nameless.

Those who objected were a minority. But they spoke loudly.

And not without reason.

“We fought against the usurpation of power, not to create a new dictator,” said a young law professor from Kyiv, the informal leader of the dissenters. “Even if that dictator is an honest one.”

His words were a cold shower.

Not hatred.

Principle.

Nazar, now head of the security service, spoke just as directly.

“Dad, the army backed you because it believes in justice. But power for life sends a dangerous signal. It could split us.”

His voice did not sound like a son’s.

It sounded like a soldier who had spotted a crack in the formation.

Makar summoned a general meeting of the newly elected MPs. The hall was full. The tension felt electrical. Every look was a question. Every pause, a verdict.

“I never sought power,” Makar began. “Do you think I wanted this title? I dreamed of going home. To the children. To my garden. This was not my choice. It is a burden you placed on me.”

His voice did not sound like a speech.

It sounded like confession.

Then Serhii Broshch rose. What he said was simple, but the hall went still.

“This was my initiative,” he said. “And I’ll explain why.

“First. We did not fight to reform the system. We fought to rebuild it completely. Presidential power is one of the key elements of the old architecture. It is a competition of promises, populism and fear. It produces corruption, not responsibility. We could not build a new state on the old foundation.

“Second. We are returning to our own roots. A Hetman was not selected from a party list. He was acclaimed at the viche. His name lived among the people. Makar did not ask for this. His name was already in their hearts.

“And third. The most important point. The system is not dead. It is hiding. Oligarchs. Agents. Nepotism. They are waiting for our first mistake. We need time. And a strong centre capable of making decisions. Long enough to carry out reforms that cannot simply be rolled back. The Hetmanate is not a privilege for him. It is a burden of responsibility.”

His words did not sound like a defence.

They sounded like a diagnosis.

And a prescription.

The debate continued all night.

No shouting.

No demagoguery.

Only a choice between fear and trust.

By morning, the majority agreed: the decision was necessary, but it could not remain unlimited.

To remove even the shadow of usurpation, Makar himself proposed the solution.

“Put it to a referendum. Five years, with the possibility of extension. The people must confirm it. Personally.”

It was more than a democratic gesture.

It was an act of conscience.

The week before the referendum became a test.

Natalia and her team presented education and social reforms:

• new schools where children would learn to think rather than memorise;
• a dignified pension;
• an affordable-housing programme for young people.

Dmytro and the agricultural committee presented a new farming model:

• cheap credit;
• support for small farmers;
• complete digitalisation of land transactions.

This was not campaign theatre.

It was a plan for survival.

And growth.

The international response was explosive. G7 ambassadors expressed “deep concern.”

Not because change was happening.

Because the change was real.

To the east, people rubbed their hands with anticipation, hoping for failure and a rapid revanche. Adriana and the newly created diplomatic wing worked around the clock, explaining that Ukraine was not dismantling democracy, but trying to save it under conditions no textbook had anticipated.

Referendum day brought record turnout.

Not because people were afraid.

Because they wanted to be part of the decision.

That evening: screens. Pulse. Waiting.

82% — YES.

The people had spoken.

Clearly.

Deeply.

Beyond dispute.

And now there was no longer any doubt:

This was not merely a revolution.

It was the birth of a new world.

CHAPTER 11: HARD WORK

A little more than a year had passed since twenty-five million Ukrainians had said yes to the Party of the Nameless.

19 April 2026—the day three hundred MPs entered the Verkhovna Rada.

Then impeachment.

Then the Hetmanate.

Then the cleansing.

The title Hetman of Ukraine was not ceremonial. It meant full responsibility—not only before the country, but before history.

Makar could veto any decision of parliament. His word was decisive in senior appointments. But with that power came personal responsibility before the people.

And before a world watching the experiment closely.

Makar was not a tsar.

But he no longer had the right to weakness.

The first year was no fairy tale.

It was digging through stone.

Lustration exposed tens of thousands of corrupt officials. But along with them disappeared many of the people who at least knew how the machinery of government worked.

The state apparatus became a skeleton without muscle.

The country needed new people.

Makar worked eighteen hours a day.

No weekends.

No permission to be tired.

His office was not a place to drink coffee. It was headquarters for rebuilding a state.

Anna—the brain of the system.

Natalia—the heart of education.

Taras Petrovych—the architect of the new economy and a new face on the team.

None of them was merely a minister.

Each carried the idea.

Justice worked almost flawlessly.

Almost.

One day, the system falsely accused a rural doctor from the Poltava region of financial fraud.

Opposition media pounced.

“AI DESTROYS PEOPLE!”

“REPRESSION BY THE NEW REGIME!”

Makar did not wait for a report.

He went to see her himself.

He entered her office carrying flowers, looked her in the eyes and said:

“Forgive me. This is my mistake. And I am responsible for it.”

The moment was broadcast live.

It changed more than a hundred press conferences could have done.

Anna’s team changed the system. Justice gained mandatory human verification for disputed cases.

Because technology is a tool.

Responsibility always belongs to a human being.

At the same time, Natalia launched pilot schools. Children were not forced to cram facts. They were taught to think. To ask questions. To search for answers.

Teachers were mentors, not guards.

Every classroom, a laboratory of the future.

Children, not objects of the system, but participants in it.

Ukraine began to recover.

Slowly.

Relentlessly.

AI tracked every hryvnia, and that changed everything.

Global investors lined up.

New roads.

Schools.

Hospitals.

Children’s camps filled with laughter again.

Homes for the elderly filled with warmth.

Aaron Marks built a gigafactory outside Kyiv producing electric vehicles for all of Europe. In Dnipro, he established a shipbuilding cluster for SpaceY.

Makar made him sign a collective agreement.

“European wages. Transparency. Protection. No tricks.”

“I told you you weren’t a fanatic,” Marks said with a grin during a video call. “You’re a trade-union boss.”

“I’m a father,” Makar replied. “And I want the children of the people who work here to live no worse than your engineers in California.”

“Ukraine is my jackpot,” Marks joked in Forbes when his fortune passed fifteen trillion dollars.

But Makar’s jackpot was not a number.

It was people.

The country was becoming the engine of the continent.

Reforms that had once seemed impossible became reality:

all mineral resources returned to the people;

a monthly social dividend for every citizen;

a four-day working week;

jury trials;

a lower retirement age;

the abolition of special pensions.

The birth rate rose to three times its level of the previous thirty years.

Emigrants returned.

With heart.

With experience.

With belief.

They were no longer fleeing war.

They were returning to life.

There was less fear in the streets.

More medicine in hospitals.

More light in schools.

Fewer empty words in parliament.

More hope in people’s hearts.

But Makar knew this was only the beginning.

Hard work was not a stage.

It was a condition.

A choice.

A vow.

PART IV. PEACE

CHAPTER 12: EUROPE’S NEW STAR

Early autumn 2027.

Makar stood on the terrace of the Hetman’s residence. Before him lay the Dnipro, calm as wise silence. The water moved slowly, steadily, reminding him that true strength was endurance.

Ukraine was changing.

Not through slogans, but actions.

Not promises, but roads, factories, schools.

The army had become one of the strongest in Europe. Not a parade army, but a real one—battle-tested, equipped with the newest technology and staffed by people who knew both loss and victory.

But to the east lay a shadow.

The enemy.

The one who had not forgotten.

And had not forgiven.

The leader of the neighbouring state—the same man who had signed the peace agreement in early 2026—now regretted it. His propaganda scattered poison like endless autumn leaves.

Its newest face was... Oleh Pechko.

Exiled.

Repainted.

The perfect mouthpiece.

Once one of “ours,” now one of theirs.

His face appeared on screens in border towns, on social media, in fabricated news reports. He said what people across the border wanted to hear, and what was designed to tear open wounds in Ukraine.

Forces gathered near the frontier.

Satellites recorded new bases, trains carrying equipment, exercises by so-called peacekeeping units.

It was obvious that the enemy was preparing for revanche.

Every day of Ukrainian development was poison to them.

Makar remembered the price of that development.

The peace signed in 2026 had been a compromise.

Not defeat.

But not victory either.

It had saved lives and left open wounds.

One fifth of the country remained occupied.

The more time passed, the harder it became to speak casually about “reintegration.”

The occupied territories were being transformed.

Not rebuilt.

Changed.

Settlers. Propaganda. The destruction of identity.

Cities where Ukrainian had once been heard now spoke another language. Schools that had taught the history of Kyivan Rus now taught a “shared past” beneath someone else’s flag.

Ukraine could not wait forever.

Military analysts had begun to hint at it openly.

“We’re ready. For the first time. They aren’t.”

AI confirmed the same conclusion. Algorithms that had once calculated budget expenditure now modelled war scenarios.

In every one, Ukraine held the advantage.

Makar could do it.

But he did not want to.

The war still lived inside him.

Its smell.

Its screams.

The silence after battle.

He did not see maps and arrows when he thought about another campaign.

He saw the faces of those who had never come home.

So he decided not to fight.

To talk.

Not because he was afraid.

Because he did not want revenge.

He wanted life.

He chose a new battlefield.

Negotiations.

Not negotiations where people exchanged polite phrases across polished tables.

A chess game in which a single move could cost thousands of lives.

Makar assembled his team.

Anna—the strategist who understood how their AI worked and how it could be used to predict an opponent’s behaviour.

Natalia—the diplomat who could speak in a way that made even an enemy question his own certainty.

Oleh—another Oleh, not Pechko—the military adviser who kept reminding them:

“Peace is good. But it has to be a peace worth having.”

Makar understood that the enemy would not be the only player at the negotiating table.

There would be people calling themselves mediators while playing their own game.

There would be those who wanted peace, but wanted reconstruction contracts even more.

And those who feared a strong Ukraine more than they feared war.

Before leaving for the first round, Makar stepped onto the terrace again.

The Dnipro was just as calm.

But now he saw not only silence in it.

He saw depth.

“We did not lose,” he said quietly to himself. “And we will not lose. But victory is not always a flag over a city. Sometimes it is a flag inside the heart.”

He knew that if these negotiations could be won, Ukraine would become more than Europe’s new star.

It would become one of its poles.

A place that pulled others towards it.

A place that could not be broken.

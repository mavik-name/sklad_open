CHAPTER 2: FIRST STEPS, FIRST ENEMIES

Makar sat at his desk, staring at the laptop screen. Dawn was already breaking outside, but the room remained half-dark, lit only by the dull glow of the monitor. The silence was thick as honey, but bitter. It did not calm him. It warned him.

Nazar stood nearby, watching the stability of the internet connection. His face had the concentration of a bomb technician about to disarm an explosive device—tense, but collected. His fingers moved quickly and carefully, as though one wrong keystroke might set the keyboard off.

"Dad, that's Aaron Marks," he said without taking his eyes off the equipment. "If he backs you... this could change everything. But you know the rule: free cheese only comes in a mousetrap. His technology and his money will also mean his influence."

His voice was an alarm signal muffled by discipline. He was not afraid. He was analysing.

The screen brightened and Aaron Marks appeared. Black T-shirt, coffee mug in hand, the light, familiar smile with that trace of irony that had made his face recognisable around the world. His gaze did more than look. It scanned, evaluated, weighed. Like a predator smiling before making an offer.

"Makar," he began immediately, "your idea isn't just an experiment. It's a revolution. I tried something similar in the States, but the political establishment there is too afraid of losing control. Ukraine, though—wounded but alive, coming out of a war—is perfect ground for a real breakthrough. My AI, Justice, developed by YAI, can turn your concept into reality. And I'm ready to fund it. Whatever it takes."

His words flew like drones—precise, technological, carrying the potential to strike. And a promise of rescue.

Makar felt his heart pounding inside him. But Nazar's warning was still in his ears. He had to ask the main question. His inner voice spoke like an old adviser: Don't trust the smile. Trust the motive.

"Mr Marks, I'm sincerely grateful. But tell me—what do you want in return?"

Marks smiled openly, without a trace of offence. It was the smile of a man accustomed to suspicion, and skilled at turning it to his advantage.

"Honesty," he said. "I need it as much as you do. Look, I'm a businessman. I build technology. But I'm sick of spending billions on lobbyists and politicians who slow progress down. I want a country where the rules of the game are honest and the same for everyone. Where government is a service, not a feeding trough. If it works here, it becomes a model for the world. Your success becomes my best advertisement. And my dividend. An honest one. In this case, my profit is your prosperity."

His voice had the smooth logic of a presentation, but there was still something in it that made a listener wonder what might be hiding between the lines.

While this conversation unfolded on the western edge of the Lviv region, another man, with no less power, was reading Makar's post in a luxurious office in central Kyiv overlooking the Dnipro.

Viktor Kholodnytskyi, a media magnate and owner of the Patriot television channel, stared at the screen with unconcealed irritation. His office resembled a throne room—leather, glass, silence. But his eyes held the fire of a man who disliked other people's torches.

"Oleh, who the hell is this clown?" he asked without looking away from the screen.

Oleh Pechko, the channel's chief anchor, elegant and permanently calm, shrugged. His face was a professional mask: neutral, polished, with eyes that knew how to kill with ratings.

"An old acquaintance. Used to be a PR man. Then disappeared. Adopted a pile of kids. People love that sort of thing."

"People love it," Kholodnytskyi mimicked. "People love fairy tales too. But Marks isn't a storyteller. He's a player. If he comes in with his money, we'll have to give ground. Get to work. Turn Zhuk into a clown. A puppet of an American billionaire. Hit him precisely. Hit where it hurts most. 'Selling Ukraine.'"

The words came like orders from headquarters—without emotion, but with consequences.

That same evening Patriot aired its first report. Pechko appeared on screen, grave and assured.

"A new social experiment financed by foreign capital. Will we allow an exhausted, war-torn nation to be used as a laboratory?"

The footage had been edited with skill: fear, doubt, patriotism. Everything required to stop a thought before it had a chance to take root.

Meanwhile, the Zhuk house was full of morning bustle. But it was not the ordinary domestic kind. This time the air was tense, almost electric, heavy with the expectation of a strike.

Adriana sat at the kitchen table scrolling through her phone.

"Dad, two million views! The hashtag #NoNameForJustice is already trending!"

Her voice trembled with excitement, but there was something in her eyes as well: the awareness that something had begun. This was no longer just a viral post. It was an idea starting to breathe.

Yuliia jumped in at once.

"And my video explaining that anyone can become an MP got a hundred thousand likes!"

Marta was already editing a new clip. Farmers, teachers and drivers appeared on screen, all saying the same thing:

"I'm ready to serve my country. Not rob it."

Their faces were real. That was exactly what made them dangerous to those accustomed to make-up.

Makar listened and felt as though pride might make him cry. But Natalia stood nearby at the stove. Her movements were as precise as a surgeon's, yet anxiety showed in every one of them. The knife struck the chopping board rhythmically—too loudly for morning. Her silence was a siren. Not loud, but unmistakable.

"Makar," she said without turning around. "Did you see what they broadcast yesterday? 'Zhuk sells Ukraine to Marks.' This is only the beginning."

Her voice was level, but something deeper trembled inside it—not fear, but foreboding. The instinct of a mother who senses a storm before the sky darkens.

He went to her and took the hand holding the knife. Her fingers were cold, not from the room, but from tension gathering like electricity before thunder.

"Natalochka, I know you're worried. But we're not people who curse the government over a drink and do nothing else. We gave ten orphans a chance. And we won: our children are ours. Lesia and Volodia don't even know another life anymore. I have no right to stay silent while I watch this country being destroyed not by the aggressor, but by its own rot. Damn pickled cucumbers!"

His voice trembled not with anger but with responsibility. This was not rhetoric. It was confession.

Oleksandra, who had been playing Hallelujah on her violin, stopped.

"Dad, what are 'pickled cucumbers'? You don't mean the ones we eat, do you?"

Makar smiled. There was light in the smile, the kind that appears when a child asks a question adults are afraid to formulate for themselves.

"It's when you put fresh cucumbers into old brine. They turn sour too. People are like that. Good people enter a bad system and become part of the rot. But we'll change that, Lesiunia."

The words were a vow spoken not before a crowd but before his own child, which made them harder to say and far more important.

Natalia remained silent. There was no agreement in that silence yet. Only worry. Then she exhaled. The breath was a decision—not emotional, but moral. The kind of decision someone makes before entering a battle whose outcome is not guaranteed.

"Yes, I'm worried. But I'm even more afraid that our children will grow up in a country where betrayal is normal and cynicism rules. So I'm with you. And as an educator, I know where a country begins. Not in parliament. In school. You break their barrel. I'll build a new school. A school where children aren't taught to obey, but to think."

Her words sounded like a manifesto, but not a political one. A human one. A declaration of faith in education as the foundation of a real state.

Makar looked at her. This was his Natalia. Not just his wife—a comrade. His support. His conscience. He saw in her more than love. He saw a future. If his idea was a spark, she was the fire that would keep it from going out.

CHAPTER 3: THE PROGRAMME AND THE FIRST TEAM

A week had passed since Makar's conversation with Aaron Marks when the expected guest arrived at the Zhuk home. An electric car stopped by the gate almost soundlessly, like a good intention. Its body gleamed in the morning sun, a symbol of a new era—clean, quiet, potentially powerful.

Anna Kravets stepped out. A thirty-year-old YAI engineer, born in Ukraine and raised in the winds of Canada. Dark hair tied in a knot, thin-framed glasses, assured posture—everything about her said that this was a person accustomed to solving difficult problems. Her steps were precise as an algorithm. Yet there was human warmth in her eyes that made it impossible to mistake her for a machine.

"Good afternoon, Mr Zhuk," she said with a smile. "Aaron Marks sent me. He believes your idea is revolutionary. And I'm here to launch Justice."

Makar led her into the spacious sitting room. Nazar, home from his studies, was checking the Wi-Fi. His distrust of "foreign technology" bordered on paranoia. His fingers moved over the keyboard as if across a minefield—carefully, but with experience.

Dmytro, as always, offered tea and fresh bread, still warm, baked by Maksym. The smell filled the room like a memory of something real, something that did not change even when the world did.

"If your AI chooses me for parliament, I'll demand a law on farmers' cooperatives!" Dmytro joked as he poured the tea.

Anna smiled, genuinely but briefly. Her eyes said she was already at work. Her mind seemed to run like a server—continuously, without distraction, toward a goal.

"Justice isn't just a program," she explained, opening a laptop with a minimalist interface. "It's a system that analyses large sets of open government data. But for full functionality it also needs access to closed databases: criminal records, finances, medical information, certificates. For your project, we're prepared to make everything open and transparent. Everything under public oversight."

"Transparent?" Nazar repeated, narrowing his eyes. His body tensed as if ready to move. "And who watches Justice itself? Marks?"

Anna removed her glasses. Her voice slowed and became more personal. This was the moment when technology yielded to the human being, when code stepped back before ethics.

"I understand your scepticism. I'm Ukrainian. I know how things work here. But the code is open. Anyone can audit it. Any citizen who wants to. The point is to build something that cannot be bought or subordinated. Not perfection. Honest rules."

She put her glasses back on.

"So. What exactly do you want this system to do?"

Makar handed her a cup of tea and sat opposite her. His eyes were calm, but something smouldered inside them. Not merely the fire of an idea. The heat of experience that had survived dozens of betrayals.

"I've laid it all out in the document. But in short—we're creating a neo-democracy."

He leaned forward. Not aggressively. Like a soldier preparing for an assault.

"Power in this country isn't the scales of Themis. It's a torn sack. Corruption exists everywhere, but ours has its own style. In the West, when people divide the public pie, the person cutting it neatly picks up the crumbs that fall and pockets them. Here? The person doing the cutting stuffs himself until he's full and throws the crumbs to everyone else. We want to break that. But even if we replace the barrel, the cucumbers can still go sour. So we need a refrigerator. Five years. Entirely within the Constitution. No violations."

At that moment Lesia entered the room carrying a plush unicorn. She had heard the last sentence.

"Dad, what's a refrigerator for gherkins?"

Makar smiled and gently ran his fingers through her hair. The touch was a blessing—not religious, simply human.

"It's when we make sure good people stay good," he explained. "So the system doesn't spoil them."

Then he turned back to Anna.

"The Constitution gives everyone the right to be elected. In practice, though, you have to buy a ticket into politics: find money, find a sponsor, force your way onto a party list. We say: enough. We'll give people a real chance. The conditions are simple: age twenty-five to sixty, sound mental health, no criminal record, legal income, willingness to serve. If you join No Name, you automatically become an applicant. Then your algorithm randomly selects candidates from that pool according to clear criteria. Four hundred and fifty people. Entirely legal. After the election, AI monitors every MP: voting record, declarations, income, public statements."

Anna listened closely while making notes. A spark appeared in her eyes. This was more than interest. Recognition. She was beginning to see not a dreamer, but an architect.

"And what does that look like in practice?"

Makar answered without hesitation. His voice sounded like a plan rather than a slogan.

"Our target is critical mass. Even a thousand supporters, if every one of them convinces five more. From there Justice forms the list. We run as a single bloc. We win a majority. On the first day after the oath, we pass a legislative package that fundamentally changes the country. Then five years of full service: state provision, salary equal to ten national averages. No 'bonuses.' The job is service. Bills come from experts and AI. Everyone who actively participates in building the state receives the status of resident-citizen. Those who don't want to participate are non-resident citizens. Everyone remains equal before the law. But people have a choice. This isn't discrimination. It's responsibility. Like a fire: anyone can warm themselves, but someone has to keep it burning."

Anna leaned back.

"Wow. It's simple. And at the same time... the most difficult thing I've ever heard. Convincing millions is a challenge. Now I understand why Aaron caught fire over it."

Her voice held more than excitement. She was moved. She no longer saw a start-up. She saw a chance.

Another guest arrived later that day. Serhii Broshch. Grey-haired, wearing a severe suit, Makar's old friend from university and one of the country's finest lawyers. He walked with the weight of a man carrying not only knowledge but years of combat—not at the front, but in courtrooms.

"Makar," he said, shaking his hand. "I saw the post. And Pechko's report. I'm with you. Enough of these sour bastards. We need a legal fortress. They'll grab at every comma."

His voice was a hammer—not for smashing things, but for building.

That evening Makar gathered the whole family around the table. The children were accustomed to conversations "about important things," but this was different. The room held the silence before a storm they themselves had summoned.

"Listen," he said, looking at each of them. "I started this because I believe we can change the country. But it won't be easy. There will be insults. Threats. Are you ready to walk this road with me?"

Adriana nodded first.

"I'm already writing posts. We'll make it go viral."

Her eyes burned not with adolescent excitement, but with conscious resolve. She was not merely offering support. She was already acting.

Marta, without looking up from her laptop, added:

"I'll make a video. About us. About people. About hope."

Her fingers flew over the keyboard like strings. She was not creating content. She was creating a record of the future.

Lesia hugged the unicorn to her chest.

"Dad... I don't want us to have pickled cucumbers."

The words were simple, but they contained the essence of the whole movement. A child who did not want rot was already a revolution.

Only Natalia remained silent. Her silence was deep as an old well. Her eyes held worry and determination at once. She did not nod. She did not speak. But her gaze was a signature written without ink and sealed in blood.

The next day, another strike appeared on Y. Pechko aired a new segment:

"Zhuk and Marks: the corporatisation of Ukraine through AI?"

"Who is really behind No Name?"

"A new form of dictatorship?"

The comments exploded. At the same time, www.noname.party could not withstand the traffic. People were registering in huge numbers.

It was a war between two worlds: the old one, which knew how to shout, and the new one, which knew how to listen.

Makar watched the screen. Support mixed with filth in the same stream. The system would not surrender easily. But the fight had begun. The team was taking shape.

This was no longer a project.

It was a front.

And everyone had a weapon: words, code, video, bread, music.

Makar—the ideologue.

Natalia—the conscience and the school.

Anna—the brain.

Serhii—the legal shield.

Nazar—security.

Adriana—the youth wing.

The children—the living heart.

And Justice was already downloading.

Inside that algorithm lived more than data.

Hope had begun to live there too.

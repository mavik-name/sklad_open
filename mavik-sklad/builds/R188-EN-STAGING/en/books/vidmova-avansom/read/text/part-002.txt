CHAPTER TWO

CAREFULLY READ

By noon, the email thread contained forty-seven messages.

By two, ninety-three.

By evening, it had more authors than some literary festivals and roughly the same amount of organisation.

The first clearly defined character to emerge was Orest Bahatotomnyi.

That was how he signed himself.

Orest announced that he had received the rejection too, but was not upset, because a true author does not depend on publishers’ opinions. What followed were links to seven of his novels, three short-story collections, a channel with video reviews, a support page, and an online shop where you could buy a mug printed with a quote by Orest Bahatotomnyi.

Matvii did not look closely enough to see which quote was on the mug.

He suspected it was a long one.

Then Yulian Pravopysnyi appeared.

He sympathised with all the rejected authors and explained that publishers often turned down manuscripts because of stylistic errors, weak pacing, structural problems, and lack of professional proofreading.

At the end, Yulian offered his own editing, proofreading, beta-reading, alpha-reading, and — judging by the prices — spiritual rebirth of the manuscript.

After him, a website designer offered everyone personal author pages.

A marketer offered promotion.

A literary agent offered a consultation on how to find a literary agent.

A psychologist offered group therapy for post-rejection trauma.

Within an hour, the email thread contained everything necessary to write a novel, edit it, heal from it, build a website, and order a mug.

The only thing missing was a publisher.

The publisher remained silent.

Matvii kept writing down the best lines.

Plot No. 4. A ship is wrecked. The passengers launch sailing courses, sell life jackets to one another, and argue over whether the captain read the manual.

At five in the afternoon, a message arrived from Borys Levytskyi.

He wrote that he had not submitted any manuscript. Several months earlier he had merely proposed reissuing books that had already been published and had added one new novel. The publisher, apparently, had decided not to burden itself with nuance and had rejected the whole package at once.

Matvii smiled.

Universality was a mark of professionalism.

A good rejection should fit a manuscript, a reissue, and a person who just happened to be walking past.

Then a message arrived from Yaryna.

She advertised no books, offered no services, and added no hearts.

Good evening.

I sent my manuscript eight months ago. It is the first novel I have ever finished. I am twenty-one. I knew they might reject it, but all this time I thought that at least someone was reading it.

Now I can see that everyone got the same reply.

I suppose I was just foolish to think I could write.

For the first time, the thread went quiet.

Myroslava did not say thank you.

Orest sent no links.

Yulian did not offer to fix the pacing.

Matvii read the message again.

Suddenly it was not funny anymore.

He knew this mechanism. A person handed someone else the right to pass judgement not only on the manuscript but on the person who had written it. One editor’s answer became literature’s answer. A publisher’s silence became the world’s silence. A standard template became a final diagnosis of talent.

Matvii clicked Reply All.

Yaryna, a publisher’s rejection means only that the publisher said no.

Everything else you wrote into it yourself.

Do not give someone else’s inbox the right to decide whether you are allowed to write. It is far too small for that job.

He reread the reply and added:

And if the novel is already finished, it exists. That is more than can be said for most great ideas.

Matvii clicked Send.

A minute later, Yaryna replied:

Thank you.

For the first time all day, the words were not funny.

Then Myroslava wrote:

Yaryna, let us read it.

Then someone else.

And someone else.

Within twenty minutes, three dozen people had asked to see Yaryna’s manuscript.

Orest Bahatotomnyi offered to post the first chapter on his page. He did not even include a link to the mug.

Yulian Pravopysnyi promised to read ten pages for free.

Matvii thought humanity was not quite as hopeless as it had seemed that morning.

It simply needed the right excuse to gather by accident.

Soon someone suggested creating a separate group, because the emails were getting lost, multiplying, and returning to their senders as forty-page chains of quoted text.

The group was created by Lana Kyivna.

Her name sounded like a pen name, a title, and a railway station all at once.

Within half an hour, one hundred and twenty-seven people had joined.

The first problem was the name.

“The Rejected” was judged too tragic.

“The Unpublished” was inaccurate, because some of the authors had already published books.

“Unbreakable Writers” sounded like an NGO raising money for a motivational workshop.

“Literary Resistance” frightened the children’s author.

Matvii wrote:

“Carefully Read.”

The name was accepted almost unanimously.

Even those who had voted against it later said they had been misunderstood.

After the name came the question of purpose.

Orest proposed mutual promotion.

Yulian proposed improving manuscript quality.

Myroslava proposed support.

Lana Kyivna proposed networking and a space for conversation.

Someone proposed a revolution in the publishing industry.

Someone asked whether the revolution would be free.

Matvii wrote:

We could simply read one another.

Ten minutes later, his message had been posted on the group page as:

MANIFESTO OF THE “CAREFULLY READ” MOVEMENT

We will not wait until someone gives us permission to exist.

We will read one another.

“I did not write that,” Matvii said.

Lana replied:

“But it follows from what you wrote.”

“What follows from what I wrote is only that people can read.”

“You’re being very modest now.”

“I’m not being modest. I’m being literal.”

His objection was added to the manifesto as an author’s comment.

The next morning, Matvii woke up as the founder of a movement.

He learned this from a post by Orest Bahatotomnyi:

Yesterday writer Matvii Sahaidak launched a new cultural initiative aimed at returning literature to readers.

Under the post were links to all seven of Orest’s novels.

Matvii messaged him privately:

Orest, I did not launch anything.

Orest replied:

That’s exactly why it looks sincere.

Matvii tried to explain the situation in the group:

Colleagues, there is no movement. There was a publisher’s mistake, then an email thread, and now we are simply talking.

Lana Kyivna turned his words into a poster:

“THERE IS NO MOVEMENT”

Below, in smaller type:

First open meeting of the “Carefully Read” movement.

Yaryna suggested reading passages from manuscripts aloud in the city park.

Matvii replied:

Read. You do not need a publisher’s permission for that.

An hour later, the phrase appeared on a poster:

“READ. YOU DO NOT NEED PERMISSION.”

Matvii stared at the screen and felt himself losing control of events he had never organised.

On Saturday, Yaryna went to a small public square with the first chapter printed out.

Beside her she placed a cardboard box marked:

“If the story was worth a coffee — send a ding.”

Myroslava, Lana, Orest, Yulian, and around thirty other people came.

Yaryna read uncertainly. Her voice shook through the opening sentences. Then someone stopped. Then someone else. Ten minutes later, several dozen listeners had gathered around her.

When she finished, no one applauded immediately.

For a moment there was silence.

Then a little girl walked over to the box and dropped in a coin.

It struck the cardboard bottom with a ding.

A woman came next.

Then an elderly man.

Matvii watched the park video on his phone.

It already had several thousand views.

In the comments, people were asking when the next reading would take place.

Orest announced that it had been the first event in a nationwide literary flash mob.

Matvii wrote:

What flash mob?

Someone replied:

The one you started.

That was when he understood: it was already too late to explain anything.

Everything he denied became a slogan.

Everything he refused became a concept.

And every attempt to stand aside only proved that a true leader did not need a title.

That was how “The Living Manuscript” was born.

Not from a programme.

Not from a budget.

Not from an order.

Not even from a manuscript.

It was born from a rejection sent to the wrong person.

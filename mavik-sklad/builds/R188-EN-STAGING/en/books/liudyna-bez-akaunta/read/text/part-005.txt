CHAPTER 5. WHERE IS MY HAND?

Mahmoud would not let go.
It was starting to get annoying.
A person could forget where he had put his keys, why he had walked into a room, the name of the classmate who sat at the first desk, what exactly he had promised his wife he would buy “on the way,” and why there was once again a jar of unidentified contents in the fridge that nobody dared throw away because it might be “for the soup.”
But Mahmoud — Mahmoud stayed.
He sat in memory like a gooseberry thorn in your finger from when you were a boy and decided to quickly pick some gooseberries in somebody else’s garden, because your own were obviously sourer and somebody else’s fruit always grows with a faint flavour of criminality.
A thorn like that seems trivial.
Not a wound. Not a tragedy. Not a fracture. Not a reason to convene the family council and call a specialist in gooseberry trauma.
But it is there.
Touch it with a finger — reminder.
Put your hand in your pocket — reminder.
Think about something lofty — and there it is, the bastard, down in the lower layer of reality.
Mahmoud was exactly that kind of thorn.
After the atelier incident he should have remained a resort anecdote from his youth.
There was a man. He used to visit. He wore a nutria-fur hat in the heat. He wanted not to sew but to rip.
He said a line that outlived the Soviet Union, most of the sanatoriums of the period and perhaps even the moral architecture of the age itself.
That ought to have been the end of it.
But memory, like a platform for writers, does not always work according to the user’s rules.
That night Mahmoud came again.
Not to the atelier.
Not with a catalogue.
Not with gold teeth illuminating the women’s-clothing corridor better than fluorescent tubes.
This time he came on a bus.
The dream began in motion.
No introduction. No explanation. No “previously on” captions. Just an old resort “barn,” a bus that connected three little towns into one long body: sanatoriums, market, private houses, mineral-water pump rooms, park, railway station, private houses again, sanatoriums again, people again with bundles, bottles, string bags, shopping bags, jars, towels and that expression with which a Soviet person knew how to travel anywhere — to work, to treatment, to visit someone, to a funeral, to a queue and to the seaside.
Yes, there were no marshrutka minibuses yet.
There were buses.
Big, loud, uncomfortable, steeped in petrol, dust, sweat, wet clothes and the endurance of the people. The locals called them “barns” — and it was not an insult so much as a technical specification.
They really did resemble barns on wheels: long, creaking, packed with people, bundles, voices, strangers’ elbows and the feeling that if the doors closed now, a separate collective farm would finally form inside.
The bus was crowded.
Not merely “a lot of people.”
It was that particular Soviet-bus kind of crowd where everyone still somehow fits, but no longer entirely as separate individuals. People stood so close that privacy stopped being a right and became a fantasy of bourgeois individualism. Other people’s elbows, bags, shoulders, smells, newspapers, bundles, wet umbrellas, somebody’s chicken in a box, somebody’s bag of jars, somebody’s voice over your ear — all of it merged into one organism that breathed, argued, swayed on corners and from time to time passed fares from hand to hand with a degree of faith in humanity that modern platforms could not provide even after two-factor verification.
Mahmoud was standing beside him.
It was Mahmoud’s second or third visit.
Memory had not retained visit numbers, only a general certainty: by then Mahmoud had already settled into “the resort.” He no longer behaved like a man who had just arrived from the desert and had two weeks to fit in everything — ozokerite treatments, mineral water, the disco, the brothel, the market and a demonstration of his financial superiority over the local small fry.
He had become almost one of them.
Not so much one of them that he stopped smelling of cologne at a distance sufficient for cultural exchange, but enough that he no longer shoved under everyone’s nose the wad of banknotes bulging through his trouser leg like a small private economy.
He had even started using public transport.
That meant a serious level of integration.
Because a tourist on his first visit to a resort takes a taxi. A tourist on his second visit already haggles with the taxi driver. But a tourist who gets onto the local “barn” has effectively admitted that life is more complicated than showing off, and that a bus is also a way of knowing the world.
He and Mahmoud were pushed almost all the way to the back row.
They stood sideways, holding on to whatever they could: a rail, a seat back, their own dignity, somebody else’s suitcase if the bus braked sharply. The dream did not remember what they were talking about. Probably nothing important. In dreams, as in life, what remains is often not the content of the conversation but a smell, a light, an intonation and one line that then outlives everyone involved.
Beside them, right by the aisle, sat a woman of about seventy.
From the countryside.
That was not a sneer, merely a fact: you could see it in the headscarf, the bundles, the hands, in the way she sat — not like a city passenger who had simply been lucky enough to get a seat, but like someone who had spent a lifetime fitting herself, a bag, a bundle, a jar, a loaf of bread, an old newspaper and the moral right to make a remark into one square metre of space.
A little bundle lay on her knees. Another sat by her feet. In one hand she held something wrapped in an old newspaper; with the other she steadied a bag so it would not shoot under the passengers’ feet at every pothole.
The bus bounced over a rut.
Mahmoud, holding the overhead rail, lightly brushed his hand across her cheek.
He did not notice.
She did.
— Please, sir, — she said, almost politely, — take your hand away from my puss.
Mahmoud looked at her.
Then at the young man. Then back at the woman. Shrugged. And they carried on talking.
The bus lurched again.
His hand lightly touched her face again.
— Please, sir, — she repeated, more irritated now, — I’m asking you, take your hand away from my puss.
Mahmoud looked at her again.
Longer this time.
He was obviously analysing the words. But the words refused to assemble themselves into a reality suitable for understanding. Language sometimes sets traps for a person worse than any ideology. Especially when one word in one place means a face, and somewhere else means something very much not a face, all inside a packed “barn” with no room left for linguistic delicacy.
Mahmoud shrugged.
Somebody was always arguing on a resort bus. It was as much part of the route as the stops. At one stop people got on, at another they got off, at a third somebody inevitably said, “Move further in,” although by that point the only thing capable of moving further in was the soul.
One wheel dropped into a pothole.
Mahmoud’s hand slid past her cheek again.
And the old woman finally snapped.
She slapped his hand the way women of her generation knew how to slap: not to injure, but to make the world return at last to the rules she had already stated three times.
— For heaven’s sake! — she cried. — I’m asking you to get your hand away from my puss! What’s so hard to understand?
The bus froze.
Not physically, of course. It kept moving, roaring, jerking, breathing overheated engine and human sweat.
But inwardly — it froze.
Public space freezes like that when something happens loudly enough for everyone to become interested, but not yet dangerously enough for anyone to intervene.
Mahmoud turned beetroot-red.
Instantly.
It was as if all the blood in Central Asia had assembled in his face for an emergency meeting. The gold teeth flashed. His eyes widened. His male dignity, linguistic confusion and resort confidence collided at one point.
And he exploded.
— What you want, woman?! — he shouted with such Central Asian force that even the driver seemed to slow down a little, not with his foot but with his heart. — Where my hand and where your pussy?!
The dream stopped there.
It did not end naturally.
It stopped.
Like film in an old projector when the frame freezes and you can see everything at once: red-faced Mahmoud, the indignant woman, the young man beside him, passengers with their mouths open, the bus-barn rattling along the road between three little towns, and that line which ought to have been merely obscene but had somehow, with the years, turned into a philosophical instrument.
He woke up.
It was not dark anymore, but morning was only gathering strength. That grey pre-dawn light when the world seems to have got up but has not yet decided whether it is worth carrying on today. Something rustled outside the window. The house was silent. The kitchen waited.
He lay there thinking:
what is this about?
Why Mahmoud again?
Why not something more respectable? A dream about the sea, for example, in which he finally finds the answer to every question. Or a wise old man who gives him a manuscript bearing the title of his future novella. Or at least a publisher weeping over his text and saying, “We’ve been looking for you all our lives.”
No.
He dreamed of Mahmoud shouting on a bus:
“Where my hand and where your pussy?!”
Consciousness, if we are being honest, could show a little more taste.
The answer came almost at once.
So quickly that he did not even have time to pretend he was deeply analysing the symbolism.
Where is my hand — and where is her pussy.
There it was.
He smiled in the dark.
Quietly.
Because the formula was crude, obscene, almost unusable in respectable literary discussion — and precisely for that reason, exact.
He had become too fixated on AI.
Far too fixated.
Yes, AI was a problem.
Yes, the badges were a problem.
Yes, the filters, detectors, suspicion, “inner feelings,” the battle for the purity of literature in which a comma, a cover and an entirely generated novel all sat in the same sack — all of that mattered.
But it was not what was really troubling him.
AI was a symptom.
A fever.
And for the past few days he had been walking around the bed of a sick world solemnly repeating:
— You have a temperature.
The world, perhaps, lay sweating under a blanket, delirious, wheezing, afraid of the future, losing attention, trust, the ability to listen, the ability to stay beside someone, the ability not to turn every pain into content, every thought into a position, every person into a suspicion.
And he stood there with a thermometer saying:
— AI. There. See? Thirty-nine point seven.
But a patient with a fever does not need to be told twenty times that he has a fever.
He needs medicine.
Or at least water.
Or an open window.
Or somebody to sit beside him without giving a lecture on the history of thermometry.
He sat up in bed.
Rubbed his face.
Mahmoud, the bastard, was right again.
Not in content, of course. In terms of content, Mahmoud could rarely claim a place in a textbook on ethics.
But in direction — yes.
“Where my hand and where your pussy?”
Where was his hand?
Where was his real subject?
And where was the thing he had been dragged into?
He was not writing about AI.
His texts were not about machines.
They were about people.
About how people lose the ability to hear one another.
About how fear turns into morality.
About how privacy becomes suspicious.
About how family remains the last fortress in an age when every window has long since been carried out into the public square.
About how a man wants to be cooler than boiled eggs but sometimes sits in the kitchen afraid of a single comment.
About how a person longs to be heard but does not want to become a product.
About how pain looks for a form and the world offers it a format.
About how suspicion replaces reading.
About how debate replaces conversation.
About how everyone competes in the precision of the diagnosis instead of treating the illness.
AI was only one of the thermometers.
Perhaps the newest.
Perhaps the most convenient.
Perhaps the most frightening because it showed not the temperature of the machine, but the temperature of the human being.
But it was still a thermometer.
Not the disease.
The disease lay deeper.
Distrust.
Loneliness.
Fear of being replaced.
Fear of being exposed.
Fear of being unnecessary.
The habit of classifying before listening.
The habit of asking not “what are you trying to say?” but “where did you get the right to speak?”
The habit of passing sentence instead of treating.
He went to the kitchen.
The kitchen greeted him in the way only a kitchen can: no pathos, no badges, no comments, no inner feelings. Table. Cup. Bread bin. Chair. Rubbish bin. Refrigerator with magnets that had long ago stopped being souvenirs and become a geological layer of family life.
He put the kettle on.
Opened the laptop.
The platform waited.
The comments waited.
Perhaps LitFox had already written something new about real writers who write in blood on birch bark and do not use autocorrect. Perhaps the reader was replying to somebody else. Perhaps the administration was preparing another reassuring post about how everything would become better after the next switch.
He did not open the platform.
For the first time in a long time, he did not open it.
He opened the file.
The same one where he had written fragments after the discussion.
On the screen were the lines:
“AI is not what kills literature. Literature dies the moment a reader enters a text not with trust, but with a metal detector.”
“A soul does not pass verification.”
“It is enough to teach the reader to suspect every polished sentence.”
Strong lines.
Not bad at all, even.
Which was precisely why they were dangerous.
Because a strong line often behaves like a good knife: you want to carry it where everyone can see it and let it flash at every opportunity. And when an author starts admiring his own knives, the reader has every right to hide his hands.
He reread them and suddenly saw what he had not wanted to see before.
They were diagnoses.
Accurate, perhaps.
Witty.
Painful.
But diagnoses.
You have a fever.
You have distrust.
You have digital segregation.
You have suspicion instead of reading.
You have fear of AI.
You have a soul as a watermark.
You have a society that no longer knows how to trust.
Fine.
And then what?
The reader reads it, nods, says, “Yes, the world really has gone bad,” leaves a like if he remembers, and goes back into his bad world where he has to pay the utilities, answer messages, watch the news, buy bread, hold himself together and somehow not go mad from the fact that every day delivers a new reason to lose faith in humanity.
He did not want to be one more man with a thermometer.
Telling the world “you’re sick” is easy.
There is even a certain pleasure in it.
A diagnosis gives the author the posture of a doctor. He stands over humanity in a white coat, looks severe, writes something in a notebook and inwardly feels almost useful.
But treatment does not begin with a posture.
Treatment begins with the question:
what do we do now?
He typed:
“I don’t want to write texts anymore that do nothing but diagnose.”
Stopped.
Added:
“A diagnosis without a prescription is an intellectual form of gloating.”
That was strong.
And unpleasant.
Because it applied to him.
He sat in the kitchen staring at the screen and felt something in him resist.
He liked making diagnoses.
No point pretending otherwise.
It was one of the few pleasures left to a person who had long since stopped believing in quick victories. A diagnosis creates the illusion of control. Name the disease — and you feel you have already done something. Notice the absurdity — and you feel a little above it. Formulate a sharp line — and it feels as though you have stabbed the monster in the side.
But the monster keeps living afterwards.
Sometimes even better than before.
Because now it has a quote for a T-shirt.
He smiled.
The world really did have a gift for digesting any criticism and excreting it back out as merchandise.
“Against the system” — 799 hryvnias.
“I’m not like everyone else” — three colours, sizes S–XXL.
“A soul does not pass verification” — that would look great on a tote bag.
He shook his head.
No.
Wrong direction.
Wrong direction again.
Where was his hand — and where was her pussy?
He opened a new page.
Typed a heading:
“Prescriptions”
Looked at the word.
It was dangerous.
Because prescriptions meant responsibility.
A diagnosis can be thrown into the world like a stone into a pond. Ripples appeared — good. No ripples — the world was stupid and did not understand.
A prescription has to be offered carefully.
The medicine may not work.
It may have side effects.
It may be ridiculous.
It may turn out to be banal.
It may make the author look not like a prophet but like an uncle who has arrived with herbal tea to treat civilisational sepsis.
But without a prescription — no.
He wrote the first point:
“Expose less. Bear witness more.”
It was not quite medicine.
But it was a direction.
Exposure places the author above the reader.
Witness sits beside him.
Exposure says: “You are sick.”
Witness says: “I was there too.”
Exposure loves a lectern.
Witness loves a kitchen.
He wrote the second point:
“Don’t begin with ‘humanity.’ Begin with ‘me too.’”
Good.
That he already knew.
Repentance does not begin with “all of you.”
Repentance begins with “me too.”
Third point:
“Don’t argue when someone is dragging you into somebody else’s pussy.”
He laughed so hard he nearly woke the house.
The line was appalling.
Completely unsuitable for a motivational poster.
And therefore priceless.
He softened it slightly:
“Not every argument is your subject.”
In brackets beside it he added:
“Where is my hand?”
That could stay.
Fourth point:
“If you see a symptom — look for the disease.”
Yes.
AI — symptom.
Badges — symptom.
Reciprocal likes — symptom.
Detectors — symptom.
The comment “I don’t feel a soul” — symptom.
The disease was not in them.
The disease was that people had stopped trusting an encounter.
Everyone arrived protected.
The reader — from deception.
The author — from humiliation.
The platform — from complaints.
The critic — from having to read.
AI — from responsibility, although AI, unlike people, at least did not pretend it had a soul.
Fifth point:
“Don’t ask for immediate love. Ask for attention.”
That mattered.
He realised that he often wanted the impossible from a reader.
Not simply to read, but immediately to feel, understand, accept, recognise him, pat him on the head and say, “Yes, you did not live in vain.”
A reader is not obliged to serve as psychological support for an author.
A reader can give attention.
And that is already a great deal.
Sixth point:
“Don’t treat everyone. Speak to one person.”
That was perhaps the hardest.
Because when you write, you want to address humanity.
Humanity, however, rarely reads. It is very busy performing humanity.
But one person — can.
One person can read.
One person can stop.
One person can suddenly think: “This is about me.”
Not “about everyone.”
About me.
Perhaps literature only works that way.
Not as an air-raid siren for the planet.
But as a match in a dark field.
He remembered the first view.
One.
Back then it had seemed almost ridiculous.
Now — no.
One is not little.
One is the unit from which a human being begins at all.
He wrote the seventh point:
“Don’t put the text above life.”
Because life will always come along and ask you to take out the rubbish.
And life will be right.
He leaned back in his chair.
There was a list in front of him.
No revelation.
No tablets of stone.
Nothing that could be photographed nicely beside a cup of coffee and captioned: “Seven Rules for a Writer in the Age of AI.”
Though it could be.
And that was frightening.
Modernity could take any honest thought and, in ten minutes, turn it into a checklist, marathon, sales warm-up, course, story, quote, T-shirt, tote bag, podcast and a post saying “save this so you don’t lose it.”
He did not want to make a checklist.
He wanted to find something to stand on.
For himself.
Perhaps for that one person who might someday read to the end.
He opened his text “Not Here for a Political Briefing.”
Now the title seemed right, but insufficient.
Because not here for a political briefing was only a refusal.
What he needed was a direction.
Not there.
But where?
He thought about Mahmoud on the bus.
About his indignation, ridiculous and crude.
About how one linguistic mistake had turned a packed “barn” into a theatre of the absurd.
About how easily people begin a quarrel when they do not understand where one person’s hand is, where another person’s face is, where one person’s boundary is, where another person’s subject begins.
Maybe the whole modern world was that bus.
Cramped.
Overheated.
Full of bundles, phones, fears, platforms, little flags, switches, offences and suspicions.
Everyone brushes against someone.
Everyone thinks they were brushed against deliberately.
Everyone shouts a word that means something completely different in another language.
Everyone turns red, gets offended, defends themselves.
And very few people stop to ask:
are we actually talking about the same thing?
He wrote:
“The world doesn’t need another diagnostician. It needs translators between kinds of pain.”
That.
He froze.
Yes.
Perhaps there it was.
Not a prophet.
Not a judge.
Not the therapist of humanity.
Not an inverse digital Pharisee.
A translator between kinds of pain.
Between a reader afraid of being deceived and an author afraid of not being heard.
Between a person who wants a living text and a person who no longer knows how to prove that he is alive.
Between a wife asking her husband to take out the rubbish and a husband who is, at that very moment, privately saving civilisation.
Between a young man on a resort bus and Mahmoud, who cannot understand why his hand has suddenly ended up in somebody else’s linguistic minefield.
Between a sick world and people tired of being told they are sick.
A translator between kinds of pain.
It was not heroic.
Not grand.
Not especially marketable.
But it was honest.
He added:
“Don’t tell a person: you’re sick. Sit beside them and ask: where does it hurt?”
Then another line:
“And if you know of any medicine at all — offer it. Not as an order. As a glass of water.”
The kettle clicked.
He flinched.
Life, as always, had arrived just in time to stop a thought from swelling into a religion.
He made tea.
Returned to the laptop.
Opened the platform.
The comments under the discussion had already spread in different directions. Somebody was arguing about authorial honesty. Somebody about whether a platform had the right to filter. Somebody about covers. Somebody about how “real talent always breaks through.” That line was especially cruel because it is usually spoken by people standing beside the door with the key in their pocket.
LitFox was replying to somebody else.
The reader was silent.
He opened the field for a new comment.
Started writing.
Then deleted it.
Started again.
Deleted it again.
Finally he wrote:
“I think we’re all looking slightly in the wrong direction. AI is an important subject, but it is more of a symptom. The real problem isn’t that a tool appeared that can imitate text. The real problem is that we were already bad at hearing one another before it arrived. AI merely lit up the fever.”
He stopped.
Added:
“I think literature should do more than expose the disease. It should also look for a way to keep a person from finally locking themselves inside fear. If a reader is afraid of being deceived, that needs to be heard. If an author is afraid of being judged not alive, that needs to be heard too. Perhaps we should begin not with badges but with a more honest conversation about responsibility: who stands behind the text, and whether they are prepared to answer for what it says.”
Pause.
He wanted to add something sharp.
About the thermometer.
About the pussy.
About Mahmoud.
He restrained himself.
Not every line that begs to enter a text has the right to go out in public without a muzzle.
He added:
“Because when a patient has a fever, it isn’t enough to tell him, ‘You have a fever.’ You have to at least try to give him some water.”
He sent it.
And this time he did not regret it.
Not because the comment was perfect.
No.
It was calm.
Without a knife.
Without the desire to win.
Without the shine of a line you want to hang on the wall.
Perhaps that was exactly why it was closer to what was needed.
He closed the platform.
Did not check whether anyone had replied.
Did not wait for a reaction.
That too was an achievement.
Small, domestic, invisible, without a medal.
He returned to the file.
Renamed it.
It had been:
“The Soul as a Watermark”
Now it was:
“Where Is My Hand?”
Then he thought and added underneath:
“Notes for a Novella”
The word “novella” suddenly clicked into place.
Not a novel.
Not a long story.
Not a drawn-out crusade against digital civilisation.
A novella.
One precise movement.
One thorn.
One man without an account.
One text that does not want merely to diagnose the world.
He sat in the kitchen thinking that perhaps this was how one ought to write.
Do not try to encompass everything.
Do not explain the age down to the last comma.
Do not prove to every LitFox that the world is more complicated than his caps lock.
Do not force the reader to give up her fear.
Do not defeat the platform.
Do not justify AI.
Do not curse AI.
Do not hide from AI.
Simply say:
here is a human being.
Here is his fear.
Here is his ridiculous pride.
Here is his private fortress.
Here is his night-time text.
Here is his Mahmoud, who for some reason comes to him in dreams and explains what no discussion managed to explain.
Here is his hand.
And here is the place he has been arguing about all this time, which is somewhere else entirely.
He laughed.
Quietly.
Because the house was still asleep.

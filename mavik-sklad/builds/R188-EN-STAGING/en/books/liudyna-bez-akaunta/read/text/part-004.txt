CHAPTER 4. THE SOUL AS A WATERMARK

After replacing the text, he did not open the platform for almost twenty-four hours.
That was an achievement.
Not a major one, obviously. Nobody gives a medal to a grown man for going twenty-four hours without checking whether somebody has liked him. Though perhaps they should. In the age of digital addiction, such a medal ought to carry at least as much weight as a decoration for restraint under fire. On the front: a man with a trembling finger hovering over the notifications icon. On the reverse: "Still alive."
He lasted until evening.
Then he opened it anyway.
Not because he expected a miracle.
He had already learned not to expect miracles. In the modern world, a miracle would probably begin by asking you to accept cookies, complete a CAPTCHA, verify your phone number and subscribe to the newsletter.
He opened the platform with the badges.
The same one where freedom began after verification, honesty was punished by a filter and an AI-generated cover sentenced a work to languish in a digital leper colony.
The platform greeted him with a new post from the administration.
The title was reassuring in that almost bureaucratic way:
"Update to the Rules on Works Using AI"
He opened it.
The administration announced that the labelling system had been simplified. Previously, an author had to state separately whether a cover had been created with AI, whether AI had been used in the text and, if so, to what extent. Now everything had been reduced to one question:
"Artificial intelligence: yes or no?"
No levels. No clarification. No distinction between a cover, editing, translation, a suggested idea or an entirely generated text.
Anything at all meant "yes."
If AI had so much as coughed somewhere near the work—"yes."
If the author had asked it to fix a comma—"yes."
If it had translated the blurb—"yes."
If it had generated a cover because the author could not afford an artist and, left alone in a graphics editor, was capable of producing only a rectangle with symptoms of depression—that was "yes" too.
As though somebody who called a taxi and somebody who stole a car had to tick the same box:
"Interacted with transport."
There would no longer be any visual labels on the work cards.
This was presented as a relief.
Nobody would stick a label on your story, they said. Your work would look like any other. No badge. No public shaming. Everyone equal. Everyone beautiful. Everyone in literature.
But the reader received a switch:
"Show works involving AI."
On by default.
Turn it off and works marked "yes" disappeared from the catalogue, search and the home page.
Completely.
They were not dimmed.
They were not accompanied by a warning.
They did not stand off to one side holding a little sign saying, "I'm here, but you don't like me."
They simply vanished.
He leaned back in his chair.
It was almost elegant.
Before, you had worn a sign around your neck.
Now they took the sign away and simply switched off the light in the room where you were standing.
Nobody banned you.
Nobody harassed you.
Nobody said, "Get out, you synthetic freak."
They simply did not show you to people who had decided in advance that they wanted nothing to do with texts over which the shadow of a machine had ever once passed.
It was called freedom of choice for the reader.
And it probably was.
The problem was that freedom of choice in the hands of a frightened person can turn into a quarantine line very quickly.
He opened the comments.
The first ones were predictable.
"Thanks for explaining."
"Much more convenient."
"Finally, that's clear."
"Should have done this ages ago."
"I support it. This is fairer."
Then he saw a comment that made something inside him stop.
Not because it was rude.
The opposite.
Precisely because it was not rude.
The reader wrote calmly, politely, almost gently:
"I'm fairly tolerant of covers made with AI. It's only an image, after all. But text is different. I want to know that I'm reading a person, not a well-constructed imitation. Before, you could see when an author honestly marked AI assistance, and I simply avoided those works. Now I'll have to rely on my own feeling. If a text doesn't sound alive, I won't read it."
He read it again.
Then once more.
And a third time.
There was nothing to grab hold of.
No rudeness. No stupidity. No "authors who use AI aren't people." No caps lock, vomiting emojis, curses, moral bludgeons or "real writers don't do that."
The opposite.
That was the problem.
She wanted to read living texts.
Was there anything wrong with that?
She wanted to read stories written by living people, with their souls.
Wasn't that what he wanted too?
Wasn't that why he sat in the kitchen at night changing "humanity" to "I," hiding behind a pseudonym, fearing slippers, fighting badges, changing covers, losing battles with online editors and trying not to become an offended genius with access to a keyboard?
He did not want to read dead texts either.
He too could taste something synthetic in many words even when they were grammatically sound, neatly arranged and possessed every sign of respectable literary posture.
He understood her.
And that was what irritated him most.
It is easier to argue with an idiot. An idiot is a gift to the nervous system. You can defeat him internally before you have even replied. An idiot brings you the hammer himself and lies down on the anvil. But a person with a legitimate fear who draws a dangerous conclusion from it—that is much less convenient. You cannot just swing a hammer at that. You have to think. And after yesterday's rubbish, Mahmoud and rewriting himself, he was not entirely ready to think.
"My own feeling."
That was the line that struck hardest.
Now a text had to pass not an editor.
Not a reader.
Not a critic.
Not even a detector.
Someone's inner feeling.
In the new literary age, the soul had become a watermark.
Nobody could see it. Nobody could describe it. Nobody had instructions for verifying it. But everyone demanded that it be legible from the first paragraph. Preferably before scrolling. Otherwise: suspicion.
He imagined the publication form of the future.
"Confirm that you are a living human being."
He clicks.
"Insufficient. Upload your soul as a PDF."
He uploads it.
"File too large."
He compresses it.
"File corrupted."
He uploads it again.
"Probability of soul: 43%."
Beside it, a button:
"Humanise — $4.99."
He laughed.
Quietly.
Without joy.
Laughter through tears often sounds less like laughter than a brief leak of air from a person reality has punctured once again.
He replied to the reader.
Not immediately.
First he wrote something sharp. Then deleted it.
Wrote something clever. Deleted that too.
After yesterday's lesson involving Mahmoud and a rubbish bag, it would have been especially elegant to climb right back onto the lectern and explain to another human being why she was afraid incorrectly.
In the end he wrote more simply:
"I understand why you're afraid of that. I don't want to read factory-made text either, something with no decision, no risk and no person willing to answer for it. But something else frightens me: if we start looking for a soul by external signs, we will very quickly learn to punish not soullessness, but anything that looks suspiciously polished to us."
She did not reply straight away.
"Then how are we supposed to tell? Authors can simply leave the label off."
There.
A normal question.
Not convenient. Not stupid. Normal.
He typed:
"Perhaps there is no guaranteed way. Just as we can't know for certain whether an author really lived through what they write about, stole somebody else's story or paid an editor to write half the book for them. We always read with some degree of trust. Otherwise literature turns into forensics."
He thought for a moment and added:
"I'm not asking us to trust everyone. I'm afraid of a world where distrust becomes the default setting."
He sent it.
It was almost indecent by now.
If she had written something stupid, he would have felt better. Instead, she stubbornly kept saying things you could not simply reject without turning yourself into counsel for chaos.
Yes, the reader had a right to protect herself.
Yes, authors could lie.
Yes, AI could generate texts by the batch.
Yes, platforms could drown in synthetic mass.
Yes, the problem was real.
And that was exactly why it was dangerous.
Because real problems are the ones most likely to produce bad solutions that look necessary.
He wrote:
"I'm not suggesting everyone should trust blindly. I'm suggesting we shouldn't replace reading with a search. Because if the first question we ask a text is 'am I being deceived?', the reader is no longer reading. They're inspecting. Inspection and encounter are different things."
He stopped.
Added:
"Maybe honest labelling is necessary. But when everything is reduced to one yes/no, and a cover, a comma, a translation and an entirely generated novel are all thrown into the same sack, we're not protecting the soul. We're sorting suspicion."
He sent it.
This time the reply did not come from the reader.
Another user inserted himself into the conversation.
Username: LitFox_ua.
Avatar: a fox in glasses.
Promising already.
"If an author uses AI, they're not an author. Full stop. Stop making excuses for laziness. A real writer writes for himself."
He looked at the message.
There he was.
Digital Mahmoud in reverse.
Mahmoud at least said honestly that he wanted to rip.
This one had arrived carrying an axe and called it a principle.
He wrote:
"'A real writer writes for himself' is a nice formula. But where exactly does this 'himself' begin? When an author runs the grammar checker in Word—is he still doing it himself? When the software underlines an error in red, is that already machine interference, or still an acceptable mercy of civilisation?"
Pause.
He smiled and added:
"Perhaps we should give up spellcheck, then. Better yet, computers altogether. Write manuscripts with a goose quill, dry the ink with sand, hide the pages in a chest and wait for a real reader to arrive on horseback, sit by the fire and say, 'Tell me, man, what hurts.'"
He almost pressed Send, but stopped.
Too sharp.
Turned the heat down a little.
There was no need to kill the other person with sarcasm. Sarcasm was an instrument too. And like any instrument, in the hands of an offended person it quickly became a blunt object.
He rewrote it:
"I'm not defending the replacement of an author with a machine. But if we begin measuring the purity of writing by the mere fact of using a tool, then we have to be consistent. Don't use grammar checking. Don't type on a computer. Don't watch modern films, because a large part of the image in them is created or altered by machines. Don't watch Avatar, because half that world was rendered by computers rather than growing organically on a tree under the supervision of a tribal elder."
He sent it.
LitFox took a long time to reply.
The reader, however, left a small neutral reaction under his comment. Not a like. Not a dislike. Something in between. The digital equivalent of somebody who has not agreed yet but has stopped throwing the stone.
He added another comment because stopping was difficult. Once a thought finally finds a crack, it pushes through like a weed between paving slabs.
"We already live in a world where most things pass through machines. A book is typed on a computer. A cover is laid out in software. Films are rendered. Music is cleaned up. Photos are processed. Cars are controlled by electronics. The phone we're using to write these comments is suggesting, correcting, selecting, sorting, hiding and showing things every second. If the mere presence of a machine makes art impure, then let's be honest: throw away the iPhone, don't buy a Tesla, give up streaming, modern cinema, satnav, banking apps and go back to telling stories around the fire at night."
He reread it.
It sounded almost like the manifesto of a techno-heretic.
But the thought was right.
LitFox finally replied:
"Don't twist it. We're talking about creativity. A machine can't have a soul."
He stared at those words longer than they deserved.
A machine cannot have a soul.
Perhaps.
Almost certainly.
But the question was not about the machine.
The question was about the person beside the machine.
If a person uses a dictionary, the dictionary does not become the author.
If an editor suggests cutting a paragraph, the editor does not live the author's life in his place.
If software fixes a comma, the comma does not receive copyright.
If AI suggests a version of a sentence and the human rejects ten others, keeps one, rewrites it, puts it into the text and answers for it under his own name—or even a pseudonym—where, precisely, does the human end?
He did not know.
And that was why he did not want to pretend that he did.
He wrote:
"I'm not claiming the machine has a soul. I'm asking whether a person loses theirs the second they pick up a new tool."
LitFox did not reply.
The reader did.
"No. But it's still important to me to feel that there's a person behind the text."
He smiled.
That was closer.
Not "prove it."
Not "show me your drafts."
Not "pass the detector."
Feel.
A bad word for an algorithm.
Useless for standardisation.
Impossible to turn into a checkbox.
But perhaps human for exactly that reason.
He wanted to reply that perhaps authorship did not live in the tool at all. Perhaps it lived where the decision was made. Where somebody chose what to say and what not to say. Where somebody risked looking like an idiot. Where somebody took responsibility for the sentence. Where the pain belonged not to the software.
He did not write it.
It sounded too beautiful.
Lately, beautiful sentences had begun to trigger a professional suspicion of himself.
He simply gave her the same neutral reaction.
A new kind of reciprocity.
Not like for like.
Doubt for doubt.
Then he opened the page of his work.
The views had risen to four.
Four.
Not seven trumpets.
Not twelve apostles.
Not forty days in the wilderness.
Four views.
But this time he did not feel humiliated.
He thought: perhaps one of those four was her.
Perhaps she had not finished it.
Perhaps she read the first paragraph and left.
Perhaps she opened it only out of suspicion.
Perhaps she was looking for the smell of AI.
Perhaps she was looking for a soul.
Perhaps she did not find one.
But she went in.
Sometimes a literary event in the new age looked exactly like that: not applause, not a review, not a letter from a publisher, not a queue for autographs.
One reader entered a text with no guarantee that there was a human being inside it.
He opened his document and wrote:
"A soul does not pass verification. It either answers or it doesn't. But if we enter a text wanting to expose a fraud, we may fail to hear even someone who is genuinely speaking."
Then he thought and added:
"Once, inconvenient authors were censored. Then they were ignored. Now there is no need to ban them or burn them. It is enough to teach the reader to suspect every well-shaped sentence."
He saved the file.
Called it:
"The Soul as a Watermark."
Then he closed the platform.
Not because he had won.
Not because he had persuaded anyone.
Not because he had become visible.
Because for the first time he felt that this novella, which he did not yet even know how to name, was no longer only about him.
It was about everyone who wrote at a time when the word "human" had stopped being self-evident.
About authors afraid of sounding too polished.
About readers afraid of being deceived.
About platforms that wanted to protect everyone and accidentally built new walls.
About critics who could avoid reading and still have an opinion.
About detectors that sold indulgences.
About covers guilty merely because they were beautiful.
About texts now required to prove that they had a pulse.
He sat in front of the dark screen and thought that Mahmoud, with his crude "I'm going to rip through every one of them," might have been the last honest man in this whole story.
At least he had not pretended he had come to sew.
The modern world, by contrast, arrived with scissors, cut trust into scraps and said:
— We're only trying to protect literature.
Then he switched off the laptop.
The kitchen went quiet.
And in that silence he suddenly understood the most unpleasant thing of all:
he would have to keep writing.
Because if he stayed silent now, it would no longer be privacy.
It would be complicity.

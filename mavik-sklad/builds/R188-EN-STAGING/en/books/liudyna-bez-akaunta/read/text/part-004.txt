CHAPTER 4. THE SOUL AS A WATERMARK

After replacing the text, he did not open the platform for almost twenty-four hours.
That was an achievement.
Not a major one, obviously. Nobody gives a medal to a grown man for going twenty-four hours without checking whether somebody has liked him. Though perhaps they should. In the age of digital addiction, such a medal ought to carry at least as much weight as a decoration for restraint under fire. On the front: a man with a trembling finger hovering over the notifications icon. On the reverse: “Still alive.”
He lasted until evening.
Then he opened it anyway.
Not because he expected a miracle.
He had already learned not to expect miracles. In the modern world, a miracle would probably begin by asking you to accept cookies, complete a CAPTCHA, verify your phone number and subscribe to the newsletter.
He opened the platform with the badges.
The same one where freedom began after verification, honesty was punished by a filter and an AI-generated cover sentenced a work to languish in a digital leper colony.
The platform greeted him with a new post from the administration.
The title was reassuring in that almost bureaucratic way:
“Update to the Rules on Works Using AI”
He opened it.
The administration announced that the labelling system had been simplified. Previously, an author had to state separately whether a cover had been created with AI, whether AI had been used in the text and, if so, to what extent. Now everything had been reduced to one question:
“Artificial intelligence: yes or no?”
No levels. No clarification. No distinction between a cover, editing, translation, a suggested idea or an entirely generated text.
Anything at all meant “yes.”
If AI had so much as coughed somewhere near the work — “yes.”
If the author had asked it to fix a comma — “yes.”
If it had translated the blurb — “yes.”
If it had generated a cover because the author could not afford an artist and, left alone in a graphics editor, was capable of producing only a rectangle with symptoms of depression — that was “yes” too.
As though somebody who called a taxi and somebody who stole a car had to tick the same box:
“Interacted with transport.”
There would no longer be any visual labels on the work cards.
This was presented as a relief.
Nobody would stick a label on your story, they said. Your work would look like any other. No badge. No public shaming. Everyone equal. Everyone beautiful. Everyone in literature.
But the reader received a switch:
“Show works involving AI.”
On by default.
Turn it off and works marked “yes” disappeared from the catalogue, search and the home page.
Completely.
They were not dimmed.
They were not accompanied by a warning.
They did not stand off to one side holding a little sign saying, “I’m here, but you don’t like me.”
They simply vanished.
He leaned back in his chair.
It was almost elegant.
Before, you had worn a sign around your neck.
Now they took the sign away and simply switched off the light in the room where you were standing.
Nobody banned you.
Nobody harassed you.
Nobody said, “Get out, you synthetic freak.”
They simply did not show you to people who had decided in advance that they wanted nothing to do with texts over which the shadow of a machine had ever once passed.
It was called freedom of choice for the reader.
And it probably was.
The problem was that freedom of choice in the hands of a frightened person can turn into a quarantine line very quickly.
He opened the comments.
The first ones were predictable.
“Thanks for explaining.”
“Much more convenient.”
“Finally, that’s clear.”
“Should have done this ages ago.”
“I support it. This is fairer.”
Then he saw a comment that made something inside him stop.
Not because it was rude.
The opposite.
Precisely because it was not rude.
The reader wrote calmly, politely, almost gently:
“I’m fairly tolerant of covers made with AI. It’s only an image, after all. But text is different. I want to know that I’m reading a person, not a well-constructed imitation. Before, you could see when an author honestly marked AI assistance, and I simply avoided those works. Now I’ll have to rely on my own feeling. If a text doesn’t sound alive, I won’t read it.”
He read it again.
Then once more.
And a third time.
There was nothing to grab hold of.
No rudeness. No stupidity. No “authors who use AI aren’t people.” No caps lock, vomiting emojis, curses, moral bludgeons or “real writers don’t do that.”
The opposite.
That was the problem.
She wanted to read living texts.
Was there anything wrong with that?
She wanted to read stories written by living people, with their souls.
Wasn’t that what he wanted too?
Wasn’t that why he sat in the kitchen at night changing “humanity” to “I,” hiding behind a pseudonym, fearing slippers, fighting badges, changing covers, losing battles with online editors and trying not to become an offended genius with access to a keyboard?
He did not want to read dead texts either.
He too could taste something synthetic in many words even when they were grammatically sound, neatly arranged and possessed every sign of respectable literary posture.
He understood her.
And that was what irritated him most.
It is easier to argue with an idiot. An idiot is a gift to the nervous system. You can defeat him internally before you have even replied. An idiot brings you the hammer himself and lies down on the anvil. But a person with a legitimate fear who draws a dangerous conclusion from it — that is much less convenient. You cannot just swing a hammer at that. You have to think. And after yesterday’s rubbish, Mahmoud and rewriting himself, he was not entirely ready to think.
“My own feeling.”
That was the line that struck hardest.
Now a text had to pass not an editor.
Not a reader.
Not a critic.
Not even a detector.
Someone’s inner feeling.
In the new literary age, the soul had become a watermark.
Nobody could see it. Nobody could describe it. Nobody had instructions for verifying it. But everyone demanded that it be legible from the first paragraph. Preferably before scrolling. Otherwise: suspicion.
He imagined the publication form of the future.
“Confirm that you are a living human being.”
He clicks.
“Insufficient. Upload your soul as a PDF.”
He uploads it.
“File too large.”
He compresses it.
“File corrupted.”
He uploads it again.
“Probability of soul: 43%.”
Beside it, a button:
“Humanise — $4.99.”
He laughed.
Quietly.
Without joy.
Laughter through tears often sounds less like laughter than a brief leak of air from a person reality has punctured once again.
He opened the reply box.
His fingers hovered over the keyboard.
Write?
Don’t write?
Reply to the reader? Would it look like an attack? An excuse? An attempt by an author nobody had asked to explain to everyone why their fears were wrong?
He did not want to attack her.
Because she genuinely had the right not to read texts created entirely by AI.
She had the right to protect her attention.
The right not to trust platforms.
The right not to want to be deceived.
The problem was that her right to caution was turning into his duty to prove he was alive.
And that was already too much.
He began to type.
Under his pseudonym.
Without a face.
Without a biography.
With no guarantee they would not start throwing slippers at him within three minutes.
“I understand your desire to read living texts. I don’t want to read empty imitation either. But something else frightens me: if the criterion becomes an ‘inner feeling,’ a living author can just as easily fall under suspicion. Simply because somebody finds his style too even, too clean or not ‘soulful’ enough.”
He reread it.
Too polite.
Almost like a letter to the housing office.
He added:
“Writers used to be afraid their text would be judged bad. Now they have to be afraid it will be judged not alive.”
That was better.
He sent it.
And regretted it immediately.
That was how every important message on the internet worked: first you pressed Send, then your soul caught up with your finger and shouted, “Where are you going, idiot?”
The reply did not come at once.
In those twenty minutes he managed to make tea, drink half a cup, forget about the tea, remember it, open the platform seven times, close it six, accidentally visit his own statistics once, see that nothing had changed, and once again confirm that, in terms of inner dignity, a person waiting for a reply in the comments is not very different from a dog waiting by the kitchen door.
At last a notification appeared.
The reader had replied.
“I understand that people can make mistakes. But so many texts are being created by AI now, and as a reader I hate wasting time on something a human being has put nothing of themselves into. I want to feel the author. Their pain, their experience, their soul. If that isn’t there, the text is dead to me.”
He stared at those lines and could not get angry.
Because all of it was right.
And wrong at the same time.
That was how it usually happened.
The world rarely breaks because of pure stupidity. Pure stupidity is too simple. The real cracks begin where two truths face each other and both are holding knives.
He replied:
“And if the author put himself into it but you didn’t feel it? Is he dead then? Or simply not your author?”
He pressed Send.
This time he did not regret it.
The question was precise.
Perhaps even too precise.
The answer came faster.
“Then maybe he’s simply not for me. But you have to admit, AI often writes smoothly, correctly, beautifully — but without a soul. You can feel it.”
There it was.
You can feel it.
The most frightening phrase of the new literary age.
Not “I saw it.”
Not “I can prove it.”
Not “I can explain it.”
Not “here are the signs.”
But “you can feel it.”
He did not object.
Yes, sometimes you really could feel it.
And sometimes a person simply did not like the text.
Sometimes the text was bad.
Sometimes it was too good for an unknown author.
Sometimes the reader was tired.
Sometimes the cover got in the way.
Sometimes the reader had arrived already suspicious, and suspicion did all the work instead of reading.
Human intuition is a wonderful instrument as long as you do not give it a prosecutor’s badge.
He wrote:
“AI really does often write smoothly. But now that means any polished text can become suspicious. And any crooked one automatically becomes ‘alive.’ It creates a strange trap: the author starts thinking not only about how to write better, but about how not to look too good.”
Pause.
Then he added:
“This doesn’t protect literature. It teaches the author to disguise himself as a human being.”
He sent it.
And immediately understood: there it was.
The point.
AI was not killing writing.
Writing died at the moment a living author began deliberately leaving scratches in the text so nobody would mistake him for a machine.
When a mistake became proof of a soul.
When carelessness became a passport of humanity.
When a reader entered a text not with openness but with a metal detector.
He opened a blank document and wrote:
“AI is not what kills literature. Literature dies the moment a reader enters a text not with trust, but with a metal detector.”
He looked at it.
Yes.
He would need that later.
He went back to the comments.
The reader’s reply was already there.
“What do you suggest, then? Just believing everyone? Authors will hide their AI use en masse. Readers have a right to protect themselves too.”
He rubbed his face.
She was right again.
It was becoming almost indecent.
If she had written something stupid, he would have felt better. Instead, she stubbornly kept saying things you could not simply reject without turning yourself into counsel for chaos.
Yes, the reader had a right to protect herself.
Yes, authors could lie.
Yes, AI could generate texts by the batch.
Yes, platforms could drown in synthetic mass.
Yes, the problem was real.
And that was exactly why it was dangerous.
Because real problems are the ones most likely to produce bad solutions that look necessary.
He wrote:
“I’m not suggesting everyone should trust blindly. I’m suggesting we shouldn’t replace reading with a search. Because if the first question we ask a text is ‘am I being deceived?’, the reader is no longer reading. They’re inspecting. Inspection and encounter are different things.”
He stopped.
Added:
“Maybe honest labelling is necessary. But when everything is reduced to one yes/no, and a cover, a comma, a translation and an entirely generated novel are all thrown into the same sack, we’re not protecting the soul. We’re sorting suspicion.”
He sent it.
This time the reply did not come from the reader.
Another user inserted himself into the conversation.
Username: LitFox_ua.
Avatar: a fox in glasses.
Promising already.
“If an author uses AI, then he isn’t an author. Full stop. Stop making excuses for laziness. A real writer writes by himself.”
He looked at the message.
There he was.
Digital Mahmoud in reverse.
Mahmoud at least said honestly that he wanted to rip.
This one had arrived carrying an axe and called it a principle.
He wanted to reply with something short.
For example:
“A real writer also thinks for himself. Give it a try.”
But he restrained himself.
A backbone is an underrated part of romance.
Instead he wrote:
“Editor, proofreader, translator, dictionary, search engine, a friend’s advice, a beta reader — all of those are help too. The question isn’t whether an author uses tools. The question is where the tool ends and the replacement of the author begins.”
LitFox replied almost instantly:
“Don’t twist this. AI isn’t a dictionary. AI can write instead of you.”
He nodded to himself.
Yes.
It could.
And that was exactly why the conversation was so slippery.
A knife can cut bread or a person. But nobody puts a badge on a kitchen knife saying “may be used in a crime,” although perhaps that was only a matter of time. Humanity loved labelling things when it did not know what to do with people.
He was about to write a short reply when he suddenly felt the bad polemicist waking inside him.
The one best kept on a leash, because he starts with arguments and ends by suggesting that his opponents go live in a cave and at least be consistent about it.
He wrote:
“‘A real writer writes by himself’ is a nice formula. But where exactly does this ‘himself’ begin? When an author runs the grammar checker in Word — is he still doing it himself? When the software underlines an error in red, is that already machine interference, or still an acceptable mercy of civilisation?”
Pause.
He smiled and added:
“Perhaps we should give up spellcheck, then. Better yet, computers altogether. Write manuscripts with a goose quill, dry the ink with sand, hide the pages in a chest and wait for a real reader to arrive on horseback, sit by the fire and say, ‘Tell me, man, what hurts.’”
He almost pressed Send, but stopped.
Too sharp.
Turned the heat down a little.
There was no need to kill the other person with sarcasm. Sarcasm was an instrument too. And like any instrument, in the hands of an offended person it quickly became a blunt object.
He rewrote it:
“I’m not defending the replacement of an author with a machine. But if we begin measuring the purity of writing by the mere fact of using a tool, then we have to be consistent. Don’t use grammar checking. Don’t type on a computer. Don’t watch modern films, because a large part of the image in them is created or altered by machines. Don’t watch Avatar, because half that world was rendered by computers rather than growing organically on a tree under the supervision of a tribal elder.”
He sent it.
LitFox took a long time to reply.
The reader, however, left a small neutral reaction under his comment. Not a like. Not a dislike. Something in between. The digital equivalent of somebody who has not agreed yet but has stopped throwing the stone.
He added another comment because stopping was difficult. Once a thought finally finds a crack, it pushes through like a weed between paving slabs.
“We already live in a world where most things pass through machines. A book is typed on a computer. A cover is laid out in software. Films are rendered. Music is cleaned up. Photos are processed. Cars are controlled by electronics. The phone we’re using to write these comments is suggesting, correcting, selecting, sorting, hiding and showing things every second. If the mere presence of a machine makes art impure, then let’s be honest: throw away the iPhone, don’t buy a Tesla, give up streaming, modern cinema, satnav, banking apps and go back to telling stories around the fire at night.”
He reread it.
It sounded almost like the manifesto of a techno-heretic.
But the thought was right.
He added one more thing:
“And that, by the way, could be a beautiful life. A cult in the woods. Complete isolation from modern society. Evening. Fire. An old storyteller. Children with their mouths open. Smoke. Stars. No algorithms. No badges. No Word arrogantly knowing where you ought to put a comma. Just a person, a voice and darkness all around. I can even see the beauty in it. But then we need to say it honestly: we are not protecting literature in the modern world. We are running away from the modern world.”
He sent it.
After that, silence settled over the discussion.
The same silence that falls in a room when somebody carelessly says not something stupid, but something uncomfortably close to the truth.
The reader replied first.
“But there is a difference between a tool and an author. Word doesn’t write a novel for you.”
He almost said, “Not yet.”
But restrained himself.
Instead he wrote:
“Yes. There is a difference. And it matters enormously. That is exactly what I’m saying. The problem isn’t the tool itself. The problem is degree. Honesty. Who makes the decisions. If a machine suggests a comma, that is one thing. If a machine writes the heart of the text instead of the author, that is another. But the current system often throws everything into the same jar: comma, cover, translation, editing, idea, paragraph, novel. All of it is ‘yes.’ All of it is suspicion. All of it goes behind the filter.”
LitFox finally came back to life.
“You just want to legitimise hackwork.”
That was almost sweet.
When people run out of arguments, they often find the word “hackwork” and feel as though they have pulled a sword from a stone.
He wrote:
“Hackwork existed long before AI. It just used to be written by hand. Sometimes very diligently. Sometimes with soul. None of that stopped it being hackwork.”
Then he added:
“A good text can also be created in dialogue with tools. The question isn’t whether a machine touched the text. The question is whether the human being is still there. Their choice. Their intonation. Their responsibility. Their shame. Their risk. Their pain.”
The reader did not reply straight away.
This time he did not yank the tab open every ten seconds.
He sat and stared at his own comment.
“Whether the human being is still there.”
Perhaps that was the heart of it.
Not “was a tool used?”
But “who ultimately answers for what was said?”
Because a real author is not someone who has never touched a machine.
A real author is someone who does not hide behind one.
Someone who can say:
this sentence is mine.
Even if I checked the comma.
Even if I looked for the word.
Even if I asked for advice.
Even if I rewrote it.
Even if somebody edited me.
Even if I argued with software, a dictionary, a friend, a critic, my wife, my own conscience and the night-time fear that nobody needs any of this.
Mine — because I answer for it.
He added that in a separate message:
“Perhaps the boundary isn’t where the tool appears. Perhaps it is where responsibility disappears. If an author does not answer for the text, then he is no longer an author. But if he answers for every phrase, every choice, every revision, every bit of shame over a failed sentence, then the machine did not take his soul away. It simply stood there beside the hammer, the dictionary, the keyboard, the editor and the cold tea.”
He sent it.
And suddenly thought that this was no longer a comment.
It was a piece of a future text.
All he had to do was not ruin it.
The reader returned to the discussion.
“I think part of the problem is that we can’t know where the truth is. An author can say he wrote it himself when he didn’t. That’s why I talk about inner feeling.”
He sighed.
There was the real knot.
Not AI.
Not the badge.
Not the switch.
Distrust.
It stood in the middle of the room like an old refrigerator nobody knew how to get out because the doorway was too narrow and their backs were not what they used to be.
He wrote:
“Yes. We really can’t know everything. But literature has never been a place of complete knowledge. We don’t know how much an author took from life, how much he stole from acquaintances, how much he spied on, embellished, lied about, suffered through, or simply invented well. We never read a passport of truth. We read a text.”
He thought and added:
“If a text touched you, then it did its job. If it didn’t, it didn’t. But when we start looking for a soul as though it were a certificate of origin, we risk ceasing to hear the text itself.”
He sent it.
This time the pause was longer.
He managed to finish his tea.
The tea, surprisingly, was still warm. A small victory of civilisation over entropy.
Then the reply came.
“Maybe you’re right. But I still don’t want to read AI texts. I want to know there is a human being behind the words.”
He stared at that sentence for a long time.
And for the first time in the whole discussion, he felt not irritated but sad.
Because there they agreed.
He wanted to know there was a human being behind the words too.
He always had.
More than that: he wrote precisely because he wanted to put a human being behind the words. Himself. Not a façade, not a profession, not an avatar, not a pseudonym, not the role of father, husband, employee, quiet man, domestic functionary who knows where the rubbish bags are kept in the house.
A human being.
With skeletons.
Every bone of them.
With shame.
With the desire to look cooler than boiled eggs in front of his wife.
With night-time texts that had begun as emotional masturbation and now suddenly demanded from him an almost theological answer to the question: where does the soul live in a sentence?
He wrote:
“I want there to be a human being behind the words too. I’m simply afraid we’ll reach the point where the human being is standing behind them, waving his arms, shouting ‘I’m here,’ and the answer comes back: ‘I don’t feel it. Probably AI.’”
He sent it.
Then added separately:
“And then it won’t be a machine that kills writing. It will be our inability to trust one another anymore.”
After that, the chat fell silent.
LitFox wrote no more.
Perhaps he had gone off to defend literature in another thread. Perhaps he was drafting a manifesto. Perhaps he had asked GPT to generate ten arguments against GPT and was now choosing the most human-sounding ones.
The reader gave him a like.
One.
Not a reply.
Not agreement.
Not victory.
Just a like.
He looked at it and did not know what he felt.
On the one hand, it was trivial. A digital nod. Pixel charity. The same conventional unit of warmth as “best of luck with your writing, hope you return the favour.”
On the other hand, it was the first like that seemed not to have been given for a blurb.
Not out of reciprocity.
Not because she expected him to visit her page.
But for a thought.
Maybe even for the human being behind that thought.
He opened the statistics for his work.
The views had risen to four.
Four.
Not seven trumpets.
Not twelve apostles.
Not forty days in the wilderness.
Four views.
But this time he did not feel humiliated.
He thought: perhaps one of those four was her.
Perhaps she had not finished it.
Perhaps she read the first paragraph and left.
Perhaps she opened it only out of suspicion.
Perhaps she was looking for the smell of AI.
Perhaps she was looking for a soul.
Perhaps she did not find one.
But she went in.
Sometimes a literary event in the new age looked exactly like that: not applause, not a review, not a letter from a publisher, not a queue for autographs.
One reader entered a text with no guarantee that there was a human being inside it.
He opened his document and wrote:
“A soul does not pass verification. It either answers or it doesn’t. But if we enter a text wanting to expose a fraud, we may fail to hear even someone who is genuinely speaking.”
Then he thought and added:
“Once, inconvenient authors were censored. Then they were ignored. Now there is no need to ban them or burn them. It is enough to teach the reader to suspect every polished sentence.”
He saved the file.
Called it:
“The Soul as a Watermark.”
Then he closed the platform.
Not because he had won.
Not because he had persuaded anyone.
Not because he had become visible.
Because for the first time he felt that this novella, which he did not yet even know how to name, was no longer only about him.
It was about everyone who wrote at a time when the word “human” had stopped being self-evident.
About authors afraid of sounding too polished.
About readers afraid of being deceived.
About platforms that wanted to protect everyone and accidentally built new walls.
About critics who could avoid reading and still have an opinion.
About detectors that sold indulgences.
About covers guilty merely because they were beautiful.
About texts now required to prove that they had a pulse.
He sat in front of the dark screen and thought that Mahmoud, with his crude “I’m going to rip through every one of them,” might have been the last honest man in this whole story.
At least he had not pretended he had come to sew.
The modern world, by contrast, arrived with scissors, cut trust into scraps and said:
— We’re only trying to protect literature.
Then he switched off the laptop.
The kitchen went quiet.
And in that silence he suddenly understood the most unpleasant thing of all:
he would have to keep writing.
Because if he stayed silent now, it would no longer be privacy.
It would be complicity.

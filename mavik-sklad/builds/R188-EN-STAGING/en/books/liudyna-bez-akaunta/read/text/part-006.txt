AUTHOR'S AFTERWORD

If, after reading this novella, someone came away thinking it was about artificial intelligence, badges, literary platforms, author filters, suspicious readers, reciprocal likes, invisible algorithms, digital segregation or a man who dreams far too much about Mahmoud at night — well, that is not entirely wrong either.

But it is not entirely true.

This novella is not about AI.

AI here is only a thermometer.

And when a person has a fever, a thermometer is, of course, very important. It shows the temperature. It confirms that something is wrong. It can even frighten you if the number suddenly climbs higher than a person would like to see after spending yesterday convincing himself that “it’ll pass on its own.”

But the thermometer is not the disease.

And it certainly is not the treatment.

It seems to me that we live in a time when humanity has become very fond of thermometers. We have learned to measure symptoms precisely. Name them. Discuss them. Label them. Filter them. Ban them. Sort them. Put them into separate categories. Add switches. Set flags. Turn off what frightens us. Hide what we do not trust.

But that does not always make the patient better.

Sometimes he simply lies in the dark with a perfectly completed medical chart.

For me, this novella is not about whether an author may use AI. Nor is it about whether a reader has the right not to want to read texts created by a machine. They do. Of course they do. A reader has many rights, including the sacred right not to read something that does not interest them, does not feel close, does not hurt, does not breathe, or simply refuses to get under their skin even after the third attempt.

An author also has the right to fear.
To doubt.
To tools.
To mistakes.
To a bad cover.
To a good cover that can now be held against him.
To a text somebody finds too even.
To a sentence which, God forbid, does not contain enough human limps to pass the soul-sniffing test.

But an author's most important right is to answer for what he says.

Not to hide behind a machine. Not to blame a tool for what he himself failed to think through, feel, suffer through and bring into form. Not to hand the reader an empty imitation and demand that it be called alive simply because the author ticked the correct box.

And the reader's most important right is to look for the human being in the text.

Not the badge.

Not the certificate.

Not a certificate of origin for the soul.

The human being.

Their voice. Their intonation. Their responsibility. Their shame. Their laughter. Their clumsy attempt to say something honest in a world that learned long ago how to sell even honesty in attractive packaging.

Perhaps someone will say I have made a simple subject too complicated.

Perhaps.

If someone did not understand my point, please forgive me: perhaps I should have used AI to simplify the meanings, optimise the emotional load, improve readability, adapt the thought to the target audience and create the conditions for better comprehension.

That would have been simpler.

For example:

People, do not be afraid of tools. Be afraid of losing the human being behind the words.

Or simpler still:

Not every kind of help is substitution. Not every suspicion is wisdom. Not every badge is truth.

Or, for those in a real hurry:

AI is not the main disease. The main disease is distrust.

But, unfortunately or fortunately, literature does not exist only to make everything simpler.

Sometimes we need it to complicate things where the world has already rushed to a diagnosis.

Sometimes — to remind us that if a patient has a fever, it is not enough to tell him, “You have a fever.”

At the very least, give him some water.

Sometimes — to sit beside him and ask:

— Where does it hurt?

Not from above.

Not from a lectern.

Not with a badge.

Not with a metal detector.

Beside him.

Because perhaps writing begins precisely where one person stops proving to another that they are alive and simply tries to speak in a way that might finally be heard.

And if they are not heard — well.

There is nothing more I can do about that.

Except perhaps bring in Mahmoud.

Because where is my hand — and where is your certainty that it is all so simple?

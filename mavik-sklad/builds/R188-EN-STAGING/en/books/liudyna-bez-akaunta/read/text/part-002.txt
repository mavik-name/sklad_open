CHAPTER 2. SEVEN LIKES AND THREE BOTS

What he had published was not a story in the usual sense.
Nobody killed anyone. Nobody fell in love with the mysterious woman next door. Nobody woke up in a world where every human being had vanished except him, a cat and a mysterious lighthouse on the horizon. There were no vampires, no elves, no policeman taking on one last case three days before retirement, no young witch discovering her powers at exactly the moment suspiciously handsome men begin disappearing from her town.
It was more of a story-message.
An ungrateful genre.
Something halfway between a confession, a manifesto, a message in a bottle, culture's suicide note and an overlong post in the family group chat after which everyone pretends they never saw it.
In it, he tried to explain why he was doing any of this at all.
Why he wrote if he was not a writer.
Why he spoke if nobody had asked him to.
Why he insisted on bringing his thoughts into a world that had long ago invented the perfect defence against thought: endless scrolling.
The text had a simple title: "Why."
He had turned the title over for ages, like an old bolt that would neither tighten nor come out. "A Letter to Humanity" sounded as though he had already called the psychiatric orderlies. "On the Meaning of Silence" sounded as though the orderlies were already on their way. "Why I Write" was too school-essay, almost eighth-grade: the sort of thing where you were obliged to mention Taras Shevchenko, Mum and love of the homeland.
"Why" was short enough not to disgrace itself before the first sentence.
In the text, he wrote that the world had not suddenly become worse. The world had not woken up one morning and said:
— Shall I turn myself into a brightly coloured rubbish dump with delivery today?
No. It had all happened much more respectably.
People had simply agreed, one step at a time, to trade large losses for small conveniences. First they gave away attention, because it was easier. Then privacy, because it was more convenient. Then dignity, because it was more profitable. Then shame, because it interfered with monetisation. And by the time they came to their senses, they discovered they still had everything except themselves.
Not his worst thought, he felt.
Almost respectable, even.
He reread the text three times. Then twice more. Then once aloud, in a whisper, because reading your own work out loud at night in a flat where your family is asleep is no longer literature. It is the first symptom.
On the fourth reading, he thought the text was brilliant.
On the fifth, he thought it should be deleted immediately and the laptop taken out with the rubbish disguised as household equipment that had failed to justify the trust placed in it.
On the sixth, he decided it could probably be published.
That was the best possible state for publication: when an author no longer believes in himself enough to be dangerous but does not yet despise himself enough to be sensible.
His anonymous account had three followers.
Three is a biblical number.
Three Magi. Three temptations. Peter's three denials. Three followers who were most likely bots.
The first had an avatar of a girl in a swimsuit who followed everyone indiscriminately and, according to her bio, helped people "unlock financial freedom through crypto, femininity and discipline." The second was a profile with no photo, no posts and a username resembling the Wi-Fi password in a small hotel. The third was called BookSoul_777, but in the past two years had shown no sign of a soul, books or the number seven hundred and seventy-seven beyond the bare fact of its existence.
An audience you could certainly rely on.
Like a three-legged stool with two of the legs painted on.
He looked at these three apostles of the digital wilderness and realised that if he wrote only here, his text would be seen by either a bot, a crypto-Aphrodite or nobody.
Writing for the drawer was no answer.
But writing in a basement where three bots played the silent game was not much better.
So he did what anyone does who has not quite lost all faith in humanity but is already close enough to act recklessly: he began looking for platforms for writers.
There turned out to be a lot of them online.
A suspicious number.
Every one promised roughly the same thing: freedom of expression, a lively reading community, support for authors, easy publishing, honest statistics, openness to new voices and something else so wonderful that you immediately wanted to look for the small print saying, "but nobody will actually read you."
He registered on every one he could find.
Not because he was an optimist.
Despair sometimes looks remarkably like method.
He used the same pseudonym everywhere. Uploaded the same text everywhere. Wrote the same blurb everywhere, because inventing five different ways to say "I've brought you some pain, but it isn't very long" was already a separate form of literary prostitution.
That left the cover.
He was not a designer.
That too had to be admitted honestly, without trying to turn helplessness into heroism. Some people have taste. Some have training. Some have an eye. He had a mouse, nerves and twenty minutes until he had to take out the rubbish before his wife looked at him with the expression that reduces all philosophy in the world to a single question:
"Are you going, or do I have to do it myself?"
So he generated a cover in GPT.
Quickly. Well. Almost beautifully.
Too beautifully, if he was honest. There was something suspiciously flawless about it. Like people who look in every photograph as though they have never once woken up with a face resembling a crumpled supermarket receipt.
The cover came out dark and restrained, with a solitary figure against a gleaming city. Nothing superfluous. No eyes in the sky, no cracks across a face, no black birds, no streetlamps hinting at "the author's profound inner conflict." He was almost surprised.
AI, unlike people, had not asked him to smile for the promo.
He uploaded the cover.
On the first platform, everything went smoothly.
On the second, too.
The third accepted his text with such immediate enthusiasm that it felt suspicious. The site announced, "Your work has been successfully published!" in the tone of a cash machine that had just dispensed someone else's money and was itself unsure whether that had been the right thing to do.
On the fourth platform, a message appeared:
"Your work is undergoing preliminary verification."
He read it again.
Then once more.
A platform whose homepage proclaimed in large letters that it was "completely free from censorship" had just informed him that freedom began after preliminary verification.
It was so beautiful he did not even get angry.
The modern world had a gift for constructing sentences that gave logic a migraine.
"Absolute freedom in accordance with community rules."
"Complete openness after moderation."
"No censorship, with your safety in mind."
"Your voice matters to us, which is why we have temporarily hidden it."
He sighed.
Fine.
If freedom wanted to see his passport first, let it look.
Two minutes later, another message arrived.
Not a day. Not an hour. Two minutes.
The verification machine had worked faster than some relatives answered the question "what should we get the child for their birthday?" although they would still buy the wrong thing in the end.
"You did not indicate that the cover was created using AI."
He looked at the screen.
— Forgive me, Your Honour, — he muttered. — I failed to recognise the criminal in myself.
He had to tick a box.
He ticked it.
After that, a badge appeared on the cover.
Not small. Not discreet. Not something that quietly informed an attentive reader where the image had come from. No. The badge landed on the cover the way a drunk uncle sits down on a minibus: broad, heavy and with no awareness that he might be making anyone else uncomfortable.
"Cover created by AI."
That was it.
There was no need to read the text now. The verdict had been delivered. What stood before you was not a work but a digital bastard of suspicious pedigree.
He stared at the ruined cover and tried to behave like an adult.
Rules are rules.
It is the phrase humanity usually uses to cover small stupidities, medium-sized cruelties and major historical catastrophes. This time, though, it was only a book cover, so he decided not to start a revolution before dinner.
At last, the text was published.
On every platform.
The same pseudonym.
The same "Why."
The same cover, except that on one platform it now carried a brand as though he had not generated a picture but brought a dangerous animal without a muzzle to a children's party.
After publishing, he felt a strange relief.
As though he had finally taken out rubbish that had been standing in his soul for several years and was beginning to negotiate with the cockroaches.
He had expected to start checking the statistics every ten minutes immediately.
He did not.
Life turned out to be less literary than his inner dramas.
The next morning he had to buy groceries. Then the handle on a cupboard door broke. Then one of the children needed something "for tomorrow," although it was obvious that this "tomorrow" had begun the day before yesterday and nobody had wanted to trouble Dad prematurely. Then his wife asked him to collect an order. Then it turned out the order was not there. Then he went to the wrong place. Then customer support wrote, "we are sorry you experienced this inconvenience," which translated from corporate language meant, "This is your problem, but we've learned to blink sympathetically."
A week passed in domestic errands.
That was the part of life that kept a person from finally losing his mind because it constantly reminded him: if you want to think about the meaning of existence, buy toilet paper first.
And then, on Friday evening, he finally sat down at the computer.
The house was not asleep yet, but it was slowly entering its nocturnal state: somebody was brushing their teeth, somebody was looking for a charger, somebody was loudly asking where his T-shirt was even though the T-shirt lay where it always lay, namely in a place inaccessible to male vision without female assistance.
He waited for silence.
Made tea.
Sat down.
Opened all the platforms in separate tabs.
Five tabs.
Five doors into the world.
Five chances that somebody had finally heard him.
The result was like a fisherman arriving at a reservoir that had just been refilled after draining, after all the fish had been taken out, the silt hauled away, the frogs sold and the ducks politely asked not to create false expectations.
On the first platform, there were three views.
Three.
That biblical number again.
Perhaps the world was insistently hinting that his entire literary fate would unfold somewhere between three bots and three views, and everything beyond that would depend on the strength of his faith and the quality of his internet connection.
He opened the statistics.
They were as concise as a person who does not want to hurt you but is already holding a knife.
Views: 3.
That was all.
No reading depth. No time on page. No "reader reached the paragraph where you finally stopped being a bore and said something human."
Three views could mean anything.
One person opened it and got frightened by the title.
The second read the first paragraph and decided life was too short.
The third was a bot checking whether he sold crypto, femininity and discipline.
He sighed.
It was not even a defeat.
Defeat requires a battle to have taken place. This was more like walking onto the field and discovering that the field had failed to load.
The second platform was better.
Seven likes.
Seven messages.
He felt a small, shameful wave of joy rise inside him.
Seven was even more biblical. Seven days of creation. Seven seals. Seven trumpets. Seven likes from unknown people who might not be bots.
He opened the comments.
The first:
"Interesting. Nice blurb. Good luck! Followed you, hope you'll follow back)))"
The second:
"Wishing you inspiration! I'd be happy to see you on my page. Follow from me)"
The third:
"Very intriguing. Drop by my page, I write about important things too!"
The fourth:
"Good luck with your writing! Supported you with a like, hoping you'll return the favour!"
The fifth:
"Interesting idea. Like and follow. See you on my page)"
The sixth:
"Great start! Supporting new authors matters to me too. Come by!"
The seventh was the shortest:
"Follow back?"
He stared at those two words for a long time.
They contained everything.
The whole literary community compressed into one commercial cough.
Follow back?
Not "I read it."
Not "I disagree."
Not "It got to me."
Not "what did you mean?"
Not "your third paragraph is strong, but after that you mounted your own high horse and rode it off into the fog."
Just:
"Follow back?"
That was not a comment.
It was a tiny sales contract disguised as support.
He clicked the first username.
A page opened belonging to an author who was writing a novel about a girl with a magical gift, two brothers, a dark past, a power of light, forbidden love and an ancient prophecy that, judging by the blurb, should have sued for overtime long ago.
Followers: 218.
Following: 217.
He clicked the second.
Followers: 341.
Following: 340.
The third:
Followers: 129.
Following: 131.
The fourth:
Followers: 502.
Following: 499.
This was no longer reading.
It was the bookkeeping of loneliness.
A platform for readers had turned out to be a platform for writers circling one another like people at a wake where everyone has come not so much to say goodbye to the deceased as to check whether the others will eventually turn up at theirs.
Everyone liked everyone.
Everyone followed everyone.
Everyone wrote "good luck," "inspiration," "drop by," "happy to return the support."
The whole system resembled a market where sellers stood opposite other sellers and spent all day buying their own tomatoes from one another so they would not have to admit there were no customers.
Or a henhouse in which every hen declared herself a nightingale and demanded that the other hens confirm it immediately with a like.
He had nothing against these people.
They probably wanted to be heard too. They too wrote after work. They too stole time from sleep, family, their backs, their eyes and common sense. They too dreamed that one day someone would open their text not from an obligation to reciprocate but because they could not stop reading.
That only made it sadder.
This was not a community.
It was a waiting room where everyone pretended to be the conductor.
He opened his own text on the platform.
Likes: seven.
Comments: seven.
Reads: zero.
A separate line in the statistics reported this mercilessly and neatly, like a doctor who already knows the diagnosis but is still looking for a pen.
Zero.
They had not even opened the text.
They liked the blurb.
They followed him.
They left a little digital gingerbread biscuit on the windowsill and went away to wait for him to bring theirs.
He found it funny.
Not amusing.
Funny.
The kind of laugh a person might make when a hospital offers them a loyalty card instead of an operation.
He thought perhaps this was the new form of literature.
Without reading.
Reading was an unnecessary link in the chain anyway, dangerous to productivity.
It took time, generated thoughts, provoked discussions, sometimes even changed a person—and who needed that in the age of reciprocal likes?
You could build a service where authors uploaded not texts but merely their expectations of support.
Name: "Follow Back."
Genres: "follow me," "drop by," "liked," "good luck," "very interesting, didn't read it."
Prize of the year for the most sincere comment on a work nobody had opened.
He took a sip of tea.
It had gone cold and now tasted like accepting reality without sugar.
The third platform had zero.
The fourth had zero too.
The fifth had zero with such confidence that it seemed the platform had not merely failed to show his text but had taken a principled objection to the fact that he had ever been born.
Zero is a very honest number.
It does not flatter. Does not promise. Does not say "good luck." Does not follow back. Zero does not fake anything. Zero looks you in the eye and says:
— For now, you're nobody.
And there was, strangely enough, a certain dignity in that.
What disturbed him most was not the absence of readers.
He had been mentally prepared for that. Or so he had thought.
What disturbed him was something else: the readers seemed to be there.
The platforms were alive. Updates, covers, rankings, comments, blogs, competitions, collections, "hot," "new," "top this week," "editor's choice," "trending now" flickered everywhere. Something was always moving. Somebody was reading somebody. Somebody was arguing with somebody. Somebody was thanking people for support. Somebody was writing a post about how important it was to support new authors. Somebody was posting a new chapter of a novel about a magic academy where the heroine accidentally turns out to be the only person capable of saving the world, preferably while trapped in a love triangle.
The world of reading existed.
It simply had not looked at him.
It was a peculiar form of invisibility.
Not a desert with nobody in it.
A packed railway station where you stand holding a sign saying "Help," while people walk around you so expertly you might as well be part of the architecture.
He opened the platform with the AI badge.
That was the one where his cover looked as though someone had stuck a warning on it:
"Caution! May contain traces of modernity."
Views: zero.
But the blog section was boiling with life.
The first post to catch his eye was titled:
"How to Clean AI Content Out of Your Feed"
He froze.
It was like finding a notice on the doors of a church: "Sinners in synthetic sandals not admitted."
The author wrote that genuine bibliophiles were finding it increasingly difficult to read true literature because their feeds were being cluttered with works that had AI covers. Covers, specifically.
Not texts. Not ideas. Not plots. Not language. Covers.
She strongly recommended going into settings and turning on a filter that hid works whose authors had honestly disclosed the use of AI.
Honestly disclosed it.
So the system punished not deception.
Honesty.
If you used AI and kept quiet, perhaps you could still pass among humans like a wolf in a fake moustache.
If you told the truth, you got the badge, the filter, segregation and a place in the digital leper colony, where your texts might be read only by similarly unclean people, a few bots and an accidental student who had forgotten to switch off all categories before bed.
He read the comments under the post.
There were many.
People were grateful.
They wrote that at last they would be able to read "normal works." They wrote that AI covers ruined the atmosphere. They wrote that an author incapable of hiring an artist did not deserve attention. They wrote that this was a question of respect for the reader. They wrote that there should also be a separate badge for texts that might have been written with AI assistance.
Might have been.
He liked that most of all.
Might have been.
By that principle, you could label the whole of human existence.
"This person might have lied."
"This woman might not have finished reading the instructions."
"This man might not have washed his hands."
"This writer might have used GPT."
"This critic might have been born without taste but with access to a keyboard."
And, funniest of all, the very same critic might have used GPT too.
Not merely might. Almost certainly would.
Because why should an honest person burn out his eyes in front of a monitor reading someone else's forty thousand characters about pain, meaning, the collapse of civilisation and a faceless man when he could spend the time much more profitably: play tennis, drink coffee, sit on a terrace, look at trees and for a few minutes feel not like a function with Wi-Fi but a mammal entitled to sunlight.
You could understand the critic too.
He was human as well.
Possibly.
His eyes were not government property either. He had a back. A family. Tennis at seven. An inner need not to read everything he would later be expected to hold an authoritative opinion about.
So he could upload the work to GPT and ask:
"Write a thoughtful but restrained review. Mention the strong authorial voice, problems with pacing, excessive essayism and potential for revision. Don't be too harsh, but make sure the author understands his place in the food chain."
And GPT would write it.
Quickly.
Neatly.
With paragraphs.
Even with that delicate falseness people for some reason call a professional tone.
Then the critic would sign it with his own name, change two adjectives, add "in my opinion" in a place where there had been no opinion, and go off to play tennis with the clean conscience of a man who had contributed to the literary process.
Because the important thing was not WHO wrote the review.
The important thing was WHO it was ABOUT.
And WHAT KIND it was.
Especially if the "who" was you.
Then every principle immediately became as flexible as a yogi at a corporate retreat.
If AI helped an author make a cover—disgrace, decline, desecration of art, a spit in the face of true literature.
If AI helped a critic avoid reading a work but write a verdict on it—well, it's only a tool. One has to keep up with the times. You can't be a reactionary. The world is changing. The important thing is responsible use of technology.
Responsible use of technology, he had noticed, always meant one very simple thing:
I may. You may not.
An author with GPT is a fraud.
A critic with GPT is an efficient professional.
A reader with GPT is a busy person who asked for a summary.
A platform with GPT is an innovative service.
A detector with GPT is a defender of humanity.
And he, with his cover, was a suspicious character who needed to be moved into a separate filter so bibliophiles would not be traumatised by the mere fact of his existence.
That was the real magic of modernity.
Not artificial intelligence.
The human ability to call the same sin by different names depending on whose hands it was in.
And it was not only critics.
The funniest part was that all these people probably used AI every day.
Asked it to write an email.
Put together a CV.
Check mistakes.
Generate a gift idea.
Explain what a blood test meant even though the doctor had asked them not to Google it.
Phrase a beautiful post about the importance of remaining human.
But a cover—no.
The cover was civilisation's final frontier.
The Rubicon.
Beyond it lay the horde.
He sat there thinking that humanity had a remarkable talent: first invent a useful tool, then collectively start using it, then pretend only the morally corrupt used it, and finally sell courses in the ethical use of the thing everyone was already doing on the toilet with their phone.
AI had become the new garlic for digital vampires.
Everyone sniffed everyone else.
— Are you definitely human?
— Show me your drafts.
— Where's the process?
— Why is your cover good if you're an unknown author?
— Why are your paragraphs so even?
— Why isn't your metaphor crooked?
— Why didn't you make a mistake where a real human being should have fallen face-first into the syntax?
Soon, perhaps, people would have to start deliberately adding a few unnecessary commas, one stupid sentence and half a page of landscape description so detectors and bibliophiles could relax:
"Yes, definitely human. A machine could never compare autumn leaves to an accountant's emotional state after stocktaking quite this badly."
He opened his cover again.
The badge really did ruin everything.
It did not inform.
It shamed.
As though the cover had been called to the front of the classroom and told:
— Now explain to the class why you're like this.
He imagined a reader scrolling the feed, seeing the dark figure against the city, pausing for a second, then noticing the "created by AI" badge and making the same face people make in a supermarket when they see cheese reduced by seventy per cent with a use-by date of "today, if you're brave."
No, he thought.
This would not do.
Otherwise his opus was doomed to languish in lists of AI literature.
He liked the word "languish." It had something old-fashioned about it. Minor civil servants could languish. Forgotten poets could languish. Houseplants in government offices could languish. So could people who had failed to adapt to an age in which even loneliness required a visual identity.
He decided to change the cover.
Not because he was ashamed of AI.
No.
He was not ashamed of the tool.
He was ashamed of the badge.
It was an important distinction that the world, naturally, would never notice, because the world had long confused morality with interface design.
He would have to make the cover himself.
He opened a simple online editor.
White canvas.
Upload image.
Add text.
Choose background.
Templates.
Premium templates.
Premium-premium templates.
Free elements that looked as though they had been drawn by people with a deep hatred of the concept of free.
He tried a black background.
On the black background, in white letters, he wrote:
WHY
It looked like the title of an anti-religious pamphlet handed out outside metro stations by people with very tired eyes.
He added a grey line.
It got worse.
Added a human silhouette.
Now it looked as though somebody had lost a road sign in a depression.
Changed the typeface.
Now the cover resembled a poster for an amateur theatre staging a drama about the loneliness of a supermarket cashier.
He tried adding a city.
The city looked like the title slide of a webinar called "How to Make Money in Property with No Starting Capital."
Removed the city.
Put the line back.
Removed the line.
Added a blot.
The blot was honest. At least it did not pretend to be art. But a cover with a blot and the title "Why" might lead a reader to suspect that what lay inside was either avant-garde literature or instructions for removing mould.
He worked for forty minutes.
In that time, the originally beautiful cover AI had produced in twenty seconds had, in his own hands, turned into proof that humanity had invented the profession of designer for good reason.
He leaned back in his chair.
Looked at the result.
Then said quietly:
— Lord, forgive me this visual sin.
On the screen was a cover that did not need to be labelled as AI-generated.
That much was obvious.
No AI would humiliate itself like that.
And that was its only advantage.
It was human.
In the worst sense of the word.
Crooked, dry, uncertain, badly balanced, with the inner aura of a person who has come to an official reception wearing a jacket that fits almost normally provided he does not move, breathe or possess shoulders.
He uploaded the new cover.
The platform accepted it without a badge.
No verification.
No warning.
No "caution: this design may traumatise people with taste."
It simply accepted it.
That was funny too.
So the system had no problem with bad.
The system had a problem with suspiciously good.
If you made something beautiful quickly, you were guilty.
If you made something bad slowly, you were human.
Perhaps, he thought, that was the central rule of the new age.
Humanity was no longer proved by depth.
But by flaws.
Want people to believe you? Be a little crooked.
Want to be taken for alive? Leave a stain, a mistake, a stupidity, a weakness, a bad typeface and a sentence an editor should have killed in infancy.
Perfection had become suspicious.
Incompetence was a passport to citizenship in humanity.
He closed the editor.
Then opened the statistics again.
Nothing had changed.
Three views.
Seven likes.
Seven empty comments.
Three platforms on zero.
The world had not rushed to read him after the cover change.
The world behaved as though it had more important things to do. And, to be fair, it did. Somewhere the news was burning. Somewhere people were buying bread. Somewhere somebody was dying. Somewhere somebody was being born. Somewhere a girl was crying over a boy, the boy over a loan, the loan over its interest—and the interest was not crying, because interest rates had the strongest nervous system in the world.
He sat in his fortress and stared at the numbers.
He felt ashamed.
Not of the text.
Not of the cover.
Not of the pseudonym.
Of the expectation.
Of the fact that somewhere inside he really had been waiting for the world to stop, turn its head and say:
— Oh, finally. We've been waiting for you.
The world had not been waiting.
The world waits for very few people at all. At most, for a courier, a salary and test results. And even then without much tenderness.
He realised that his desire to be heard was not quite as pure as he wanted to believe.
Yes, he wanted to say something important.
But he also wanted somebody to acknowledge that the nights at the keyboard had not been wasted. That he had not kept silent by day for nothing. That he had not hidden behind a pseudonym for nothing. That he had not gathered his skeletons, counted every little bone and tried to turn guilt into action rather than self-flagellation for nothing.
He wanted a witness.
But perhaps he wanted a little applause too.
Not an ovation. No. He was not that shameless.
Small applause.
Modest.
Domestic.
The sort nobody would notice he was waiting for.
He smiled.
Now that sounded more like the truth.
A person can tell himself as often as he likes that all he wants is to convey meaning. Somewhere in a dark corner of the soul there will still be a small, plump performer in a glittering waistcoat asking:
— Will there be flowers?
He did not like that performer.
But he had to admit that the fellow was part of him too.
He opened his text.
"Why."
The first sentence seemed too solemn.
The second too clever.
The third sounded as though he had genuinely decided to speak to humanity while humanity was standing in a queue for shawarma without the right change.
He changed nothing.
Not today, at least.
Instead, he opened a blank document and wrote:
"People don't read because they've become stupid. They're simply tired of people who keep turning up to save them."
He looked at the sentence.
Yes.
That could be the beginning of something more honest.
Because perhaps the problem was not only the world.
Perhaps the world truly did not want to listen.
But perhaps he himself had not yet learned how to speak in a way that did not make people want to switch him off immediately.
It was an unpleasant thought.
Like all useful thoughts.
He added:
"A prophet who doesn't know how to be human very quickly turns into nothing more than a bore with a bad cover."
He liked that.
He laughed.
Quietly.
Because the fortress was asleep.
And he sat in the kitchen among five open tabs, three views, seven reciprocal likes, three bots, one ruined cover, one even worse cover and one extremely unpleasant suspicion:
having something to say is not enough to make people hear you.
You also have to survive the moment when nobody answers.
And not turn, afterwards, into an offended genius with access to a keyboard.
He saved the new file.
Named it:
"After Zero."
Then closed all the tabs.
And for the first time in a week, he did not check the statistics before going to bed.
It was not a victory.
But perhaps this was what a small act of repentance looked like:
not demanding an immediate answer from the world simply because you had finally found the courage to ask it a question.

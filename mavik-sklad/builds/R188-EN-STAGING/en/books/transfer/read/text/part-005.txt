The booking was supposed to be simple.

A quick run to Rzeszów in Poland. Only about a hundred and fifty kilometres from the border.

Get there. Collect the client. Come back.

What could possibly go wrong?

While I was crossing the border, the client was chatting actively with me in the messenger and constantly checking when I would be able to collect her.

First the meeting was set for seven in the evening.

Then nine.

Then eleven.

And, pleasantly enough, this time I was not the one running late.

One force majeure after another kept appearing on the client’s side, and the meeting time drifted steadily farther into the night.

The moment I crossed the border into Poland, a new message arrived: pickup moved to seven the next morning.

But there was a nuance.

The client very much wanted me specifically to drive her, so she was willing to pay for a hotel and add extra for the waiting time.

I called my wife. Explained the situation.

Something curious appeared in her voice. A slight suspicion. Possibly even jealousy.

Because the situation really did sound interesting: an unknown woman in Poland moves our meeting into the night, then into the morning, pays for my hotel and insists that I be the one to collect her.

“And naturally, you absolutely have to go to this particular woman?” my wife asked.

“It’s work.”

“Of course. Premium class.”

That was the end of my arguments.

I found a hotel through Booking.com, checked in and tried to get some sleep.

During the night another message arrived: pickup moved to 9:36 in the morning. But this time, almost one hundred per cent certain.

It was the “9:36” that finally convinced me this was a serious person.

Not nine thirty. Not ten to ten.

Nine thirty-six.

By then I was thoroughly intrigued.

Closer to the meeting time, the client sent me her carriage number and described what she would be wearing.

I parked the Mercedes in the station’s underground car park, went up to the platform and waited.

The train arrived on time. I spotted my passenger in the crowd almost immediately. She, meanwhile, stood there looking around in confusion.

I quietly came up behind her and whispered in her ear:

“Looking for the beard?”

She did not even flinch.

“I am!”

“Ha. I cut it off.”

Pause.

“At a client’s request.”

“Seriously?!”

“Not all of it. Two thirds. The rest is a strategic reserve.”

That was how we met.

We loaded her things and set off.

My passenger was an attractive American of Ukrainian origin. She was thirty-two, though I would not have guessed more than twenty-five. She had a twelve-year-old son. And she was a barber.

So that was where the dog was buried.

She had chosen me from among the drivers because of my profile photo.

Because of the beard.

And I had gone and cut it off.

A marketing catastrophe.

“A barber would never have allowed you to do that,” she said.

“That’s why I’m a writer and not a barber.”

She looked at me again.

“Actually, you really do look more like a writer than a driver.”

I sighed.

“You’re already the second person this week. I’m beginning to suspect a conspiracy.”

At that point, the repetition stopped surprising me and became a professional diagnosis.

Her profile name was Lyubimka. I never did remember her real name afterwards. Lyubimka suited her much better.

A bright, open person. She worked as a barber, supported her child on her own, and somehow still managed to travel.

On the road I asked her about life in the United States: small business, clients, taxes, everyday life, attitudes toward Ukrainians. In her experience, it was very different from what she had encountered in Poland.

Lyubimka turned out to be a veteran of our company. This was already her seventh trip.

“The prices are fine, the cars are fine, the drivers are fine too,” she summed up. “That’s why I keep coming back.”

“Especially to the ones who no longer have beards?”

“You still have to earn that privilege.”

I was pleased. I had only recently entered this business, but I was already beginning to feel that I had joined a service where more was expected of the driver than simply moving a body from point A to point B.

Lyubimka also assured me that at the border she was a genuine lucky charm.

“I’ve crossed six times. Forty-five minutes maximum.”

“Excellent. So today I’ll finally see what a normal border looks like.”

We checked the drivers’ groups, chose the checkpoint with the lightest traffic and headed there.

The lucky charm did not operate at full power that day.

Three hours.

Which, after some of my previous adventures, could almost be considered a success.

Even this time, though, there were enough moments when the behaviour of border staff — on both the Polish and Ukrainian sides — made you lower your eyes in embarrassment.

Not because you had done anything wrong. Because you were standing nearby and understood: this is what your country, or the country people call your ally, looks like right now to someone who simply wants to cross a border.

We got through and drove toward Lviv.

On the road we talked about America, Ukraine, work, children, people. About why in one country you feel like a guest, while in another you feel like an unwanted petitioner.

In Lviv, a young man was waiting for Lyubimka too. A serviceman. A person currently defending our country.

I took her things out of the boot.

“Grow the beard back,” she ordered as we said goodbye.

“That’s already the second client request concerning my appearance. I’ll have to add a field to the CRM.”

She laughed and walked toward her soldier.

I remained beside the car again with that strange feeling I was beginning to recognise.

Another completely random person had travelled a few hundred kilometres in the back seat — and stayed somewhere inside me.

I was not yet trying to explain to myself why I needed all these meetings.

I simply stacked them in my memory like other people’s suitcases in the boot: carefully, so nothing would get lost.

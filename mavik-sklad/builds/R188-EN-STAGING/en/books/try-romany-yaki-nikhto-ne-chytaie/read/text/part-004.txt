CHAPTER 4. THE SYNOPSIS, OR HOW TO BURY A NOVEL IN THREE PAGES

The next morning Artur woke with the feeling of a man who had defeated a small dragon during the night only to discover at dawn that it had merely been the guard at the entrance.
The blurb was finished.
That ought to have been satisfying.
But beside the file called “Blurb_novel_1_not_embarrassing_to_send” there was already another blank document.
It was called:
“Synopsis_novel_1”.
Artur opened it before work, just to see what he was dealing with.
The white page stared back as if it had the authority to deny him a literary future.
Artur opened ChatGPT.
“What is a synopsis and why does a publisher need one?”
ChatGPT replied:
“A synopsis is a concise account of the entire plot of a work, including the ending. It allows an editor or publisher to quickly assess structure, conflict, the logic of events, genre fit and the manuscript’s potential.”
Artur reread it.
Then typed:
“Including the ending?”
“Yes. A synopsis should reveal the ending.”
Artur leaned back in his chair.
— Wonderful. I spent two years building suspense so I could surrender it on page two.
He imagined his final plot twist as a captured hero being marched through an editorial office with his hands in the air.
“Don’t shoot. I’m the plot twist.”
ChatGPT, failing to grasp the tragedy of the moment, continued:
“A synopsis is not promotional copy. Its purpose is not to intrigue but to explain.”
— So the blurb is the first date, — Artur said, — and the synopsis is when, on the first date, you immediately show them your medical records, credit history and messages with your exes.
He had forty minutes before work.
Forty minutes was enough time to eat breakfast, take a shower, answer three emails or make one fatal mistake — try to summarise his own novel.
Artur tried.
“The protagonist was born in…”
Deleted.
Too far back.
“After an event that changed his life…”
Deleted.
An event that changes a life appeared in every second synopsis, every third insurance advert and every first conversation with a therapist.
“The novel begins when…”
Artur stopped.
Formally, the novel began with the first scene. But in truth it began with the hero’s childhood, old fears, other people’s decisions, a random sentence he had heard at seventeen and because of which he later ruined three important relationships.
How was all of that supposed to fit into three pages?
Artur wrote:
“The novel begins long before its first page.”
He looked at it.
— Beautiful. And completely useless to a publisher.
Deleted.
At work he spent the whole day trying to behave like a normal person: answering emails, smiling on calls and pretending the phrase “we need to strengthen the customer journey” was not a crime against the Ukrainian language.
At ten thirty his manager asked him to prepare a short description of a new product.
— One page, — she said. — But it has to have everything: value, benefits, customer pain points, solution, results, emotion, structure, and no fluff.
Artur slowly raised his eyes to her.
— Are you serious?
— Yes. Why?
— Nothing. Life has simply decided to work in the genre of mockery today.
— What?
— I said I’ll do it.
He opened a work document and, in twenty minutes, wrote a description of a product that sold corporate solutions for optimising internal processes.
The copy came out clear, dynamic and even almost honest.
Artur looked at it with disgust.
For somebody else’s product, he could write briefly.
For his own novel — no.
At lunch Denys sat opposite him with a salad that looked as though it had been assembled out of apologies.
— You look gloomy today, — Denys said.
— Writing a synopsis.
— Sounds like a diagnosis.
— Almost. It’s where you have to retell your novel in three pages, including the ending.
Denys chewed a piece of cucumber.
— Makes sense.
— What makes sense?
— The person has to understand what happens in it.
— The person should read the novel.
— The person has a job, children, a mortgage, a boss and TikTok. You seriously overestimate their free time.
Artur put down his fork.
— You’re on the system’s side again?
— I’m on gravity’s side. It’s unpleasant, but if you ignore it, you get hurt.
— Literature should not have to obey gravity.
— Then don’t be surprised when it floats somewhere up high while readers walk around down below and never see it.
Artur wanted to answer, but found nothing.
Denys nodded, satisfied.
— See? Practical people are useful sometimes. We’re like salt. Not a meal on our own, but everything is bland without us.
— You’re more like baking soda.
— Why?
— Because you keep reacting with acid.
Denys laughed.
— All right. Tell me in simple words: what is your novel about?
Artur opened his mouth.
And closed it.
Any writer can talk about his novel for two hours. But ask him to explain it simply and he looks like someone who has forgotten his own name at passport control.
— It’s about choice, — Artur began.
— Boring.
— Thank you.
— I’m helping. Go on.
— About a man trying to remain himself in a world where everyone wants to make him convenient.
— Better. What happens to him?
— He gets caught in a system that…
— Stop. “System” is what an author says when he still hasn’t decided who is to blame.
— Not true.
— True. In real life, specific people, rules, fears, money, stupidity or bad communication are to blame. “The system” is when it’s all of them together and nobody is responsible.
Artur looked at Denys more closely.
— You’re suspiciously useful today.
— I’m always useful. You’re usually just busy with your inner monologue.
— Denys, you’re irritating.
— That’s useful too. Irritation is an ancient engine of literature.
Artur smiled.
— Fine. The hero has a chance to get everything he wants, but to do it he has to become the kind of person he despises.
Denys raised a finger.
— Oh. Now that sounds like a story.
Artur froze.
That really was the core.
Not every subplot. Not every symbol. Not every pain. Not every subtle allusion he loved so much and which, most likely, only he and a very attentive God ever noticed.
The core.
— Say it again, — Denys said.
— What?
— What you just said. Write it down.
— I’ll remember.
— Write it down. Writers always think they’ll remember. Then they walk around looking like people who lost a brilliant sentence beside the supermarket till.
Artur took out his phone and wrote:
“The hero has a chance to get everything he wants, but to do it he has to become the kind of person he despises.”
Denys finished his salad.
— See? A synopsis isn’t the funeral of a novel. It’s an X-ray.
— X-rays aren’t very romantic.
— But they show whether the text has a spine.
Artur looked at him suspiciously.
— Have you secretly taken some kind of editor course?
— No. I just live without metaphysical fog.
— My condolences.
— Likewise.
That evening Artur came home earlier than usual.
He did not go to the gym.
This was a serious decision: in his internal value system, a missed workout was roughly equivalent to a medium-grade moral failure.
He put the kettle on, took from the fridge a piece of chicken his mother would have described as “sad,” and opened the document.
At the top he wrote:
SYNOPSIS
Then he added the sentence from lunch.
It held the text in place the way a nail holds a shelf. Not always elegant, but reliable.
Artur began.
This time he did not try to sound brilliant.
He simply retold the story.
Who the hero was.
What he wanted.
What stood in his way.
What price he had to pay.
Where he went wrong.
Whom he lost.
What he understood.
How it all ended.
The first paragraphs moved like an old lift in a block with no functioning residents’ association.
Then the text started moving.
Once Artur stopped protecting the novel from simplification, the novel revealed its frame.
The frame was strong.
That was reassuring.
There were, admittedly, unnecessary rooms. Corridors that could be explained more briefly. One subplot which, in the synopsis, looked as though it had wandered in from another novel and was too embarrassed to admit it.
Artur marked it in red.
— And who exactly are you? — he asked aloud.
The subplot remained silent.
In the novel itself it had seemed interesting.
In the synopsis — unnecessary.
That was an unpleasant discovery.
The synopsis turned out to be a mirror without filters. In it, the novel could not hide behind mood, a long scene, a beautiful sentence or the author’s affection for a character who “creates atmosphere” but does nothing.
Artur opened a separate file and wrote:
“Maybe remove the subplot with…”
Then stopped.
— No. Not now. I am not letting a synopsis dismantle my life on a weekday evening.
He returned to the main document.
At half past midnight the first version was finished.
It was six pages long.
Artur looked at the requirements.
“2–3 pages.”
He slowly exhaled.
— Of course. Because if an author is suffering, that doesn’t mean he’s suffering compactly enough.
He asked ChatGPT:
“Cut this synopsis to three pages, but preserve the logic, causal links, ending and central drama.”
ChatGPT replied:
“Of course.”
A minute later it produced a version in which everything was technically still there, yet Artur felt as though his novel had undergone surgery without anaesthetic.
— No, — he said. — You didn’t shorten it. You amputated it.
He began restoring things.
Put one important motif back.
Removed two secondary ones.
Replaced an abstract explanation with a concrete action.
Kept the ending.
The ending hurt most.
He wanted to write:
“What happens next is best discovered by reading the novel.”
But by now he knew: that was not allowed.
A synopsis is not flirting.
A synopsis is an interrogation.
And if the interrogator asks where the body of the final plot twist is, you should not answer: “It will find you itself on page 384.”
At two in the morning, the synopsis fit into three pages.
Not two.
Three.
Artur decided this was a compromise between market requirements and his human dignity.
He saved the file:
“Synopsis_novel_1_three_pages”
Then renamed it:
“Synopsis_novel_1_publisher_version”
Then once more:
“Synopsis_novel_1_no_tears”
The last title was untrue but psychologically necessary.
He closed the laptop and went to the kitchen.
The tea in the cup had gone cold long ago.
The chicken in the fridge remained sad.
Artur poured the cold tea down the sink.
His phone flashed with a message from Denys:
“Well, Tolstoy? Buried the novel?”
Artur smiled and replied:
“No. Took an X-ray. Spine intact.”
Denys replied almost immediately:
“Then bar on Saturday. A spine needs taking out for a walk sometimes.”
Artur looked at the message.
Then at the laptop.
The blurb was done.
The synopsis was done.
That did not cancel the unfairness or guarantee anything.
But it was no longer resentment towards the world.
It was action.
He created a new folder and called it:
“Publisher_package”
Dragged the blurb into it.
Then the synopsis.
Thought for a moment and created one more blank document:
“Letter_to_publisher”
The cursor blinked on the first line.
Artur smiled.
— Well then, — he said. — If we’re already bidding for the contract, I may as well show up in a jacket.
And typed the first word:
“Hello.”

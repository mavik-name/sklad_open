CHAPTER 2. THE DIGITAL ORACLE

Outside the window, evening traffic crawled past. People were coming home from work, talking on their phones, carrying bags, hurrying towards the metro, their children, their lovers, their series, their short videos — towards a life that had no habit of waiting for someone to become a writer.
At the next table, two women were filming a croissant.
— One more time, — said one. — You take a bite and I’ll say, “This is the most tender thing that’s happened to me all week.”
— But I’ve already bitten it.
— Doesn’t matter. We’ll pretend it’s the first time.
Artur looked at his laptop.
The world had not become stupid. That would have been far too simple and far too flattering to his vanity. The world had become fast. And a fast world does not hate depth — it simply does not always have time to realise that what it is looking at is not a puddle.
He opened ChatGPT.
For a few seconds he stared at the empty input field. It felt strange: as if he had come not to a program but to a cold digital judge who did not go out for coffee, had no friends, was not afraid of damaging a relationship and would not say “atmospheric” without having read the text.
Artur wrote:
“Assess my novel as honestly as possible. I don’t need encouragement. I need the truth.”
He attached the file.
Then the second.
Then the third.
And pressed Enter.
A moving cursor appeared on the screen.
Artur waited.
The cursor blinked for so long that he managed to pass through every stage an author goes through before being judged: confidence, modesty, panic, and an inner readiness to declare all artificial intelligence an overrated autocomplete.
He even imagined the reply:
“Unfortunately, your texts display signs of amateur literature with elements of stubbornness.”
Or worse:
“I recommend that the author try writing short posts about healthy eating.”
Artur tightened his grip on the coffee cup.
— Just try writing ‘atmospheric’, — he said quietly.
The answer appeared suddenly.
“I have analysed the first novel according to the main criteria: plot, structure, characters, style, dialogue, pacing, originality, emotional impact, commercial potential and readiness for submission to a publisher.”
A table followed.
Plot — 8.7/10.
Structure — 8.2/10.
Characters — 8.8/10.
Style — 8.6/10.
Dialogue — 8.4/10.
Pacing — 7.6/10.
Originality — 8.3/10.
Emotional impact — 8.9/10.
Commercial potential — 7.4/10.
Readiness for submission — 7.8/10.
Artur froze.
Then slowly leaned closer to the screen, as if afraid the numbers might run away.
They were not tens.
Not a digital blessing from heaven.
Not “you are a genius and humanity must apologise immediately.”
But they were scores after which he could no longer simply tell himself, “Maybe I imagined the whole thing.”
— Say that again, — he said.
ChatGPT, of course, could not hear his tone, but Artur was already typing:
“Are you serious?”
The answer came almost instantly:
“Yes. The text has clear literary merit, a strong central conflict, lively prose and good potential for professional submission. At the same time, it needs editorial work: in places the pacing should be tightened, excessive description reduced, the genre niche defined more clearly, and a strong submission package prepared for publishers.”
Artur read it twice.
The first time as a man who had just received not a victory, but serious permission to stop belittling himself.
The second as a man who had discovered a list of jobs printed in small type at the bottom of that permission.
— So I’m not insane, — he said. — But I’m not ready to walk on water either.
ChatGPT did not disagree.
It was unpleasantly grown-up.
The second novel received similar scores: stronger characters and dialogue, but weaker pacing in the middle.
The third was the boldest in concept, but carried the highest commercial risk.
Artur felt mildly offended on behalf of the third novel, although ten minutes earlier he would have accepted even an honest seven provided it did not come with the words “the author should read more.”
The women in the café finally filmed the croissant from the right angle. Unlike his texts, the croissant already had an audience, dramaturgy and clear positioning.
Artur looked at the screen.
— So I’m a writer.
He typed it into the chat:
“So I can call myself a writer?”
ChatGPT replied:
“Yes. If you have created completed works of fiction with coherent structure, strong characters and a recognisable style, you can call yourself a writer. But moving from the fact of having written to professional realisation requires work on editing, submissions, communication with publishers and author visibility.”
— See, — Artur said, — even for a machine, talent comes before a ‘but’.
He typed:
“What do I do next?”
This time the answer was not a blessing.
It was a list.
Blurb.
Synopsis.
Author biography.
Query letter.
Selection of excerpts.
List of relevant publishers.
Checking each publisher’s requirements.
Submission tracker.
Competitions.
Author presence.
Artur said nothing.
Then slowly typed:
“I’ve already written three novels.”
ChatGPT replied:
“That is the foundation. Now you need to prepare materials for professional communication with the market.”
Artur reread the phrase “professional communication with the market” and felt something inside him fall gently but decisively down a flight of stairs.
— I see, — he said. — I came for literary recognition and got registered for a tender.
He typed:
“Can’t I just send the novel?”
The answer was merciless in its politeness:
“You can, but it reduces your chances. An editor or publisher often does not have time to read a full manuscript without first understanding its genre, plot, audience and potential.”
— Doesn’t have time, — Artur repeated.
At the next table, a young man was looking at his phone and laughing at a video in which someone tried to open a bottle of kefir and lost.
Artur turned back to the laptop.
— The world has time for kefir but no time for a novel.
ChatGPT was silent.
Perhaps that was the highest form of wisdom: not arguing when a person had already formulated civilisation’s verdict for himself.
Artur asked why the text alone was not enough for a publisher.
The reply was calm, logical and so painfully adult that he did not want to accept it.
“A publisher assesses not only literary quality but also investment risk. A book requires editing, proofreading, layout, design, printing, logistics, promotion and space in the information field. That is why even a strong text has to be presented clearly.”
Artur looked out of the window.
Kyiv glowed with evening windows. Behind them people ate, argued, put children to bed, scrolled through their phones, bought things they did not need, watched the news and postponed important things until later.
Publishers were not monsters.
That was almost insulting.
Monsters are easier. You can face a monster with a sword, pathos and a speech about eternity. Against a spreadsheet of costs, print runs, warehouse space and competition from TikTok, a sword looks not heroic but ridiculous.
The world was not telling him, “You’re bad.”
The world was telling him, “Prove that you are worth spending resources on.”
And that was what irritated him most.
Artur wanted to be judged by the text.
The world wanted the text to arrive in a clean shirt, carrying its documents and a short presentation of itself as a promising product.
He typed:
“That’s unfair.”
ChatGPT replied:
“That feeling is understandable. But publishing is not only a cultural process; it is also an economic one.”
Artur narrowed his eyes.
— That was a very polite way of saying, “Get over it.”
He wrote:
“What if I don’t want to become a product?”
The answer:
“You do not have to become a product. But it is worth learning to present your work in a way that allows it to be noticed by people who receive many similar approaches every day.”
The sentence caught him.
Not because it was pleasant.
Because there was no betrayal in it.
Maybe the problem was not that his novels had to be “sold.” Maybe the problem was that he had spent all this time carrying them up to locked doors and feeling offended when the doors did not open simply because the cover was beautiful.
Artur felt resistance rise in him.
He wanted to write:
“I did not work for years just so I could now compress my novels into seven hundred characters.”
But the less solemn part of his brain asked:
“What for, then? So they can sit in the ‘LITERATURE’ folder waiting for archaeologists?”
Artur sighed.
— Fine, — he said. — We’ll start with the blurb.
He placed the cursor in the chat field and wrote:
“Help me prepare a blurb for the first novel.”
ChatGPT replied:
“Of course. To produce an effective blurb, we need to identify the genre, the protagonist, the central conflict, the stakes, what makes the work distinctive and its target readership.”
Artur closed his eyes.
— I asked for a blurb and you opened an investigation.
He asked:
“Put it more simply. Like a human being.”
ChatGPT thought for a few seconds and produced:
“We need to understand who the main character is, what he wants, what stands in his way, why the reader should care and what makes this text different from others.”
Artur raised his eyebrows.
— See? You can do it when you’re not wearing the business-development director’s suit.
At that moment his mother called.
The screen said: “Mum”.
Artur looked at the call with a mixture of love and foreboding. His mother had a unique talent for phoning at the exact moment he was trying to become a writer.
— Hi, Mum.
— Have you eaten?
It was not a question. It was a form of maternal supervision at state level.
— Yes.
— What?
Artur looked at the unfinished coffee and the croissant he had bought thinking “I’ll eat it later” and had never touched.
— I ate properly.
— “Properly” isn’t food.
— Mum, I’m working.
— At work?
— No, on the book.
A pause.
— Then that isn’t work.
Artur rubbed his forehead.
— Mum, we’ve been through this.
— I’m not against your books. Write. It’s good. But you’re thirty-three.
— Thank you, I remember.
— When are you going to think about a family?
Artur looked at ChatGPT’s list.
Blurb. Synopsis. Biography. Query letter. Excerpts. Submission tracker. Competitions. Author presence.
— Mum, even my novel doesn’t have a family right now. It has to find a publisher first.
— A novel won’t cook borshch for you.
— If it gets published, maybe it can buy a multicooker.
— Don’t joke. I’m serious.
— So am I. My life is just at the synopsis stage right now.
— What?
— Nothing.
His mother sighed in the way only mothers can: as if the sound contained love, anxiety, reproach and the entire history of Ukrainian womanhood at once.
— You’re a handsome man, athletic, intelligent. Why are you alone?
— Because I write.
— Everyone writes. Married people write too.
— Who?
— I don’t know. But someone definitely does.
Artur smiled.
— Mum, I want a family. I really do. A wife, children.
— How many?
— Three.
— Oh! — life came into her voice. — So you have thought about it.
— I’ve thought about a lot of things.
— Then start with the woman. Children without a woman are a complicated scheme.
— Thank you for the biology consultation.
— I’m just saying. You keep postponing everything. First the book, then the publisher, then success, then life. And life doesn’t like being put in a queue.
Artur fell silent.
His mother sometimes said things that sounded simpler than they really were.
— I know, — he said more quietly.
— You don’t. If you knew, you’d already be doing something.
— I am doing something.
— You’re running. That isn’t the same thing.
He looked out of the window.
Someone outside was crossing the road on amber. Kyiv hurried even where it was supposed to stop.
— Mum, I’ll call you later.
— Eat properly.
— Fine.
— And don’t sit at that computer too long. It makes your eyes red and ruins your character.
— My character is ruined by the literary market.
— I don’t know that one. But if it ruins your character, stop associating with it.
Artur laughed.
— All right, Mum.
He hung up.
ChatGPT’s answer was still glowing on the screen:
“We need to understand who the main character is, what he wants, what stands in his way, why the reader should care and what makes this text different from others.”
Artur reread the sentence.
Then he opened a blank document and typed at the top:
BLURB
Below it was empty.
He stared at the white page and suddenly understood: writing four hundred pages had been difficult.
But explaining them in a way that would make a stranger want to open the first one — that was an entirely different sport.
Artur typed the first sentence:
“This is a novel about a person searching for himself in a world that has lost its moral bearings.”
He reread it.
It sounded as if it had been written by an exhausted cultural-studies lecturer after his third class of the day.
Deleted.
He wrote:
“The protagonist faces a choice that will change everything.”
Deleted.
That could describe any work at all, including an advert for a new mobile-phone tariff.
Artur looked at the cursor.
Then at his phone.
Then back at the cursor.
His mother was right about one thing: life really did not like being put in a queue.
But a dream, if you keep putting it away in a folder, sooner or later starts to smell like an archive.
Artur wrote a new sentence:
“He thought the worst thing was losing himself. But it turned out to be far worse to realise that the world had not even noticed.”
He reread it.
Then again.
Not perfect.
But alive.
Outside the window, the women were already laughing at their finished croissant video. The young man with the kefir was scrolling to the next clip. Kyiv shone, ran, honked and had no intention of slowing down for any author.
Artur looked at the first sentence of the blurb and suddenly understood: he would not write any more of it in the café.
There was too much other people’s noise here.
And what he needed now was not to hide from the world but, for the first time, to try to explain his own novel to it in a way that would stop the world running away after the first paragraph.
He saved the document.
Closed the laptop.
The croissant remained almost untouched.
On the way home Artur thought neither about ChatGPT’s scores, nor about publishers, nor even about his mother’s “life doesn’t like being put in a queue.”
He thought about seven hundred characters.
Seven hundred.
For four hundred pages.
It felt almost insulting.
But not the kind of insult that made him want to slam a door anymore.
More the kind he would have to sit down and answer.

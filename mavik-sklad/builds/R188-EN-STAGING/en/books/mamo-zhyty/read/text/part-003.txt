Chapter 9: A Shadow Over Germany
In Germany, in the sterile corridors of the clinic where every day held both hope and fear, Anna prepared for the most important event of all—the boy's surgery. Things that had once seemed trivial now carried enormous weight: was the blanket warm enough, was the pillow comfortable, was it time for the medication? She tried to stay strong because the boy needed her calm and certainty. The doctors were optimistic. The remission was holding, and that gave all of them strength.
She was sitting in a small waiting room going through paperwork when her phone rang. The number was unfamiliar, but it was Ukrainian. She answered, her heart tightening with the sense that something was wrong. The voice on the other end was male, calm, but carrying the dry official tone of someone delivering bad news. He introduced himself as the commander of the unit where her sister served.
"Ms. Anna, we've received information about your sister..." he began, and every word pierced her like an icy needle. "During a combat mission in the Donetsk sector, the group she was with was ambushed. There was close fighting, and afterwards we lost contact with her. Based on preliminary information, we have reason to believe she may have been captured. We don't have details yet, but we're doing everything possible to locate her."
The world around Anna seemed to tilt. The phone slipped from her trembling fingers and hit the floor.
Captured?
Her sister?
It could not be real. It had to be a nightmare she would wake from any second. Viktoriya's words echoed in her head: "I'll come back. I have to come back. For my son."
A deep, paralyzing shock seized her. Her breath caught, a scream stuck in her throat. How could she tell the boy? How could she tell a child who had just been given a chance to live that his mother, his one true anchor, had disappeared? Should she tell him anything at all now, only hours before such a serious operation? Her mind refused to work, cycling through a million possibilities, none of them right.
Memories flashed before her like frames from an old film.
Viktoriya, young and radiant in a white wedding dress, walking toward the altar while Dmytro, her future husband, looked at her with such love that he seemed to light the whole room. Anna had stood beside her as bridesmaid, seeing the way they held each other's hands, promising to stay together through grief and joy. It had been one of the happiest days of their lives, full of laughter, music, and an endless sense of possibility.
Then another memory: the maternity hospital. Dmytro and Anna standing beneath the windows, shouting for Viktoriya to show them the baby. With his endless energy and need to make everything special, Dmytro had stolen an old wooden ladder from a nearby construction site so he could climb higher and pass Viktoriya a huge bouquet of her favorite white roses. Viktoriya had laughed, holding her tiny son in her arms, while Dmytro beamed from below and waved to them, shouting: "My girls! The best girls in the world!"
The boy had been their miracle. Their joy. Their shared dream.
Those memories—so bright and painful at once—tore Anna apart. Her sister, the only close family she had left, the woman who had already survived so much loss, was trapped somewhere now. And the boy... he was so fragile, so vulnerable.
Anna picked the phone up from the floor, hands still shaking. She had to pull herself together. For her sister. For the boy. She was the only one who could protect him now.
She looked at the clock. Only a few hours remained before surgery.
She had to make a decision.
And just like Viktoriya's decision to go to war, it felt like the hardest decision of her life.

Chapter 10: The First Interrogation
The darkness that had swallowed Viktoriya inside the "motolyha" did not release her at once.
She regained consciousness in a small damp room where the only source of light was a dim bulb hanging from the ceiling. Her head hurt unbearably, a blunt pulse beating in her temple. Nausea climbed into her throat, and every movement brought vertigo.
Her hands were bound behind her to a wooden chair bolted firmly to the floor. The air was heavy with dampness and fear.
Across from her sat a man in military uniform wearing the rank insignia of an FSB major. His face was calm, almost indifferent, but cold predatory curiosity lived in his eyes. Beside him stood another man, broad-shouldered, empty-eyed, ready to carry out any order.
"Well, sweetheart?" the major began in Russian, his voice velvet-soft, almost intimate. "Tell us about yourself. Who are you? Where are you from? What were you doing at the front?"
Viktoriya said nothing. She only looked at him with contempt.
Inside, one thought screamed over and over: Don't give them anything. Don't break.
She knew that any word beyond what was required could be used against her. She thought of her son, of his promise to live, and of the promise she had made him—to fight to the end. She had no right to fail either of them.
"We're not animals, girl," the major continued, spreading his hands as if trying to reason with her. "We understand you're not here by choice. They used you. Lied to you. But you can still fix everything. We're offering you the chance to become the new Savchenko. A media figure who tells the world the truth. The truth about how they abandoned you. How they used you. You'll be safe. You'll have money. We'll show the whole world our humanity."
He watched her with manufactured sympathy, waiting for a reaction.
But Viktoriya only repeated the words of her oath to herself. Her son's words. She would not become a tool of enemy propaganda. Rage filled her toward these people who had destroyed her life and taken those she loved.
"Viktoriya Petrivna Kovalenko," she said clearly, looking straight into his eyes. "Rank: soldier. Service number: AB123456."
Each word left her like a steel projectile.
The major sighed, his expression darkening slightly.
"A pity, Viktoriya. I tried to be humane. But there's no other way with Banderites."
He nodded toward the larger man.
"Petrov. The phone."
Without a trace of emotion, Petrov stepped toward her. He yanked roughly at her uniform tunic, tearing the fabric across her chest. Her breasts were exposed, and heat flooded her face with shame and fury. She tried to wrench herself free, but the restraints were too tight.
Petrov took out an old field telephone, its casing chipped and worn. He unwound two bare wire ends and, without looking her in the eyes, fastened them to her nipples.
Viktoriya clenched her teeth, determined not to make a sound. Her body trembled, but she forced her mind onto her son—his laughter, his wish to live.
I will never break, Maryanchyk, she told him silently.
"Last chance, Viktoriya," the major said, his voice cold now, openly cruel. "Agree, and nothing happens. Or..."
She raised her head, hatred burning in her eyes. She looked at his self-satisfied face, gathered the last of her strength, and spat directly into his eye.
The major cried out and recoiled.
Petrov, his expression unchanged, turned the crank of the field telephone.
A sharp, unbearable current tore through her body as if a thousand needles had struck at once. But even that pain almost disappeared behind something greater: the desperate struggle to keep her dignity, to keep the last part of herself free.
Through the pain she could still hear her son's voice in memory. His laughter. Feel the warmth of him.
It gave her strength.
Then the room vanished and the world fell into deep, silent darkness.

Chapter 11: The Burden of Silence
The days after surgery moved slowly, thick as syrup. Maryanchyk lay in the hospital room, his small body threaded through with wires and IV lines. He was pale and weak, but alive.
The surgery had gone well. The tumor had been removed, and the doctors cautiously spoke of "clean margins" and "encouraging prognosis." Every breath he took, every barely visible movement, felt to Anna like proof of a miracle.
She sat beside his bed holding his fragile hand, relief and unbearable pain filling her at the same time.
The pain came from the news about her sister.
The commander's call—Viktoriya missing, probably captured—had crashed over her like ice water. Again and again the phrases repeated in her mind: lost contact; suspected captivity; doing everything possible.
But what did "everything possible" mean when Anna was thousands of kilometers away in another country with no influence over anything? She felt absolutely powerless, cut off from information and from any real chance to help.
The worst question was whether she should tell Maryanchyk at all.
He had just passed through hell. His small body was fighting for life. And now was his mind supposed to fight the thought that his mother, his only true support, might never return?
Anna watched his sleeping face, the long eyelashes trembling slightly, and knew she could not inflict that pain on him.
Not now.
Not while his health still hung by a thread.
She decided to keep silent.
The weight of that silence settled on her like a stone, but she was willing to carry it for him.
News from Ukraine came in fragments, and every fragment felt like a knife.
Viktoriya's unit officially listed her as "missing in action, suspected captured." Then came word that the enemy had returned the bodies of the soldiers who had been with her in the vehicle that terrible day.
It did not answer the question of her sister's fate. It only deepened the fear.
If the others were dead, what had happened to Viktoriya?
Was she alive?
Was she suffering?
Despair kept trying to take hold of Anna, but she could not afford to fall apart.
She was here in Germany with the boy.
And now another task stood in front of her, one no less important: rehabilitation.
The doctors explained that surgery was only the first step. Ahead were months, perhaps years, of intensive work to restore functions damaged by the disease and treatment. Physiotherapy. Speech therapy. Neuropsychology.
All of it demanded her complete attention, endless patience, and, most of all, money.
She remembered Viktoriya's words: "You'll be his mother there while I'm here earning money for his future."
Now Anna had to become not only a mother to him, but his only support, the one person from whom he could draw strength.
She had to find the courage to carry that burden and fight for her nephew's future despite her own fear and the uncertainty surrounding her sister.
Every day brought another challenge.
She would meet it.
For her nephew.
For her sister.

Chapter 12: A New Home, New Responsibilities
The burden of silence about her sister pressed heavily on Anna, but she knew the most important thing now was the boy.
His rehabilitation required not only her time and patience but considerable money. She could not sit and wait.
Fortunately, through volunteers helping Ukrainians in Germany, she heard about the Schwarz family.
The Schwarzes—an elderly, fairly wealthy, childless couple—were looking for a household manager. Their large home required constant care, and they could no longer handle everything themselves.
For Anna, it was more than a job. It was rescue.
She understood immediately that this could give her the means to pay for the boy's rehabilitation despite not knowing what had happened to her sister.
Her first meeting with Mr. and Mrs. Schwarz went well. They were kind, understanding people who treated her situation with respect. Anna told them honestly about her nephew, his illness, and the fact that she would need to devote a great deal of time to him.
To her surprise, they accepted that without complaint. Perhaps being childless themselves made them more sensitive to someone else's grief.
The job seemed to offer only advantages.
Along with a decent salary, she would receive housing: a separate small apartment on the property. That alone was an enormous help. Food was provided as well, significantly reducing expenses.
Most valuable of all, she was allowed to use one of the owners' cars, which meant she could take Maryanchyk to every procedure and therapy session without difficulty.
A maid, a cook, and a gardener worked under her supervision—experienced employees who knew their jobs well. Anna's responsibilities were not overwhelming. Most involved organization and oversight, which allowed her to build a schedule that still gave her enough time for her nephew's rehabilitation.
Every day was now planned almost minute by minute.
Morning began with a visit to Maryanchyk at the clinic. She spent time with him, helped him through exercises, spoke with doctors and therapists. Then she returned to the Schwarz estate, handled the day's practical matters and coordinated the staff. In the evening she went back to her nephew.
She read him books, sang lullabies, told him stories about their home in Ukraine, trying to keep alive in him that small flame of hope and the desire to live.
Seeing her devotion to the boy, the Schwarz family treated her with growing respect. Sometimes they even offered help of their own and asked how Maryanchyk was doing.
Anna was grateful for that support. In this foreign country, they had become a small island of stability.
Despite every difficulty, despite the pain of not knowing what had happened to her sister, Anna felt she was moving in the right direction.
She was fighting.
Fighting for the boy, for his future, for the day he could run again, laugh again, live a full life.
And that fight gave her enough strength to keep moving, step by step, toward victory.

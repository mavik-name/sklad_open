Chapter 24: Revenge and Truth
Darkness became their ally.
When the last light disappeared below the horizon and the ravine vanished beneath a thick black cover, Serhiy felt new energy return.
His shoulder still throbbed, but the thought of evacuation—of survival, of the promises he had made to people waiting for him—kept him moving.
He checked his men.
Maksym was pale but conscious. The others were exhausted and wounded, but alive.
Sometime around midnight, when the tension had stretched almost to breaking point, a "Marder" infantry fighting vehicle rushed toward the edge of the ravine at high speed, looking to them like an ark arriving in hell.
The engine had been cut before the final approach, and it rolled in almost silently, only the soft grinding of the tracks breaking the night.
The men were ready. Their eyes had adjusted to darkness and their ears were tuned to every sound.
Loading took seconds, a ritual practiced until it no longer needed words.
The wounded were pulled inside carefully but fast. Serhiy cast one last look at the ravine that had almost become their grave and climbed in after them.
The hatch shut with a heavy metallic thud.
An hour later they were at a stabilization point—a place ruled by chaos, but organized chaos.
Doctors and medics moved around the wounded like bees, stopping bleeding, stabilizing patients, preparing the worst cases for evacuation farther to the rear.
Once his own wound had been dressed, Serhiy, furious as a grizzly in rut, headed straight for headquarters.
Rage had distorted his face. Revenge burned in his eyes.
He could not wait.
He could not sleep.
His men were dead, and he needed to know who had killed them from the inside.
At headquarters, everyone already knew.
Word of the failed raid and the betrayal had reached senior command quickly. Maps and papers covered the table. Officers with tight faces spoke in low voices.
Serhiy came through the door looking for anyone who might have had a hand in it.
"Commander!" His voice was rough with anger. "We walked into a trap. Somebody sold us out. My men are dead!"
The brigade commander—the same man who had given them the mission—looked up.
His face was exhausted, but pain and determination sat together in his eyes.
"Serhiy, we know. We're already working on it. Several people are under suspicion. They've been detained. That whole group was supposed to die. Because of you, at least a few survived. What you did out there was extraordinary."
The words cooled Serhiy only slightly.
The need to find whoever had done this was still boiling inside him.
For the next two days he barely slept. He worked with counterintelligence, gave statements, reconstructed the raid, went through every detail again and again.
Pain and loss had sharpened his memory. Every sound. Every movement. Every face came back clearly.
Two days later, they found the mole.
It was a long-planted sleeper agent, a person who had spent years passing classified information to the enemy while operating under cover high inside the command structure.
The traitor's name hit headquarters like thunder from a clear sky.
Many could barely believe it.
Serhiy felt a bitter satisfaction.
Justice, however ugly, had caught up.
The man responsible would answer.
His dead would not simply disappear into another report.
But even that victory could not silence the loss.
Serhiy remembered the faces of the men who had died. Their jokes. Their plans. The futures they had assumed they still possessed.
He remembered Viktoriya—the prisoner he had never met, the woman whose story Anna had entrusted to him, the mother who had promised her son she would come back.
And he remembered Anna herself. Her tears. Her faith.
He had survived.
He had returned.
The price had been too high.
Now that the mole was exposed, he could turn toward the next promise.
Find Viktoriya.
He had given his word.
He meant to keep it.
After the evacuation and the exposure of the traitor, Serhiy spent several days in a military hospital. The wound in his shoulder was serious but not critical. Doctors stabilized him quickly, and even through the pain he was already impatient to return to his unit.
But at that moment his most important mission was not on a battlefield.
It was a phone call.
He knew Anna had been waiting to hear from him after the operation, and the silence forced on him by the nightmare he had just survived must have been unbearable.
He held the phone in his hand, fingers trembling.
Her number.
He had dialed it so many times before, but never like this.
His heart hammered against his ribs as though trying to break free.
Far away in Germany, Anna had just put Maryanchyk to bed.
She sat on the sofa staring at the positive pregnancy test on the coffee table. Thoughts of Serhiy, of what might have happened to him, of a future that had become terrifyingly uncertain, were tearing her apart.
The anxiety had not released her for a moment.
Every few minutes she checked the phone again.
Nothing.
Then it rang.
An unfamiliar Ukrainian number.
Anna jerked, her heart stopping for an instant.
She hesitated.
The last calls from Ukraine had brought only pain.
But something made her answer.
"Hello?" Her voice was barely audible.
"Anya... it's me. Serhiy."
The voice on the other end was hoarse, tired—and alive.
Her legs gave way.
She fell onto the sofa, almost dropping the phone.
"Serhiy? Oh God... you're alive! I... I was so scared. I didn't know what to do with myself!"
Her voice broke into sobs. Tears ran down her face—relief, joy, everything she had been holding in.
"I'm alive, Anya. I'm all right. I'm in hospital, but I'm okay," he said warmly. "I'm sorry I couldn't call earlier. It was... bad. I missed you so much. Both of you."
They talked for a long time, interrupting each other, trying to pour everything that had happened into the call at once.
He told her about the raid, the trap, the men who had died, the mole they had exposed.
Anna told him about Maryanchyk, his progress, the way he still talked about his mother.
Then Serhiy hesitated.
"Anya... there's something else. Something I need to tell you. When I was lying in that ravine... I kept thinking about you. About the boy. And I understood something. I want... I want us to be a family. Together."
Anna went silent.
Her heart was pounding.
Her eyes moved to the test on the table.
This was the moment.
A chance at another life. Another hope.
"Serhiy... I have something to tell you too," she whispered, her voice shaking. "I'm... I'm pregnant. We're going to have a baby."
Silence filled the line.
Long enough for fear to rise inside her.
Maybe she had said it too soon.
Maybe this was not what he had meant.
Then he spoke.
His voice was so rough with emotion she barely recognized it.
"Pregnant? Anya... that's... that's the best news I've ever had. I'm going to be a father. My God... it's a miracle."
His joy was so raw, so complete, that she began crying again.
This time there was no fear in the tears.
Only happiness.
She could hear him laughing and choking back tears at the same time, their voices running over each other.
For a few minutes, despite the war, despite the dead, despite the uncertainty around Viktoriya, a new light entered their lives.
A new hope.
A new life they could imagine building together.

Chapter 25: A Ray of Hope Through the Darkness
The news that he was going to be a father ran through Serhiy's exhausted body like electricity.
It was more than a ray of light in darkness.
It was a beacon.
A direction.
The thought gave him another kind of strength.
Now he was fighting not only for Ukraine, not only for the captured woman he had promised to find, but for the future he and Anna might have together—for the new life already growing inside her.
He had promised to return to her.
Now that promise carried even more weight.
Without wasting time, despite the pain in his shoulder and a wound that had not yet properly closed, Serhiy went to senior command.
Grief for his dead men was still written across his face, but determination burned through it.
He entered the corps commander's office, where the general sat over maps with a tired, concentrated expression.
"Permission to report, General," Serhiy began, standing straight despite the pain. "We've exposed the mole. He's a valuable asset. I ask... no, I insist that we use him in an exchange."
The general, gray-haired with deep lines across his forehead, looked up.
He knew Serhiy was not simply another soldier. He was a man who had gone through hell, brought people back, and helped uncover a serious breach.
"Serhiy, we understand what that traitor is worth. But an exchange is complicated. Who are you proposing in return?"
"Viktoriya Kovalenko. Drone operator. Missing in action, suspected captured. Service number AB123456."
Serhiy's voice was hard as steel, though his heart hammered.
This was the chance.
"She disappeared a year ago during a combat mission. There has been no confirmed information since. She's one of ours. She's a mother who gave everything for her child. And her child needs her."
Doubt crossed the general's face, mixed with respect.
"Serhiy, we don't have reliable information on where she is. We don't even know whether she's alive. The enemy never confirmed holding her. That's the problem. We can't exchange a prisoner this valuable for someone whose status hasn't been confirmed."
"I believe she's alive," Serhiy said.
It sounded less like an argument than an oath.
"She's strong. She'll fight. And that bastard is our best chance to get her back. We can't hand him over for nothing. We need something valuable in return. The life of one of our people is valuable enough. And it tells every soldier we have that we don't abandon our own."
The discussion lasted almost an hour.
Serhiy argued. Refused to let the subject be closed. Returned again and again to the same point.
Eventually, senior command agreed to put Viktoriya's name forward.
It was risky. There was still no confirmation she was alive.
But the services responsible for prisoner exchanges began the quiet, painstaking work of tracing her.
Each day became waiting.
Serhiy slept little and called constantly, trying to learn anything.
He thought about Viktoriya's story as Anna had told it to him. Her courage. Her decision to leave her son in order to finance his chance at life.
He had promised Anna.
He would keep the promise.
Two long weeks passed.
Every day felt like a year.
Hope began to thin under uncertainty.
Serhiy heard reports about small exchanges, but the names meant nothing to him. The enemy resisted almost every negotiation.
He was beginning to lose faith when his phone rang one morning.
The voice was official, but underneath the formality was a note of triumph that made Serhiy stop breathing.
"Serhiy, we have news. Viktoriya Petrivna Kovalenko, service number AB123456, has been located. She's alive. Her inclusion in the exchange has been agreed. The exchange should take place within the next few days."
His knees nearly gave way.
Alive.
She was alive.
She was coming back.
Joy struck so hard he almost shouted. Tears filled his eyes—relief more than anything else.
Anna and the boy.
They had to know.
Far away in Germany, in the small apartment on the Schwarz estate, Anna's phone rang just after she had put Maryanchyk to bed.
She flinched and answered, her heart racing with the strange sense that something had happened.
It was Serhiy.
His voice was hoarse but so full of happiness that she understood before he finished the first sentence.
"Anya... she's alive! Vika is alive. They found her. She's on the exchange list. She's coming home!"
Anna's legs weakened and she dropped onto a chair.
Tears filled her eyes.
Joy.
Relief.
Gratitude.
After a year of not knowing, after so many sleepless nights and prayers, her sister was coming home.
"Maryanchyk, wake up!" she cried, rushing into his room.
She wrapped her arms around him and, through tears, whispered:
"Mom is coming back, sweetheart. Your mom is coming home."
The boy was sleepy and confused at first.
Then he saw the tears on Anna's face and the light in her eyes.
Something in him understood.
"Mom?" he whispered.
There was so much hope in the single word that Anna thought her heart might break from happiness.
The nightmare was ending.
Something else could begin.

Chapter 26: The Meeting at the Border
The Ukraine-Belarus border.
A gray, heavy morning that seemed to mirror the tension in the air.
On opposite sides of the crossing stood two different realities.
On the Ukrainian side, people had gathered in enormous numbers.
Hundreds of families. Thousands of hearts beating with hope, fear, impatience.
Ukrainian flags moved above the crowd. Blue-and-yellow ribbons. Signs carrying the names of the missing. Photographs of sons, daughters, husbands, wives.
In the middle of that human sea, humming like a disturbed hive, stood Anna, Maryanchyk, and Serhiy.
Anna held the boy's hand tightly.
Her heart beat violently and her throat was closed around a hard knot.
Serhiy stood beside them. His face was tense, his gaze fixed on the road.
Maryanchyk did not fully understand everything happening around him, but he felt the electricity in the crowd.
He hugged a small stuffed bear Serhiy had given him and stared toward the road where the bus was supposed to appear.
Then it did.
At first only a speck in the distance.
Then a larger shape.
A white bus.
A bus carrying prisoners home.
The crowd erupted.
Voices rose into chanting and shouts. Joy collided with sobbing.
"Glory to Ukraine!"
"Glory to the Heroes!"
Thousands of voices answered one another until the cry seemed to tear open the gray sky.
The bus rolled slowly onto the Ukrainian side and stopped in the designated zone.
The doors opened.
People began to step down.
One at a time.
They were in terrible condition.
Thin. Exhausted. Hollow-eyed. Dirty. Some could barely stand and had to be supported by medics and volunteers.
Every person leaving the bus carried visible proof of the hell they had survived.
Anna searched every face.
Her heart hurt for all of them, but she was looking for one.
Her sister.
Maryanchyk's hand tightened around hers. His small body trembled.
Serhiy scanned the people coming down, focused and silent.
Then a wasted figure appeared in the bus doorway.
Viktoriya.
She was frighteningly thin. Her clothes hung from her body. Her face was pale, dark shadows lying beneath her eyes.
But those eyes still held the same unbroken will Anna remembered.
Life was still there.
After everything, life.
"Mom!"
Maryanchyk's voice rose above the entire crowd.
He tore his hand free and ran.
"Vika!" Anna screamed, her voice breaking into a sob as she ran after him.
Viktoriya stopped when she saw them.
Her eyes widened.
Then filled with tears.
She opened her arms.
Maryanchyk flew into them like a small bird.
Viktoriya held him with everything she had left, feeling his warmth, his smell, the body she had imagined for a year while trying to survive.
She kissed his hair, his face, whispered his name again and again.
Anna reached them and wrapped both of them in her arms.
Three bodies closed together around a year of pain, absence, fear—and an impossible amount of love.
Their tears mixed with joy, relief, and everything that could not be repaired even by a homecoming.
Serhiy stepped closer, his own eyes wet.
Viktoriya noticed him and eased back slightly without letting go of her son or sister.
She looked at him, then at Anna.
"Vika," Anna said, her voice shaking but full of pride. "This is Serhiy. He's the one who helped find you. He's a scout. He brought you back to us."
Viktoriya looked up at him.
Their eyes met.
Despite exhaustion, there was deep gratitude in hers. Understanding too. Recognition of another person who had crossed his own kind of hell.
She stepped toward him.
Serhiy embraced her carefully so he would not hurt her, but firmly enough that it felt like protection.
It was not the embrace of two strangers anymore.
It was the embrace of two people connected by war, by survival, by the family standing beside them.
Around them the crowd was still chanting "Glory to Ukraine!"
But for Anna, Viktoriya, Maryanchyk, and Serhiy, the moment belonged only to them.
A small private victory.
Love against war.
Hope against despair.
Life against death.
They were home.
Together.

Afterword
The meeting at the border was not only the end of Viktoriya's long and brutal road. It was the beginning of another chapter for all of them.
She came back from hell wasted and exhausted, but not broken.
Her body needed long rehabilitation.
Her mind needed healing.
But Anna, Maryanchyk, and Serhiy were beside her—family, support, light.

The Aftermath of Captivity and Viktoriya's PTSD
The first weeks were especially hard.
Viktoriya often woke from nightmares with her body shaking and terror fixed in her eyes. Sudden loud sounds made her flinch violently. Unfamiliar male voices could trigger panic.
She could not eat meat; even the smell made her nauseous. Sleep remained shallow and fragile.
Anna stayed close.
Her patience and gentleness helped Viktoriya return to the present one small step at a time.
Sometimes they talked for hours.
Sometimes they sat without saying anything.
The presence itself mattered.
With professional help, Viktoriya gradually began working through what captivity had done to her. She learned to trust the world again. Other people. Herself.
Her greatest reason to keep trying was her son.
Maryanchyk, who had waited so long for his mother, hardly left her side now.
His impossible child's faith had come true.
That was the miracle.
He helped his mother heal in the only ways a child could: reading books to her, telling her about German school, new friends, ordinary small victories.
His laughter returned to their life and became better medicine than almost anything else.
He was her anchor in a world she had returned to but did not yet completely understand.
Anna, after carrying so much for so long, could finally breathe a little.
Her sister was home.
Her nephew was healthy.
And inside Anna herself, another future was already growing.
The pregnancy that had first terrified her became a symbol of new life pushing through pain and rubble.
She felt strength returning with gratitude beside it.

The Aftermath of War and Serhiy's PTSD
Serhiy stayed close after keeping his promise.
His physical wounds healed, and a mind that had spent years moving through war found moments of quiet in Anna's love and Maryanchyk's unguarded honesty.
But the war remained inside him too.
Sometimes a loud sound tore him awake.
His dreams carried the faces of dead comrades.
He could sit for hours without speaking, buried somewhere inside his own thoughts, his eyes fixed beyond the walls in front of him.
Anna understood enough not to force him back before he was ready.
She stayed nearby, gave him space, offered support without demanding performance.
Their conversations were often short, but they no longer needed many words to understand one another.
Serhiy watched Viktoriya slowly return to life and felt a deep, quiet satisfaction that he had been able to help bring her home.
His place, now, was beside them.
Beside his new family.
The war was not over.
Its echoes reached them even in comparatively peaceful Germany.
But they knew they would keep fighting in the ways left to them.
For peace.
For a future.
For every life that could still be saved.
They had become living proof that even in the darkest time, love, hope, and faith could still produce something that looked like a miracle.
They were ready for another life.
New difficulties.
New victories.
Together.

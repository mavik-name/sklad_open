Chapter 17: A Raid Behind Enemy Lines
Returning to Ukraine hit like a blow. The quiet of the German clinic and the warmth of the woman he loved were replaced by the hard reality of Donbas.
Serhiy wasted no time. His unit was waiting. His experience was needed.
He went straight back to his brigade and to his men, who welcomed him with respect and genuine relief. Within days he was once again in full combat gear, commanding a reconnaissance team and preparing for another mission.
The Donetsk front greeted him with the familiar thunder of artillery and the smell of propellant. Every meter of ground seemed soaked in blood and sweat. The face that had softened a little during rehabilitation hardened again into steel.
This was where he believed he belonged, however much pain and loss came with it.
The briefing took place in a dugout lit by a dim bulb.
The brigade commander, a broad man with exhausted eyes, spread a map across the table.
"We have intelligence on a detention center not far behind the line. Our prisoners are being held there. More importantly, a group of senior FSB officers has just arrived. We need exchange assets. Not rank-and-file occupiers—officers. We need prisoners who can talk. And if the situation allows it, we get our people out too."
Serhiy listened closely, eyes moving over the map and the route.
Thirty kilometers into enemy territory.
A deep raid. Extremely dangerous.
But the objective mattered too much.
Capturing high-value prisoners could help bring Ukrainians home.
And perhaps it might lead to something about Viktoriya.
The thought went through him like current.
He had promised Anna he would look for her sister.
This might be his chance.
"The mission is to take one or more FSB officers alive," the commander continued. "If conditions allow, we also extract our prisoners. Be extremely careful. This isn't just a raid. It has to be surgical."
Serhiy nodded.
He felt the weight of the mission—the responsibility for his men, for the prisoners, for Viktoriya, for the promise he had made to Anna.
Her face rose in his mind. Her tears. Her faith in him.
She was waiting.
The boy was waiting.
He had to come back.
He looked at his scouts. Young men, but battle-hardened already. Every one of them was ready to follow him into fire.
They began preparing equipment: weapons, radios, night-vision devices, medical kits. Every movement had been practiced so often it no longer required thought.
Adrenaline began pulsing through him.
Fear was there too, but mixed with resolve, readiness, the need to do what had to be done.
He was a reconnaissance soldier.
This was his life.
And he was prepared to risk everything to complete the mission and come back with answers.

Chapter 18: Waiting in the Shadows
Nearly a full day of moving covertly through occupied territory drained them.
Every step had to be measured. Every sound could mean danger.
The reconnaissance team moved like shadows, avoiding patrols, minefields, and surveillance cameras. Kilometer after kilometer, they felt every rise in the ground, every movement in the leaves.
Tension hung in the air, but every man knew what the raid might cost.
Eventually they reached the observation point.
What lay ahead tightened Serhiy's chest.
The occupiers had established their filtration and detention center inside a former orphanage.
Playgrounds once filled with children's voices stood empty and bleak. The windows stared out like hollow eye sockets.
A place once meant to protect children had been turned into a prison and torture site.
Hidden in dense vegetation, Serhiy studied the compound through binoculars.
There were not many enemy soldiers outside. Most appeared to be mobilized locals—uncertain, apathetic, carrying rifles as though the weapons belonged to someone else.
But from time to time men in civilian clothing moved between the buildings.
The way the soldiers reacted to them—straightening, saluting, avoiding eye contact—made it obvious who they were.
FSB.
Exactly the kind of people the Ukrainians had come for.
Their movements were assured, their eyes cold. Authority followed them everywhere.
A full day passed in observation.
Each hour seemed endless.
Serhiy lay hidden, memorizing guard positions, patrol routes, the windows where light appeared, the patterns of movement inside the compound.
His mind worked constantly, breaking down every detail.
Sometimes, during those stretches of tight silence, his thoughts escaped to Germany.
To Anna.
To the boy.
He remembered her gentle hands, the warmth of her body, her breath against his skin. The need to feel her beside him again tightened his chest.
Her scent, the remembered taste of her, stayed with him like a promise.
Those memories gave him air in the darkness around him.
He pictured Maryanchyk running and laughing while Anna watched him with that small smile of hers.
Those images anchored him inside the hell around them.
The team waited for dusk.
Darkness would improve their chances.
Night was an ally, concealing movement and hiding them from enemy eyes.
With every minute the tension grew.
But the scouts were ready.
Ready for action.
For risk.
For the fight.
For Ukraine.
For their own.

Chapter 19: The Bloody Trap
Dusk settled over the orphanage and the surrounding ground, wrapping everything in thick darkness.
Their time had come.
The scouts gathered silently around Serhiy like ghosts. Their faces were focused, every movement controlled.
The plan was simple, but it demanded perfect timing.
"All right," Serhiy said quietly. His voice was calm, but steel ran through it. "We enter from three sides. Cover team—you take the perimeter and block withdrawal routes and possible reinforcements. Assault team—you neutralize the outer guards, fast and quiet. We want the men in civilian clothes alive. I'll handle locating and freeing our prisoners. If everything goes to plan, we pull them through the western side, where extraction is waiting."
He looked at each man.
Readiness stared back at him.
They were professionals shaped by combat, and they trusted their commander without hesitation.
They knew the risk.
They also knew the value of the mission.
"Remember: silence and speed. No unnecessary movement. No unnecessary sound. We work as one mechanism. Good luck. For Ukraine."
Every man nodded.
Final equipment checks.
Then the group dissolved into the dark.
Serhiy moved toward the central building with two men covering him. Intelligence indicated the prisoners were being held there.
The assault began almost at once.
The first muted shots came nearly simultaneously—snipers and assault elements quietly eliminating the outer sentries.
Everything was working.
Serhiy felt his heartbeat settle into the rhythm of the operation.
He was almost at the entrance when automatic fire suddenly tore the night apart.
Then another burst.
Then another.
Not from the expected guard positions.
From inside the building.
"Trap!" someone shouted over the radio.
The voice cut off.
Everything collapsed into chaos.
Rifle barrels appeared in windows that had looked empty seconds earlier and a storm of fire poured out.
This was no firefight.
It was a slaughter.
The enemy had been waiting.
They knew about the raid.
"Fall back! Fall back!" Serhiy shouted from behind the cover of a wall.
His mind raced, trying to understand how.
A mole.
Someone in command.
There was no other explanation.
They had been sold out.
He watched his men go down.
A young soldier on the cover position fell first, body folding to the ground as blood spread beneath him.
Pain struck Serhiy, sharp and immediate, but there was no time to stop.
He fired back, trying to protect whoever could still move.
"Cover me! I'm pulling the wounded!" one of his men shouted, dragging another soldier.
The next burst hit him and dropped him beside the man he had been trying to save.
Pain.
Rage.
Despair.
Serhiy fired as if the rifle had become part of his arm.
Enemy faces appeared in the windows, and hatred burned in him with the same raw intensity Viktoriya had once felt staring at burning occupiers through a drone feed.
He fired for every one of his men.
For every dead Ukrainian.
Then pain tore through his shoulder.
A bullet passed through, burning flesh and muscle.
He groaned but stayed upright.
His hand clamped over the wound, warm blood filling his palm.
It hurt badly.
But weakness was not available to him.
He was the commander.
He had to get whoever remained out.
"Fall back! Anyone who can move, get to the rally point. I'm covering you!"
His voice was hoarse but hard.
He saw what remained of the group—only a handful of men—retreating while firing back.
They had been shattered, but not broken.
Serhiy kept shooting, pulling enemy attention toward himself.
His strength was draining fast.
But he remembered who he had promised to return to.
For the moment, that was enough.

Chapter 20: The Ravine
After the slaughter in the night, they kept moving almost until morning.
They twisted through tree lines and gullies, changed direction repeatedly, froze beneath trees while drones passed overhead, then moved again the moment they heard pursuit somewhere behind them.
They took turns carrying the wounded.
By dawn, the pursuit seemed to have fallen back, but they still could not stop. They needed more distance.
When they finally dropped into a thick ravine, the sun was already almost overhead and burning the ground without mercy.
Of the twelve men who had begun the raid, five remained.
They gave themselves five minutes.
No more.
Every movement required force.
Thirst had become agony.
Their throats felt like sandpaper. Their tongues stuck to the roof of the mouth. Even swallowing saliva hurt.
Wounds still bled, leaving dark sticky stains across torn uniforms.
Serhiy's shoulder burned and pulsed as though a coal had been buried inside it. Every heartbeat sent another blade of pain through the wound. Blood continued to seep through the field dressing he had applied in haste.
Every tourniquet in their kits had been used on men more badly wounded than they were—men they had ultimately been unable to carry out of the killing ground at the orphanage.
The group had been torn apart.
Only a few remained, and every one of them was wounded.
A gunshot through a leg.
Fragment wounds in an arm.
A concussion that would not stop the world from spinning.
Every man could feel strength leaving his body, muscle refusing orders, consciousness starting to drift.
Above them, enemy drones buzzed in the hot sky like horseflies.
The monotonous sound was a constant reminder of what hung over them.
Serhiy imagined an enemy operator many kilometers away staring into a screen, perhaps wearing the same indifferent expression as the FSB officer who had interrogated Viktoriya.
The operator had probably guessed they were somewhere in the ravine. It was the logical place to hide after the failed raid.
But dense brush closed around them on every side, and for the moment the drones could not see through it.
That bought them time.
Nothing more.
A few borrowed minutes of survival.
The problem itself remained.
A brutal dilemma opened in front of Serhiy.
There were very few choices, and every one seemed lethal.
Break through to Ukrainian positions?
The nearest friendly line was still about ten kilometers away. Every meter already felt endless. They lacked strength and ammunition.
Every round mattered.
Every step demanded more than the body had left.
Hide here and hope the enemy never found them?
That would only postpone the end—a slow death from wounds, thirst, and hunger.
Surrender?
Die?
Serhiy looked at his men.
Their faces were pale and distorted by pain and exhaustion, but a small spark still lived in their eyes.
Faith in him.
Their commander.
A third of the men beside him, maybe more, would not survive until evening without medical help.
The wounds were too serious. Blood loss was becoming critical.
One of them, a young soldier named Maksym who had only recently married, was barely holding back a groan while pressing both hands against his wounded leg. His face had turned green. His breathing came in short jerks.
If they surrendered, Serhiy believed the wounded would be killed immediately. He had seen enough to expect no mercy.
The thought sent another wave of rage through him.
Fight here and die?
There was no sense in it. It would be suicide, an empty death that changed nothing.
And once the drones located them, FPV strikes could finish the rest in minutes.
Through pain and thirst, one thought kept forcing its way back into his mind:
he had promised to return.
To Anna.
To the boy who had asked whether he would find his mother and father.
That promise was enough to keep him from lying down in the grass and closing his eyes.
First, he had to get his men back to their own side.
Everything else could wait.

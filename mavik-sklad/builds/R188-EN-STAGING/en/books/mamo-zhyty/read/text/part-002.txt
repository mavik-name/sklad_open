Chapter 5: Tears of Hope
A month passed. A month of exhausting training for Viktoriya, a month of unbearable waiting for her sister, who was already in Germany with the boy at one of the leading clinics. Every day was filled with anxiety and hope. Viktoriya caught every free minute she could to call her sister and ask how her son was doing.
One evening, after another drone-control session, she received a message from Anna: "Video call in ten minutes. We have news." Her heart began to beat faster. She hurried back to her tent, where there was at least some signal.
Her sister's face appeared on the phone screen, and beside her was Maryanchyk's pale but smiling face. Viktoriya felt tears fill her eyes. He was so thin, but there was a brightness in his eyes she had not seen for a long time. His child's will to live, despite everything he had endured, remained unbroken. He dreamed of coming home, playing with his friends, running barefoot through the grass again.
"Mom!" the boy cried, and though his voice was weak, it carried so much love that she barely held back a sob.
"My sweetheart! How are you, my love?" She tried to sound calm, but her voice trembled.
"I'm okay, Mom. Aunt Anya says the chemo helped," he said, smiling a gap-toothed smile.
Anna took over, her face serious but her eyes shining with hope. "Vika, the doctors say the chemotherapy worked. He's in stable remission. The tumor has shrunk, and it's... it's almost a miracle."
Viktoriya felt an enormous weight lift from her shoulders. Remission. That was the word she had waited for, prayed for.
"But..." Anna's voice grew careful again. "The doctors are insisting on urgent surgery. They need to remove what remains of the tumor before it begins growing again. It's very risky, but they say this is our best chance for a full recovery."
Joy immediately gave way to another wave of fear. Surgery. One more ordeal.
"Mom..." Maryanchyk suddenly began to cry, his little face twisting with pain and fear. "Mom, I love you so much. I want to live, Mom! I want to come home to you! I want to play like before..."
His words went straight through her. She saw his tears on the screen, felt his pain, his fear. And in that moment she knew there was nothing that could stop her. Not fear, not exhaustion, not distance. She would go through fire and water for this little boy who wanted so desperately to live.
"I love you too, my darling," she whispered, no longer able to hold back the tears running down her cheeks. "I'll be there soon. I promise. Just hold on, my brave boy. We'll get through all of this. I promise you."
Anna held the boy silently and stroked his head. She understood that Viktoriya did not need words now, only the sight of someone there beside him. The video call, full of tears and hope, became one more confirmation that her decision had been the right one. She was fighting not merely for money, but for every breath, every smile her son might still have. And she would not stop until she saw him healthy and happy.

Chapter 6: Baptism by Fire
Another two weeks passed—weeks that felt like an eternity to Viktoriya and vanished at the same time in a single breath. Training was over, and she arrived at the positions with her unit. The land here had been scorched by war, the trees shredded by fragments, the air saturated with smoke and metal. Every cell in her body felt ready for action and exhausted by constant tension at once. She was no longer merely a drone operator but part of a large, coordinated mechanism defending the country. The atmosphere was taut, but everyone knew their role. They breathed almost in rhythm with one another, held by the certainty that the people beside them would stand to the end.
Her first combat mission came without warning. Late evening. A black sky. Only stars and the moon lit the ground, throwing strange shadows across the landscape. A cold wind cut through her clothing, but she barely felt it; adrenaline warmed her from within. Then an urgent but controlled voice sounded over the radio:
"Enemy vehicle movement detected! BMP with infantry aboard, heading toward our flank. We need fire correction!"
Her heart hammered like a crazed bird inside a cage. This was what she had spent a month preparing for, yet reality was far more intense than any simulation. Her palms went damp. Her breathing became shallow. But fear did not paralyze her. It sharpened everything.
She took her place at the monitor, hands moving over the controls with the same practiced precision as if she were flying a wedding drone rather than a machine over a battlefield. The drone rose into the night like a hunting bird, its buzz disappearing into the wind. A blurred but recognizable image appeared on the screen: the silhouette of an infantry fighting vehicle moving along a dirt road, with the dark shapes of soldiers clinging to the armor like spiders. With the thermal camera, even in complete darkness, Viktoriya could see the enemy's heat signatures clearly, their figures glowing on the screen like ghosts.
"Target acquired," she reported, her voice surprisingly calm despite the storm inside her. "BMP, approximately five hundred meters from grid A-3. Infantry riding on top. Six or seven personnel."
The coordinates went to the artillery crew. Holding her breath, she watched the screen. The BMP stopped. The soldiers began jumping down in a hurry, their figures moving frantically through the grainy image.
Then came the explosion.
Not merely a sound, but something that passed through the earth beneath her. The first round landed nearby, throwing up a column of soil and smoke. The enemy panicked, scrambling for cover, their silhouettes scattering in every direction like cockroaches.
"Correction—one hundred meters left, fifty closer," she ordered clearly, eyes fixed on the monitor, every part of her mind working at the edge. She could see every movement, every frantic attempt to hide, almost feel their fear and confusion. In that moment she had become the eyes and hands of revenge.
The next shell struck the BMP directly.
It was not simply an explosion. It was hell. A bright flash blinded her for an instant even through the screen. Thick black smoke climbed into the sky. The vehicle caught fire and became a burning heap of metal. Figures wreathed in flame leapt from the armor, ran like living torches trying to beat the fire from their clothes, then collapsed onto the ground, twisting in their final convulsions. Their screams could not reach her through the noise of battle, yet she imagined she could hear them in the air. It was the first BMP destroyed under her correction, her first combat success written in enemy blood.
Watching the burning invaders run, Viktoriya felt no pity. Not a drop of compassion. Instead, a fierce, consuming rage grew in her chest. She no longer saw only enemy soldiers. She saw the faces of those who had taken her parents, shot in Bucha; those who had taken her husband, Dmytro, who would never see his son grow up. Every burning shape on the screen became an embodiment of the pain and loss that had torn her life apart.
She thought of Maryanchyk—his pale little face, his tears, his desperate wish to live, his small hands reaching for her. The aggressor had nothing directly to do with her son's illness, and still fury tore through her because she had been forced to destroy them instead of being beside her child, holding his hand, reading him stories, listening to his laughter. That anger became fuel. She had been forced to become a soldier so her child could live, and the injustice of it ripped through her from the inside.
"Target destroyed," a satisfied voice came over the radio. "Well done, operator. Excellent work."
She lowered her head in silence, fists clenched so tightly that her nails dug into her palms. She had done what she was supposed to do. She had defended. She had taken revenge. And she was earning the money that might keep her son alive.
But the price was unbearable.
Even in success, there was no triumph inside her, only the bitter taste of loss and an endless struggle. She felt split between two worlds: the war, where she had become a ruthless fighter, and Maryanchyk's world, where she was simply his mother. She knew that fracture would remain with her until the war ended and her son recovered.

Chapter 7: Darkness on the Donetsk Front
After her baptism by fire, Viktoriya spent several more weeks at the positions, sharpening her skills as a drone operator. Her accuracy and her calm under fire quickly earned the respect of the others. But war never stood still, and experienced specialists were needed everywhere. One morning the order came: Viktoriya was being reassigned to the Donetsk sector, where aerial reconnaissance had become critically important because of intense enemy activity and difficult terrain.
"Be careful, Vika," her commander said, shaking her hand. "It's hot there. But you'll manage. You're our guardian angel."
The road was long and exhausting. She rode in an armored vehicle with several comrades—the driver, a rifleman, and another drone operator. Destroyed villages, scorched fields, blackened trees slid past the windows. Every kilometer brought her closer to another test, another danger. She tried to think of Maryanchyk, of his smile, of the two of them walking through a park when all of this was over. Those thoughts were her shield against the fear creeping up inside her.
The vehicle was almost at the point where their new drone position was supposed to be set up when the sky suddenly tore open with a roar.
"Incoming!" the driver shouted, wrenching the wheel.
The vehicle lurched sideways and Viktoriya slammed into the interior wall. A second explosion came a moment later, closer. The armored vehicle shook like a toy. Windows burst. The inside filled with the smell of burning material.
"Out! Sabotage group!" the rifleman shouted. She saw that he was already firing toward the tree line.
They were in an ambush.
Dazed but still thinking, Viktoriya jumped from the vehicle. Her comrades were taking defensive positions, firing at an enemy they could barely see. Automatic fire, bullets, grenade blasts cut through the air. The familiar rage returned—the same rage she had felt in her first battle. Not only for herself, but for the people beside her, for everyone who had suffered because of this enemy.
She pulled her rifle free and took cover behind the damaged vehicle. Hands trained for precise movements on a controller now gripped a weapon. She fired at muzzle flashes and shadows moving between the trees. She saw enemy figures fall and felt a strange satisfaction mixed with revulsion.
Then an enormous blast erupted only a few meters away. An RPG round struck a tree behind her. The shock wave threw her backward, and a sharp pain exploded through her head. The world smeared before her eyes. Sound collapsed into a dull roar. Bodies became blurred shapes.
Concussion.
She fell to the ground, trying to understand where she was, consciousness fading and returning in waves. Her head hurt unbearably. Her ears rang. She tried to rise, but her body would not obey. Through the veil of pain she saw her comrades still firing back, but there were fewer of them now.
Then something moved at the edge of her vision.
A figure stepped from behind the bushes behind her. Tall. Dark uniform. Rifle in his hands. He was so close she could see his face twisted with hatred.
The last thing she saw was the butt of his rifle swinging toward her temple with a vicious whistle.
Then the world went out and sank into complete, soundless darkness.

Chapter 8: Captive in the Dark
The darkness did not lift all at once.
At first Viktoriya felt only the deep throbbing pain in her temple, pulsing with her heartbeat, and an unbearable ringing in her ears. It felt as though her skull might split open. Every sound seemed magnified tenfold, driving sharp needles into her brain.
Then came the cold and damp. The smell of diesel fuel, metal, blood.
She tried to move, but her body would not respond. Every attempt sent a sharp burning pain through her. Her arms and legs felt foreign, heavy, as if filled with lead. It was dark around her. Only a faint strip of light came through gaps somewhere in the metal, enough for her to make out vague shapes.
She realized she was lying on the floor of a vehicle that shook and rattled as it moved.
A "motolyha"—an enemy tracked armored carrier.
The truth crashed down on her like freezing water.
She was not alone.
Another body lay beside her in the same cramped darkness. She strained her eyes until they adjusted and recognized one of her comrades—the young rifleman who had ridden in the vehicle with her. His face was pale. A dark patch of blood was spreading across his abdomen. He breathed heavily, each breath costing him enormous effort.
Voices came from outside through the thin armor. Rough, alien voices full of cruel amusement. They were speaking Russian.
"Well, you khokhols, look where all that jumping got you," one voice mocked.
"Look at the pretty one. I'd screw her," another said, and nausea rose in her throat.
"Then cut her head off. Or better yet, fill her up with foam," a third voice said.
Her parents flashed through her mind. Bucha. That cruelty. That absence of anything human. A cold wave ran down her spine and her stomach clenched with fear and disgust.
The wounded soldier beside her groaned.
With enormous effort she reached toward him. Her fingers found skin cold and sticky with blood.
"Hold on, brother," she whispered hoarsely.
His eyes barely opened. Pain clouded them, but there was still a spark of awareness.
"Vika... tell mine... if you survive... tell them I... I died thinking... of them..."
His voice broke. He coughed, and blood ran from his mouth.
Her heart felt as though it were being torn apart.
The concussion, the pain in her body—everything retreated behind the unbearable pain inside her. She was lying here in captivity while her comrade died beside her. And somewhere far away her little boy was waiting for her, waiting for surgery, waiting for rehabilitation.
"No... no, you're going to live," she whispered, trying not to sob.
But tears were already running down her cheeks, mixing with dust and blood.
She thought of her son. His pale, beloved little face. His hunger for life. His words: "Mom, I love you so much. I want to live, Mom!"
The pain she felt now was nothing beside the thought that she might never see him again. Never hold him. Never hear him laugh. Never tell him another bedtime story.
She had betrayed him.
She had failed to be there when he needed her, and now perhaps she never would be.
"Maryanchyk... sweetheart..." Her lips formed his name without sound.
Despair began to close over her—cold, sticky, threatening to swallow her whole.
But somewhere deep inside, beneath the pain and terror, a tiny spark still remained.
Rage.
Resolve.
She had to survive.
For her son.
She had to come back.
It became an oath to herself, a promise to her child, the only road toward salvation she had left.
And darkness covered her again.

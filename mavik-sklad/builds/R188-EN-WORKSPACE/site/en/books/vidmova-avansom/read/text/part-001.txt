CHAPTER ONE

THE MANUSCRIPT THAT DIDN'T EXIST

Matvii received the email on Tuesday at 10:47 in the morning, between a notification from his bank about insufficient funds and an ad for a course called “How to Write a Bestseller in Seven Evenings.”

The bank told him nothing new.

The course offended him.

The email surprised him.

The subject line read:

“Response Regarding Your Submitted Manuscript.”

Matvii opened the message.

Hello!

Thank you for your trust and for sharing your text with us. We know how much time, attention, and inner courage stand behind every manuscript, so we deeply value the opportunity to read yours.

Matvii looked around.

There was no one behind him.

“Good start,” he said. “At least they correctly identified me as a living person.”

The publisher went on to assure him that they had carefully reviewed his text but were not currently prepared to offer cooperation. The decision, they said, did not mean the manuscript lacked value or would never find its reader. It was simply connected with editorial plans, the publisher’s current direction, and the number of projects they were able to take on at that moment.

At the end, Matvii was advised not to lose faith in his own voice.

The message was signed warmly.

They had even added a heart.

Matvii read the email a second time.

Then a third.

On the fourth, he opened his Sent folder.

There were no messages to this publisher.

He checked Drafts, Archive, Trash, and even Spam — just in case one of his novels had lost patience and gone looking for a publisher on its own.

Nothing.

Matvii thought about it.

Maybe he had submitted a manuscript in his sleep.

People called ex-lovers after a third drink, sent resignation letters to their bosses at two in the morning, and ordered inflatable swimming pools in November. Why couldn’t a writer send off a novel without waking up?

Except he would first have had to write a novel he did not remember.

He pulled a thin notebook from under the keyboard and wrote:

Plot No. 1. A writer receives a rejection for a novel he wrote in his sleep. Tries to reconstruct the text from the editor’s comments, but there are no comments.

He felt better immediately.

Matvii loved plots. Especially the ones life delivered for free. Unlike literary courses, life did not demand prepayment and never promised a certificate.

He looked at the email again.

This was not even an ordinary rejection.

An ordinary rejection had to be earned: write a novel, edit it, prepare a synopsis, find the publisher’s address, send everything off, and then spend several months checking your inbox with the concentration of a bomb technician examining a wire.

Matvii had done none of that.

He had been rejected in advance.

The publisher was effectively saying:

“We don’t know what you might offer us, but already — no.”

Matvii wrote:

Plot No. 2. A person receives a rejection for every future request. Lives peacefully because there is no longer any point in being afraid.

Then he noticed the “To” line.

There were many recipients.

A great many.

So many that the personal letter began to resemble a national census.

Hundreds of email addresses ran one after another in a solid wall: writers, translators, psychologists, musicians, people with pen names, people without pen names, one Dark Lord, two Kyiv Witches, and someone whose address began with the words genius_forever.

The warm personal letter written separately to every living person had been sent simultaneously to all living people.

Matvii was touched.

It was about as intimate as a priest switching on a loudspeaker during confession so the congregation would not have to waste time in line.

He wrote:

Plot No. 3. Factory-made individual compassion. Minimum order: one hundred souls.

His inbox pinged.

Someone had clicked Reply All.

The message consisted of one word:

Thank you.

Matvii assumed that would be the end of it.

But a minute later, a woman named Myroslava replied:

Thank you for your response! Wishing the publishing house inspiration and prosperity!

Another minute later, someone else wrote:

Myroslava, thank you for your warm words!

Myroslava answered:

Thank you too!

Then a man named Oleksii joined the gratitude exchange and thanked both of them for the positivity.

Matvii poured himself coffee.

He had a feeling the day was going to be productive.

Usually his days were far more prosaic.

By day, Matvii wrote advertising copy. Thanks to him, toothpaste “gave you confidence,” concrete “created space for the future,” and cat food “turned every meal into an act of unconditional love.”

Matvii had never seen a cat treat food as anything else.

He was paid for advertising copy.

His fiction he published for free.

One acquaintance called this economic suicide.

Matvii objected:

“Suicide implies the person was economically alive to begin with.”

He did not consider money evil. On the contrary, he treated it with respect, especially when he had some. He simply disliked the idea that the price of a work should be established before the reader had even seen the first sentence.

All his stories and novels were available freely on self-publishing platforms. Beneath every text was a small button:

“Was the book worth a coffee? Send the author a ding.”

The button did not ding often.

Last month it had dinged twice.

The first payment was fifty hryvnias with the comment:

“Really liked it.”

The second was one hryvnia:

“Didn’t like it, but finished it.”

Matvii valued the second payment more. It was the most honest royalty of his life.

His literary principle was simple:

Art should not begin at the cash register.

Play first.

Then, if someone stops, listens to the end, and feels something they did not feel a minute ago, they can decide for themselves whether that encounter was worth a coin.

Matvii loved street musicians.

They did not promise to change a passer-by’s life. They did not sell the right to hear the first three notes. They did not force anyone to register, subscribe, and agree to personal-data processing before the first chord.

They simply played.

If the music touched someone, that person stopped.

If it did not, they kept walking to buy potatoes.

It was an honest bargain.

What irritated Matvii instead were celebrities who walked onstage after thousands of people had bought tickets, paid for the cloakroom, parking, service fees, and a plastic cup of water priced like a small oil well, then spread their arms wide and shouted:

“I’m giving you a new song!”

Matvii always wanted to ask:

“Then what exactly did we pay for?”

Delivery of the gift?

The ceremonial handover?

The right to stand in the building where it occurred to you to bestow this generosity upon us?

He felt the same way about books.

A person could read his text. If it made them feel good, hurt, laugh, or at least feel that their time had not been entirely wasted, they could ding him a coffee.

If they could not, that was fine too.

Art did not become worse because the reader did not have fifty hryvnias.

Nor did it become better because of a hard cover, spot varnish, and an author photograph in which the writer stared into the distance as if he had just spotted his own print run on the horizon.

Acquaintances sometimes repeated:

“An artist must be hungry.”

Matvii had noticed that this was almost always said by people who had just eaten well.

His inbox pinged again.

This time someone wrote:

Hello! I don’t understand who all of you are replying to. Is this some kind of glitch?

The message asking who everyone was writing to was also sent to everyone.

Matvii finished his coffee.

The rejection he had never requested was gradually becoming the most interesting literary event of the year.

And the publisher, apparently, had no idea it had already published it.

CHAPTER THREE

THE LIVING MANUSCRIPT

Within a week, writers were reading in parks in five cities.

Within two weeks, twelve.

Within a month, musicians, artists, actors, translators, and one sculptor had joined them. The sculptor placed an empty pedestal in the middle of a square with a plaque that read:

“The work was carefully reviewed by the publisher.”

The sculptor earned the most.

In a Kharkiv underpass, a man read a historical novel about the princely era. The novel was so detailed that metro passengers could trace the formation of Kyivan Rus between two trains.

In Lviv, a poet read free verse in the rain.

In Dnipro, a playwright performed a play with himself because the rest of the actors had mixed up the time.

In Chernivtsi, a crime writer read the ending first to see whether anyone would stay for the beginning.

In Kyiv, a man declaimed the manual for a washing machine.

He was exposed only at the section on spin speed, but the audience decided it was postmodernism and gave him even more money.

Rules appeared.

The text had to be your own.

The reading had to be free.

You could place a hat, box, jar, or cup nearby.

The listener owed nothing.

Neither did the author.

Matvii protested against the rules.

“When art begins with rules, it usually ends with a committee,” he wrote.

The next day, the sentence was added to the rules as a preamble.

Television channels began talking about a new cultural movement.

Journalists called Matvii.

“Are you the organiser of The Living Manuscript?”

“No.”

“But it all began with your comment.”

“It all began with someone else’s mistake.”

“Who is on your team?”

“The person who clicked Send to All.”

“What is your promotion strategy?”

“Don’t stop people from doing stupid things. Sometimes culture comes out of them.”

That line was pulled apart into quotes as well.

One magazine called Matvii “the ideologue of a new immediacy.”

Another called him “the voice of an unpublished generation.”

A third wrote that he was “challenging the capitalist model of cultural consumption.”

Matvii spent a long time trying to remember when exactly he had managed to issue that challenge.

That day he was writing an advertisement for a new kind of cat litter.

Orest Bahatotomnyi suggested publishing a collection of Matvii’s manifestos.

“I don’t have any manifestos.”

“We’ll collect your messages from the group.”

“They’re not manifestos.”

“That is exactly what manifesto writers always say.”

The number of Matvii’s readers grew first tenfold, then a hundredfold.

His free books were downloaded, discussed, criticised, praised, and recommended to people he had never met.

Video essays appeared.

On one channel, a man spent forty minutes explaining what Matvii had meant in a short story.

Matvii watched half of it and realised he had meant considerably less.

The “ding me a coffee” button began ringing so often that he turned off notifications.

Twenty, fifty, a hundred hryvnias at a time.

The comments varied:

“For the manuscript that didn’t exist.”

“So there’ll be something to reject next time.”

“For coffee for the starving artist.”

“Do not spend before the contract is signed.”

One reader sent five hryvnias and wrote:

“First chapter was good. Then you ruined it.”

Matvii replied:

“Next time I’ll ruin it earlier and save you time.”

His answer was shared several thousand times.

The less Matvii tried to become famous, the more famous he became.

This irritated many people who had spent years trying to become famous.

Articles appeared about his carefully designed personal-brand strategy.

A communications expert explained that denying his own leadership was a brilliant way of strengthening his leadership.

A marketer called it “negative-positioning methodology.”

Matvii wrote to him:

I simply did not want to be a leader.

The marketer asked permission to use the line in a training course.

And then the publisher wrote to Matvii again.

The same one.

This time the email was personal.

There was only one address in the “To” field.

The subject line read:

“Proposal for Cooperation.”

The publisher wrote that it had been closely following Matvii’s creative journey, greatly valued his distinctive voice, and saw strong potential in a joint project.

There was no mention of the manuscript they had once carefully read.

Matvii was invited to a meeting.

He agreed.

Not because he had changed his views.

He was curious to meet people capable of rejecting a text before it existed and then offering to publish an author after the author no longer needed them.

Three people came to the meeting room.

An editor.

A marketer.

And a man in a dark suit who did not introduce himself but looked as though he had personally invented the printing press.

On the table stood bottles of water, logo notebooks, and copies of books arranged so that each one would accidentally appear in any photograph.

“We’ve been watching you for quite some time,” the editor said.

“Since the first rejection?” Matvii asked.

The editor smiled the professional smile of someone trained not to answer a question without appearing silent.

“Your case has generated considerable public attention.”

“My case demonstrated the Reply All button.”

The marketer leaned forward.

“You have built a powerful community around yourself.”

“It built itself around someone else’s mistake.”

“That is precisely what organic growth means.”

The man in the suit nodded approvingly.

Apparently organic growth was within his area of expertise.

“We would like to offer you a book deal,” the editor said.

“For which book?”

The three of them looked at one another.

Matvii had a feeling he was asking inconvenient questions again.

“We can select the text with the strongest commercial potential.”

“But you already read one.”

The editor picked up a bottle of water but did not open it.

The marketer rescued the situation.

“The important thing now is not a specific text. The important thing is your name.”

“And the book?”

“The book becomes a vehicle for your brand.”

Matvii looked at the editor.

Then at the marketer.

Then at the man who had invented the printing press.

“I thought a name went on a book because somebody wrote it.”

“The modern market is more complicated than that.”

“That much I’ve understood.”

The marketer opened a presentation.

The first slide contained a photograph of Matvii taken from social media. In the original, he was holding a bag of potatoes. The bag had been cropped out, so now Matvii looked like a thinker reaching one hand toward the future.

The second slide read:

MATVII SAHAIDAK — THE VOICE OF A NEW LITERARY FREEDOM

The third:

TARGET AUDIENCE: 18–45, ACTIVE, CREATIVE, INCLINED TO REFLECTION.

“Do people stop reflecting after forty-five?” Matvii asked.

“It’s a marketing segment.”

“My most devoted reader is sixty-two.”

“She can be included in the secondary audience.”

Matvii imagined his reader being included secondarily and stopped interrupting.

The final slide displayed large gold letters:

WE WILL MAKE YOU A STAR.

The marketer said it aloud.

“We will make you a star.”

Matvii looked at the screen.

“Why?”

The marketer hesitated.

Apparently that question was not in the presentation.

“So people will know you.”

“People already know me.”

“So they will read you.”

“They already read me.”

“So they will buy your books.”

“They read them for free.”

“That is precisely what we intend to fix.”

The editor placed a contract in front of Matvii.

Under the contract, the publisher received the rights.

Matvii received royalties.

The reader received the opportunity to purchase what they had been able to read for free yesterday.

“And what does art receive?” Matvii asked.

The three representatives of the publisher looked at one another.

Art was not a party to the contract.

Matvii took a copy home.

That evening he read it carefully.

This time, genuinely carefully.

The next morning, he wrote:

Dear Publishing House,

Thank you for your trust and for sharing your proposal with me.

I have reviewed it carefully, but unfortunately I am not currently prepared to offer cooperation on this project.

This decision does not mean your proposal lacks value or will not find its author. Often such decisions are connected with my creative plans, current areas of activity, and the number of publishing houses I am able to take on at this time.

I wish you every success and encourage you not to lose faith in your own voice.

Warmly,
Matvii Sahaidak

He clicked Send.

This time — to one recipient only.

A few days later, Matvii was walking down into the metro.

In the underpass, a young woman stood reading a story aloud.

Beside her lay a printed page, a plastic cup, and a sign:

“If the story was worth a coffee — send a ding.”

Matvii recognised the text.

He had written it several years earlier.

Back then, twenty-three people had read the story.

One had clicked Like.

Another had pointed out a missing comma.

Now a crowd was listening in the underpass.

The young woman did not know the author was standing only a few steps away.

She did not need to know.

She simply read.

Some people stopped.

Some kept walking.

Some smiled.

A woman carrying heavy bags wiped her eyes.

A teenager who had been laughing took off his headphones.

A man in an expensive coat dropped a banknote into the cup and walked away quickly, as though afraid someone might catch him showing compassion.

Matvii took out a coin too.

He dropped it in.

It struck the plastic bottom with a ding.

The young woman looked up, smiled, and kept reading.

There was no stage in the underpass.

No posters.

No presentation, target audience, or gold lettering about a future star.

Only a voice, a text, and people free to stop or keep walking.

Matvii stayed a little longer, then headed for his train.

Now he knew exactly where the people at the publishing house had been wrong.

Mediocrity can be made into a star.

Real talent catches fire on its own.

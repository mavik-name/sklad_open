CHAPTER 7: RECOGNITION

Sarah Jenkins arrived in Ukraine—a political correspondent for The New York Times. Her work was read by heads of state, quoted in NATO analytical briefs and discussed at closed-door meetings. She did not write about events. She wrote about symptoms. Every paragraph was a diagnosis.

At forty-five, she looked like someone who had seen everything: regimes collapsing, revolutions losing their illusions, little flowers of democracy pushing through empty asphalt. Her eyes were tired and attentive. Whenever she did not believe the person speaking to her, her pen tapped against her notebook. It was her gesture of distrust.

And a warning.

She arrived without a delegation or protocol. One assistant. Two suitcases—one with clothes, the other with archives.

Her first target was Makar Zhuk.

They met in the Lviv office. Sarah did not like interviews. She liked collisions. Her method was not questioning but pressure. If a person broke, they were not worth her time. If they held, she listened.

“Mr Zhuk,” she began without the slightest diplomacy, “in the West, your idea is being called a dangerous utopia. Random selection of MPs. Governance with the help of AI. That sounds like either chaos or a techno-dictatorship. Who guarantees that Aaron Marks will not become Ukraine’s shadow dictator?”

Makar did not flinch. There was no indignation in his voice. Only fatigue and truth.

“Openness, Ms Jenkins. The code is open. Every transaction, every decision, every change is public. And as for randomness... is the system we have now any less absurd? We elect not the best candidates, but the most expensive ones. We aren’t drawing names from passers-by in the street. We’re selecting from millions of people who have passed integrity checks. Teachers. Doctors. Farmers. People who know how this country actually lives.”

Sarah did not write anything down.

She watched.

Her eyes were scanners. Her silence, a protocol. She did not interrupt. But the pen kept tapping.

Quietly.

Evenly.

A metronome of distrust.

“You call it a hive. Explain.”

“It’s a metaphor Marks came up with, and it works for me. In a hive there are worker bees—the non-resident citizens. They live, work, pay taxes. There are warrior bees—the resident citizens. They serve: in parliament, the army, government. And there is a queen—not a person. An idea. A country. Ukraine. We are the hive. Not for honey. For order.”

She listened closely. The scepticism had not vanished, but something shifted.

The pen stopped.

It was her private signal: This is not nonsense. This is a system.

After Makar, she travelled to Kyiv, where Viktor Kholodnytskyi received her.

“This Zhuk is a puppet,” he said, pouring himself whisky. “Marks wants to turn Ukraine into a testing ground. He wants our land, our people, our resources. I’m a patriot. I’m defending sovereignty.”

Sarah said nothing.

She only tapped the pen.

Steadily.

Mechanically.

Like a metronome.

Her eyes did not brighten. They registered.

She did not argue.

She collected material.

She spent another three days in Ukraine. She visited the Lviv headquarters, spoke with volunteers, reviewed the Justice source code, talked with Adriana, Marta and Serhii Broshch. Her notebook filled not with quotations, but with vectors. She was not hunting for scandal.

She was looking for symptoms of the future.

A week later, her article appeared in The New York Times:

“THE UKRAINIAN HIVE: REVOLUTION OR TECHNO-COLONIALISM?”

It was balanced. No admiration. No condemnation.

But between the lines, one thing was clear:

Something genuinely new is happening here.

And it deserves attention.

For the Nameless, it was a breakthrough.

Not approval.

Recognition.

And that was enough to make the world begin to listen.

CHAPTER 8: THE CAMPAIGN’S FINAL CHORD

Two weeks before the election, Viktor Kholodnytskyi went all in.

He did not merely attack. He unleashed full-scale information artillery.

This was no longer a campaign.

It was an attempted purge.

He bought prime time across every major national television channel for the premiere of his own “investigative” film:

TECHNO-DICTATORSHIP. THE THREAT FROM Y.

In the film, Pechko used his usual calm, confident voice to mix half-truth with invention and paint a nightmare world: AI controlling every movement; Marks as a technological vampire draining the country’s resources; Zhuk as a puppet suffering from a saviour complex.

The editing was aggressive.

The music, ominous.

The images, deliberately dark.

It was not analysis.

It was fear packaged as documentary television.

The Nameless responded asymmetrically.

Not with rebuttals.

With a challenge.

Adriana challenged Pechko to a live debate.

At first, he laughed. He even joked on air:

“I’ll explain to the child how the world works.”

Then he accepted.

The whole country watched.

It was more than a television moment. It was a test of truth.

Pechko sat in a suit, armed with a perfectly trained voice and manipulations that had worked for years.

Adriana came without theatrics. Plain clothes. Warmth in her eyes.

A blade in her voice.

He spoke first—about sovereignty, the “sale of the state,” the “utopia that will end in tyranny.” His words sounded like a script written before the broadcast.

But something inside him was already cracking.

Adriana stayed silent.

Watched.

Listened.

Then came a pause.

Short.

Tense.

And then her voice.

“Mr Oleh. You are a talented journalist. I watched your reports from 2014. You spoke about dignity. About fighting corruption. Tell me... what happened to you?”

Pechko jerked slightly.

The smile vanished.

It was not a question.

It was a mirror.

And he could not bear his own reflection.

For the first time in many years, he had no answer.

“I’ll show you,” Adriana said.

Documents appeared on the screen.

“This is Mr Kholodnytskyi’s declaration. And these are offshore records that international investigative journalists helped us obtain. Here—billions taken out of Ukraine. They did not go to the army. Not to reconstruction. They went to villas and yachts. And here...”

Click.

“This is the agreement for your house in Spain, Mr Oleh. Purchased one week after your first report about the Nameless aired.”

The studio went dead silent.

Not dramatically silent.

Deeply silent.

Like the moment before a sentence is handed down.

Pechko turned pale. He opened his mouth, but no sound came.

Millions of people saw more than confusion on his face.

They saw raw, animal fear.

“You say we are selling sovereignty,” Adriana continued calmly. “But isn’t robbing your country during a war an act of betrayal? You accuse us because factories are being built. Your masters simply steal. So tell me—who is the traitor here?”

It was a knockout.

Not theatrical.

Final.

Not because of emotion.

Because of fact.

And because of the silence that followed it.

Pechko disappeared from television that same evening.

Kholodnytskyi stopped appearing in public.

His headquarters went silent.

His advisers stopped answering calls.

His pages emptied.

The following day, support for the Nameless did not merely rise.

It broke through the ceiling.

And for the first time, not because of slogans.

Because of trust.

The Lviv office was quiet.

Adriana sat on the floor with her arms around her knees. Natalia brought tea. Makar silently rested a hand on her shoulder.

“You did it,” he said.

“I just told the truth.”

“And that was enough,” Serhii Broshch said. He had just returned from a meeting with international observers. “They say there hasn’t been a broadcast like that in any country in twenty years.”

On Y, the clip passed forty million views.

On TikTok, hundreds of thousands of remixes appeared.

Telegram channels gained waves of new subscribers.

At campaign offices, queues of new volunteers formed.

This was not hype.

It was a turning point.

That evening, after everyone had gone home, Makar stood by the window.

Snow was falling again.

But now it did not look like cleansing.

It looked like the silence before an election.

PART III. POWER

CHAPTER 9: THE DAY OF A NEW BEGINNING

Spring 2026.

Kyiv.

The sun pushed timidly through the clouds, as if it too could not quite believe a new day had arrived. The sky remained grey.

The ground was already different.

Tension hung over the city, the same charge that comes before a storm. But instead of lightning, there were long queues outside polling stations, silent and patient.

People stood for hours—the old and young, teachers and veterans, mothers with children.

Not under compulsion.

Not out of fear.

Because they needed to be part of the change.

Many held phones with the Nameless app open on their screens. It was more than a voting tool.

It had become a symbol of trust.

Three hundred MPs were to be elected. The rest of the country was still under occupation. The peace agreement signed by the previous government had been a bitter pill swallowed because there had been no choice.

Not accepted.

Endured.

The old political system, like an old shadow, had not disappeared. It hissed from billboards, hidden messages and black propaganda. Even on election day, every rule imaginable was broken.

But the country stayed quiet.

Not from indifference.

From resolve.

Millions of posts filled Y. The hashtag #NamelessForJustice drew twenty million views in a single day.

It was not a trend.

It was a referendum of conscience.

Makar stood outside a polling station in Pechersk. People recognised him. They came up to shake his hand. Some embraced him.

“This is our chance, Mr Makar,” an elderly teacher whispered, holding her ballot as though it were an icon. “We believe in you.”

“And I believe in you,” he replied.

Two security guards remained nearby like shadows. Five assassination attempts over recent months had taught them all that the world had changed.

Natalia stood beside him, holding his hand—firmly, gently.

“They believe in you,” she said quietly. “The peace they signed isn’t ours. The President... yes, he showed courage at the beginning of the war. But his time has passed. And the people around him... that isn’t the future we wanted.”

The election results were announced that evening.

The Nameless: 300 out of 300.

An absolute victory.

Not merely a majority.

Total responsibility.

Maidan erupted—joy, tears, embraces. Adriana cried while livestreaming.

“We did it! We changed history!”

But the system did not surrender.

Within an hour of the results, lawsuits began appearing:

“AI interference.”

“Manipulation.”

“Observers denied access.”

International experts were invited.

And they confirmed it:

No violations.

The algorithm was clean.

The will was real.

A month passed.

The new parliament assembled in the Verkhovna Rada building, repaired after the war. During those weeks, the old system had raced to save itself: money moved abroad, people fled, evidence disappeared.

Not everyone succeeded.

Some vanished.

Some stayed.

But without power.

On parliament’s first day, the Nameless did what no one expected.

They voted to impeach the sitting President.

He accepted it... with dignity.

Perhaps he understood too that his time had passed. Better to leave than be washed away.

Then came a series of laws:

• abolition of special pensions;
• closure of the borders to those who had moved capital out of the country;
• uncompromising lustration.

The army—battle-hardened, exhausted, but healthy at its core—supported the process.

This was not revenge.

It was sanitation.

That evening, Anna Kravets and Serhii Broshch entered Makar’s office.

Anna broke the silence first.

“Makar... this is from parliament. They have adopted constitutional amendments. The office of President has been abolished. In its place—Hetman. For life. And... they elected you.”

Makar said nothing.

His breathing grew heavier.

Slowly, he sat down.

“Is this a joke?” he finally asked.

Serhii answered firmly.

“No. It is a decision of the Constitutional Assembly. It is legal. It is... the will of the people. Fifty-four per cent of Ukrainians are party members. Another twenty-eight per cent are active supporters. This is the highest level of trust in the history of democracy. And it is your responsibility.”

Natalia stood nearby, fists clenched, eyes filled with fear and pride at the same time.

Lesia entered carrying a cup of tea.

“Dad, are you going to be like Bohdan Khmelnytskyi?”

Makar smiled and pulled her close.

“No, Lesia. I’m just Dad. And I have to make sure the country you live in is fair. Even if I have to pay the highest price for it.”

25 May 2026.

St Sophia Square.

The inauguration of the Hetman of Ukraine.

No motorcades.

No parade.

Makar wore a vyshyvanka. His family stood beside him. The hive behind him.

In the square, not fans.

Citizens.

Not slogans.

Belief.

That night Makar and Natalia sat on the terrace. Stars shone above the Dnipro.

“I never wanted to be Hetman,” he whispered. “But if that is the price of their future, I’m ready.”

She squeezed his hand.

Her silence contained agreement.

And love.

And faith.

And a quiet that was no longer fear.

It was a beginning.

CHAPTER 1: A BARREL OF PICKLED CUCUMBERS

Lviv Region. Autumn 2025.

The morning fog—thick and milky, like fresh warm milk—slid between the ancient trees, carefully wrapping the tired but unbroken contours of the Carpathians. Its silence was not emptiness but memory: something old, primordial, living in tree roots and cracks in stone. The earth had not yet awakened from its night thoughts, and only a thin wind, barely stirring the withered leaves, reminded the world that it was still moving.

The wooden house, warm and almost hidden in the haze, breathed with the smell of fresh baking and the dampness drifting in from the forest. Its walls held the whisper of nights, children's laughter, and the silence that comes after tears. On the porch, in an old wicker rocking chair, sat Makar Zhuk. He was already past fifty. Not exactly old, yet his thick white beard made him look like a kindly Santa. His eyes told another story—deep, tired, half-shadowed by memory. They were the eyes of a man who had seen a great deal and still had not lost the ability to look ahead with hope. Like a well, they held everything that had settled in him: joy, disappointment, and the pain of a generation that should never have had to live through a war, but did.

Strong coffee steamed in his hands, bitter as life itself. Its rich aroma lazily mixed with the smell of wet woods and decaying leaves. Coffee was his ritual, a prayer that required no words. But Makar seemed not to notice anything around him, submerged in the swarm of thoughts humming in his head like disturbed bees.

The house was still asleep. Ten children—adopted, taken in, yet his to the bone—breathed quietly in their sleep, finishing their childhood dreams. This was their fortress. Built from bricks of resolve and cemented with tenderness, it had risen not on a foundation of illusions but from the ruins of disappointment. Their dreams were not merely escape. They were repair, a reconstruction of an inner world someone had once tried to destroy. Makar and Natalia had no biological children of their own, but they had something larger: an immense, almost painful need to give a chance to those from whom fate—or the cruelty of adults—had taken that chance without explanation.

The first to join their family were "Ours"—six siblings rescued from sadistic parents. Every television channel had carried their story, and the footage still lived behind Makar's eyes. He remembered more than the images. He remembered the smell of that day, the trembling in his hands when he first embraced them, the silence in the room where they fell asleep without fear for the first time.

The eldest, Nazar, an eighteen-year-old cadet at a military academy, had the gaze of a grown man from whom childhood innocence had already been stripped. His face was almost always serious, nearly stern, and his posture carried the discipline and responsibility he had taken upon himself far too early. His silence was louder than words.

Beside him was seventeen-year-old Dmytro, a future agronomist, calm as a field after rain. He found comfort in working the soil—in its care, rhythm and quiet. His hands already knew how to heal the land and, perhaps, the soul.

Fifteen-year-old Olha, quiet and thoughtful, kept a diary—her private world, into which she admitted no one. Her words were seeds. They germinated in silence, but one day they might change the landscape.

Fourteen-year-old Yuliia lived in dreams of fashion and runways, sketching dresses with such passion that every drawing seemed to be a possible future. Her sketches hung on the walls like windows into another world, one in which beauty was not a luxury but protection.

Twelve-year-old Marharyta was a soul balanced on an edge—between violin, paint and wind. Her presence was like music: not always heard, always felt.

And the youngest girl, Lesia—or Oleksandra, as she was actually named—was ten and possessed the kind of gift no one can teach: an instinctive ability for art, sport, music. She seemed to glow from within. Her talent was not ambition. It was simply how she breathed.

Later, the sisters Adriana and Marta joined the family, seventeen and fifteen. Their story was bitter: first removed from their biological parents, then returned to an orphanage by a foster family that, after two years, gave them back without explanation. Their hearts already bore scars, yet perhaps because of that they had become both more flexible and stronger.

The last to arrive, shortly before the full-scale war, were two brothers: Maksym, fifteen, quiet and serious, a gifted baker whose ceremonial loaf became a symbol of the home; and nine-year-old Volodymyr, the youngest of all, once forgotten by an institution and now surrounded by attention, gentle and observant, everyone's favourite.

Yesterday's visit from an official in Kyiv had disturbed something deep inside Makar. Pompous, wearing a self-satisfied smile and a flawless expensive suit, the man had arrived with an entire camera crew to get "a nice picture." He shook Makar's hand with paternal warmth, scattered smiles in every direction, posed with the children, ate the pastries he himself had brought as a gift, and left. His smile was like lacquer: glossy, thin, cracking at the first touch of truth.

An hour later, social network Y—formerly known as Qwitter—was full of posts:

"Helping the Zhuk family! Building the future!"

"Even during the war, we are here for you!"

"Vote for us!"

But Makar knew the bitter truth. The same official whose words poured so sweetly from television screens had been among the chief lobbyists behind a law in the Verkhovna Rada allowing oligarchs to privatise thousands more hectares of Ukrainian land and continue the ruthless destruction of the Carpathian old-growth forests. His signature had been the first among those who enriched themselves amid explosions and deaths, selling hectares and conscience alike. Their faces never changed. Only their suits grew more expensive.

"Pickled cucumbers..." Makar muttered, almost soundlessly.

It was more than a metaphor. It was a verdict.

For over thirty years Makar had worked in communications. He had been a PR man, an adviser, and for six years an assistant to a member of parliament, overseeing social projects. He had been through both Maidans—2004 and 2014. Both times he had come away not with a sense of victory but with another crack in his soul. His faith was like glass: transparent and fragile. Every revolution left another fracture. He had sincerely believed in change, constructing ideal public "images" for politicians, only to watch each new government become a copy of the one before it.

After the Revolution of Dignity, he gave up. Stepped away. Together with Natalia, who had always believed in education rather than re-education, he moved to western Ukraine to build not political parties but a family. To raise people, not politicians. He wanted to be an example—not someone who talked, but someone who acted. No more "kitchen-table democracy." Something real, here and now. His home became his mandate. His children, his programme. His silence, his protest.

When the full-scale war came, he once again wanted to go to the front. They refused him: a father of many children had another front no less important. Besides, an old back injury no longer allowed forced marches. The family, formally a family-type children's home, was evacuated abroad, but a year later they returned. They could not live in someone else's country. Ukraine was not merely soil. It was pain that would not let go, and hope that could not be forgotten.

And now, while the war was still going on but already seemed to be approaching its end, the "cucumbers," as Makar contemptuously called them, began stirring again. Elections were in the air because international partners were insisting on them. The sale of places on party lists gathered speed. Oligarchs and new "heroes" enriched by the war began hunting for parliamentary seats. What had once been hidden was now passed off as honesty. Cynicism had evolved into strategy. Ideals had become merchandise. Truth was merely another tool of manipulation.

"A barrel of pickled cucumbers" was what Makar called the Verkhovna Rada. Whatever the barrel—old or new—once cucumbers went into it, they soaked up the same brine. Fresh ones became just like the sour ones. And the problem was not the cucumbers. It was the barrel.

This was more than criticism. It was his formula for systemic rot.

"Dad," a voice came from behind him. "You're thinking about politics again, aren't you?"

Oleksandra stood barefoot on the cold wooden porch, wrapped in a blanket, her hair tousled, her eyes worried. Her voice was quiet as an autumn leaf falling. Makar smiled, trying to warm her with his gaze. There was more wisdom in those eyes than in many adults he knew. And more trust than he deserved.

"No, Lesiunia. I'm thinking about all of you," he said, gently stroking her hair. "And why are you awake, my star?"

"Do you dream about the war?"

"I dream about the future. Go sleep a little longer, my star."

She nodded without a word and, before going inside, leaned her shoulder against him. Makar felt the touch like a vow. He had no right to remain silent. Not for himself. For them. The touch was a seal—invisible, unbreakable.

The thoughts would not leave him alone that day. He paced the house like a lion in a cage. His country's existence was threatened not only by an invasion of hostile lice, but by whole colonies of domestic nits multiplying rapidly within it.

When night came and the house sank into silence, Makar opened his laptop. His tired but determined face was lit by the screen. Social network Y hummed like an alarmed hive: posts, comments, memes, analysis. He opened an old profile, the same one he had once used during political campaigns, and began to write. Slowly. Each word like a bullet, each sentence like a blade.

It was not a post.

It was a manifesto.

It was a challenge.

It was a beginning.

"I'm tired of lies. Here's an idea: the No Name party. A party where every honest Ukrainian can become a member of parliament. Not through money. Not through connections. Through the right our Constitution gives us. Let Artificial Intelligence impartially select 450 people who will serve the people, not oligarchs. No populism. No bribes. Only justice. Enough pickled cucumbers. Time to grow something fresh."

He clicked Publish and leaned back in his chair as if he had just done something irreversible.

Or something necessary to stay alive.

His heart was pounding not with fear, but with hope—a hope he had long considered lost, like an old photograph unexpectedly found between the pages of a forgotten book.

"Dad," came a familiar voice behind him.

It was Adriana. Her eyes were shining, a phone in her hand.

"This is cool," she said. "But... are you sure? They'll say it's a utopia."

"Maybe it is," Makar said thoughtfully. "But I don't want you to live inside a lie."

His voice was calm, yet it carried the same resolve that had once taken him to the Maidan. Now that resolve was quiet, like water wearing away stone.

Without waiting for permission, Adriana was already sharing the post in several groups. Makar smiled. From that moment on, there was no road back. It was more than an action. It was a gesture capable of changing a trajectory—the first step into darkness when you do not know whether there is ground beneath your foot.

By the next day, millions had read his words. Among thousands of comments was one that made him freeze:

"@earonmarks: This could be bigger than a flight to Mars. DM me."

Natalia, who had just brought him tea, looked at the screen. Her face darkened. She was not afraid of people. She was afraid of the system. Because she knew it did not forget and did not forgive.

"You're doing it again," she said wearily. "Politicians don't forgive anyone who breaks their game. And this Marks... what does he want?"

Makar looked at her with the same tenderness he had carried since the first days of their life together. Her presence was his compass. Whenever the world tilted, she restored his balance.

"I think—I hope, Natalia—he wants the same thing I do. To prove the world can be better."

His words were not a slogan. They were a promise. Not only to her—to himself, to the children, to the country.

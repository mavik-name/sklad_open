THIRD CIRCLE


15. MIRROR

A mirror is not always glass.
Sometimes it is another person's phrase, tossed out casually, that suddenly lands exactly where you have refused for years to turn on the light.
And you stand there.
Grown. Intelligent. Experienced. Armed with arguments.
And somewhere inside, someone small suddenly asks:
"Is that about me?"
We do not like mirrors.
Not the kind where you can straighten your collar, tighten your face, and pretend everything is still holding together.
The kind that shows not wrinkles,
but motives.
Not tiredness,
but envy.
Not principle,
but fear that put on a suit and learned to speak in respectable words.
The hardest thing to look at is not sin.
Sin at least has a shape.
The hardest thing to look at is self-deception.
Because it always explains so well why you had every right to be exactly as you were.
Why you did not call.
Why you stayed silent.
Why you struck with a word.
Why you disappeared.
Why you called indifference respect for another person's freedom.
Why you hid pride under the word "dignity."
A mirror does not judge.
It simply refuses to take part in your defence speech.
It stands there and shows.
Without anger.
Without pity.
Without wanting to destroy you.
Maybe that is exactly why it is so unbearable.
Because you can still negotiate with a person.
Not with a mirror.

16. GUILT

Guilt is a heavy stone.
At first you put it in your pocket.
You think: I can carry it.
Then into a bag.
Then onto your shoulders.
And one day you discover that you are no longer walking.
You have simply arranged your life around the stone.
You wake with it.
Drink coffee.
Make jokes.
Work.
Write texts.
Answer: "I'm fine."
And you are barely even lying anymore.
Because a person can grow so accustomed to weight that they begin calling it posture.
There is honest guilt.
It comes after trust has been broken and says:
"Look. Don't turn away. You did this."
That guilt must be heard.
Not argued away.
Not talked over.
Not shifted onto circumstances, childhood, weather, nerves, or a difficult period.
Simply heard.
Because without that, a person does not grow up.
But there is another guilt.
Imposed.
Inherited.
Placed in the cradle beside somebody else's fears.
Guilt for existing differently from the way someone would have preferred.
For having your own voice.
For refusing to become a convenient extension of someone else's biography.
That guilt needs to be returned.
Carefully.
Without hysteria.
Without a grand speech.
Simply place it outside the door it once came through and say:
"This is not mine.
I will carry what belongs to me.
Take this back."

17. ANGER

Anger is not always a sin.
Sometimes it is the last living part of a person who has been taught for too long to be convenient.
It rises from inside like a fire in an abandoned house.
Frightening.
Burning.
Lighting what would be easier not to see.
Anger often arrives when love is already tired of knocking at a locked door.
When patience has stopped being a virtue and become a way of slowly disappearing.
When silence is no longer wisdom but participation in your own erasure.
I am not afraid of anger.
I am afraid of people who have made it their permanent address.
Because anger is a good signal.
And a bad landlord.
It can show you where the boundary is.
But it does not know how to build a home.
It can break down the door behind which you were being kept.
But it does not know where to go next.
Anger says:
"Enough."
Sometimes that is a holy word.
But afterwards you still have to learn other words.
"It hurts."
"You can't do that."
"I won't allow it."
"I'm leaving."
"I'm staying, but not as the person I was."
Because if anger is not translated into human language, it will begin speaking the language of ruins.
And then you may not notice when you start resembling the very thing you were defending yourself against.
Anger should be listened to.
But it should not be given the steering wheel on a slippery road.

18. LONELINESS

Loneliness does not always mean there is no one around.
Sometimes the room is full of people who know your name, your work, your habits, your favourite jokes, even the way you hold a cup.
And still none of them knows where it truly hurts inside you.
Loneliness is when you have already started a sentence and suddenly understand that explaining the whole road to this pain would take too long.
So you say:
"It's nothing. Forget it."
Loneliness is when someone asks, "How are you?"
and you calculate how much truth this conversation can survive.
Usually not much.
So we have learned to answer briefly.
"Fine."
That word may be the largest warehouse of unpreserved pain in human language.
Everything is stored there.
Exhaustion.
Fear.
Resentment.
Love that had no address.
A prayer no one heard.
And the small wish that somebody would not believe you.
That somebody would say:
"No. Not fine. Sit down. Talk."
But that does not happen often.
So people carry themselves alone.
And later wonder why, over the years, the soul begins to resemble a suitcase with the handle torn off.
Loneliness is not cured by a crowd.
It is cured by presence.
One person who is not in a hurry to fix you.

19. DIGNITY

Dignity is not a pose.
Not a chin raised so high you can no longer see the people in front of you.
Not coldness.
Not the ability to leave first and slam the door hard enough to shake the neighbours' conscience.
Dignity is a quiet boundary inside a person.
The place where they say:
"Up to here I can still endure.
Beyond this point, I begin betraying myself."
Dignity needs no audience.
It does not always look impressive.
Sometimes it is simply refusing to answer humiliation with humiliation.
Sometimes it is getting up from a table where for years you were fed love mixed with poison.
Sometimes it is asking forgiveness without theatre, without "but," without explaining that you actually meant something else.
Dignity is not never having fallen.
It is not beginning to live on the floor after you fell.
Not turning dirt into a philosophy.
Not convincing yourself that this is simply how things are supposed to be.
Dignity is the soul's memory of its own height.
Even when life very persistently invites you to bend down to the level of the skirting board.
Dignity does not shout:
"I am special."
It says quietly:
"I am human.
That is enough."

20. MEMORY

Memory is an unreliable witness.
It likes rearranging the furniture in the rooms of the past.
Brightening one face a little.
Darkening another.
Making you nobler than you were.
Making another person's cruelty sharper than honesty allows.
Memory does not lie directly.
It edits.
Cuts pauses.
Changes the angle.
Adds music where, in reality, there was only awkward silence.
That is why it is dangerous to trust your memories completely.
Especially the ones in which you are either flawless or utterly destroyed.
Life is rarely that tidy.
Memory is necessary.
Without it, a person becomes a room without windows.
But memory needs airing from time to time.
Old resentments must be carried out—things that lost their bodies long ago but still occupy half the flat.
You need to check whether that voice from the past truly has the right to command your life today.
Sometimes we are not living with people.
We are living with copies of them printed by memory on an old printer of pain.
Memory is not a court.
And not a temple.
It is an archive.
And archives, too, must occasionally dispose of documents whose validity has expired.

21. TENDERNESS

Tenderness is not weakness.
It is a very precise strength that knows where another person's skin is thin.
And does not press.
Tenderness is not always sweet.
Sometimes it is dry, brief, almost awkward.
"Did you eat?"
"Put on a hat."
"Call when you get there."
"I'm not asking questions, but I'm here."
There is no literary grandeur in these sentences.
But they contain much of what the world often rests on.
Tenderness is when you do not use your knowledge of another person's weakness against them.
When you do not open an old wound simply because you know exactly where it is.
When you could strike precisely but lower your hand.
Not from fear.
From love.
Tenderness is not needed only by children, women, old people, or those it is convenient to call vulnerable.
Tenderness is needed by everyone who has looked strong for too long.
Because strong people are often not protected.
They are used like furniture.
Like walls.
Like a bridge everyone crosses to the other bank, rarely asking whether it is cracking in the middle.
And then everyone is surprised when the bridge suddenly collapses.
Tenderness is not baby talk.
It is the ability to see a person before they break.
And to place your palm, in time, exactly where the world has begun to split at the seam.

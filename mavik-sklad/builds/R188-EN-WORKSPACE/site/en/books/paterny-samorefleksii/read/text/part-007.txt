SIXTH CIRCLE


36. THE FEAR OF LOOKING RIDICULOUS

A person is not afraid only of death.
Death, at least, is serious.
It wears a black suit, carries heavy silence, has official status, and the right to enter without knocking.
But laughter is a small hooligan.
It can jump out of somebody else's mouth at exactly the moment you finally dare to be real.
And that's it.
You are no longer the prophet of your small truth.
Not the man who was about to say something important.
Not the person holding something very fragile in their hands.
You are ridiculous.
And sometimes that is more frightening than pain.
Because pain can still be hidden.
Laughter puts you in the middle of the square with nothing but your heart.
No armour.
No explanations.
No chance to say:
"You misunderstood."
That is why we stay silent.
Not because we have no words.
Because we are afraid our words will fall at someone's feet like an awkward little bird.
We are afraid to say: "It hurts."
What if someone smiles?
We are afraid to say: "I love you."
What if someone turns it into a joke?
We are afraid to say: "I believe."
What if they look at us like someone who still carries a childhood toy in their pocket?
The fear of looking ridiculous turns the soul into a well-locked shed.
Everything is in there.
Old dreams.
Unfinished letters.
Songs no one heard.
Prayers that never learned to stand straight.
Texts a person never showed the world because the world might curl its mouth the wrong way.
But perhaps the ridiculous is exactly what is most alive.
Only the dead always look dignified.
Living things fall.
Blush.
Mix up their words.
Say too much.
Reach out a hand without knowing whether it will be pushed away.
Perhaps adulthood begins where you finally allow yourself to be a little ridiculous.
Not pathetic.
Not false.
Not a cheap clown at someone else's table.
Alive.
Someone who did not betray what was real in them merely because somebody in a corner might smile.

37. ENVY

Envy rarely arrives with its face uncovered.
It does not say:
"Good afternoon. I've come to spoil your soul."
It enters quietly.
Sits beside you.
Looks with you at somebody else's joy and whispers:
"Why not you?"
At first it almost sounds like justice.
Really—why not you?
You tried too.
Lost sleep too.
Carried big words inside you that nobody hurried to hear.
You had hands, a heart, a little talent, and a great deal of stubbornness too.
And then somebody else suddenly received the thing you had asked for so long that you had begun to feel ashamed of asking at all.
Envy does not hurt because of someone else's happiness.
Their happiness is not guilty of anything.
What hurts is the place where your own life failed to match the map inside you.
You thought there would be a city there.
There are weeds.
You thought a person would be waiting there.
There is a station after the last train.
You thought there would be recognition.
There is silence that does not even pretend to be interested.
Envy is an embarrassing wound.
You do not want to show it.
Because a decent person is supposed to be happy for others.
That is what people say.
And it is true.
But truth does not cancel the fact that sometimes another person's joy knocks on exactly the bone inside you that never healed properly.
Do not feed envy.
It is ravenous.
Give it an hour and it will ask for a day.
Give it a day and it will move into your eyes and teach you to see the world as a queue in which everyone is constantly cutting in front of you.
But pretending it does not exist is no good either.
Then it starts working underground.
Better to sit down with it at the table and ask:
"What exactly hurts inside me when I see somebody else's light?"
Because sometimes envy is not proof that you are vile.
It is a letter from the part of your soul that never got to happen.
An unpleasant letter.
Written in bad handwriting.
Poisonous in places.
But if you read it honestly, you may discover what you never really allowed yourself to ask of life.

38. INGRATITUDE

Ingratitude does not always look rude.
Sometimes it has a perfectly respectable face.
A person simply gets used to goodness.
Like water from a tap.
Turn it on—it flows.
Call—someone answers.
Arrive—someone receives you.
Fall—someone lifts you.
Betray yourself—and someone still stays beside you, pretending not to notice how ashamed you are.
We get used quickly to the people who protect us.
They become part of the furniture.
Like a table.
Like a lamp.
Like an old chair you can always throw your coat over.
Only when the chair suddenly breaks do we remember it held our tiredness for years.
Ingratitude is when goodness stops being a miracle and becomes a service.
When another person's care is received not as a gift but as something owed.
When someone no longer says thank you because somewhere inside they decided their pain issued them a lifetime licence to receive warmth.
Yes, life was hard for you.
Yes, you were not loved enough.
Yes, you carried a lot alone.
But that does not mean everyone who held out a hand now works in your private heaven without days off.
Ingratitude does not consume the person who was never thanked.
It consumes the ability to see.
You stop seeing the glass of water.
The late-night message.
The person who stayed silent after your sharp words, not because they had nothing to say, but because they decided not to finish you off on your bad day.
Gratitude is not a ceremony.
It does not require flowers, a card, solemn words, or the correct intonation.
Sometimes gratitude is simply not behaving as though another person's love was issued to you by the state at your registered address.
Sometimes it is noticing.
In time.
Before the person who was your quiet shore for so long becomes a distant line on the horizon.

39. POWER

Power does not begin in offices.
Not with seals.
Not with security outside the door.
Not with a microphone into which someone suddenly starts speaking in the voice of history.
Power begins where one person can change another person's breathing with a single look.
At home.
In the kitchen.
At the table.
In bed.
In a message deliberately left unanswered so the other person will suffer a little.
Power is knowing another person's weakness and deciding what to be:
a hand or a hook.
You can control someone without giving orders.
It is enough to teach them to fear your mood.
Enough to stay silent in exactly the right way until they begin apologising for things they never did.
Enough to give warmth in portions.
A little today.
Nothing tomorrow.
The day after—as a reward for correct behaviour.
And a person begins living not their own life, but the weather forecast of yours.
Domestic power is the most treacherous kind.
Because it is hard to point at.
It does not always shout.
Does not always hit.
Does not always leave bruises that can be photographed.
It simply moves into another person's nervous system and puts up a small throne there.
I am afraid of power without self-reflection.
Because every person has power over someone.
Over the one who loves them.
The one who depends on them.
The one who is weaker.
The one waiting for their word as permission not to hate themselves today.
The question is not whether you have power.
You do.
The question is whether you notice when your hand is no longer on a shoulder, but on a throat.
Love without respect quickly learns to command.
Care without boundaries changes clothes and calls itself control.
Being right without mercy becomes a regime.
And a person who never examines their power sooner or later begins calling somebody else's submission harmony.

40. RESENTMENT

Resentment is a good tenant only for the first week.
It arrives with a suitcase of pain, sits beside the door and says:
"I won't be long.
Only until you sort things out."
And you let it stay.
Because it really does hurt.
Because it really was unfair.
Because somebody said something, did something, failed to come, failed to hear, failed to choose you in the place where you had already set a chair in your mind for their love.
At first resentment feels like protection.
It warms you.
Strangely, but it does.
It has its own dark little stove.
You can sit beside it and replay the same moment again.
Here is where he said it.
Here is where I stayed silent.
Here is where I should have answered differently.
Here is where everything became clear.
Resentment loves cinema.
It edits the past without stopping.
Shows you the same scenes from different angles, adds new lines, raises the music, makes you a little cleaner and the other person a little worse than truth allows.
And with time it is no longer a guest.
It owns the place.
Rearranges the furniture.
Decides whom to let in.
Whom not to trust.
Where you should stay silent.
Whom to punish with your coldness even when that person has not been part of your life for a long time.
Resentment is dangerous because it gives a sense of moral superiority.
You were wounded—therefore you are right.
You suffered—therefore you are pure.
You were not understood—therefore you are now allowed to understand no one.
But a wound does not automatically make a person wise.
Sometimes it merely makes them very inventive in finding ways not to go on living.
Letting go of resentment does not mean saying nothing happened.
It did.
And it hurt.
And perhaps it left a mark forever.
But not every mark has the right to become a road.
One day you have to open the door and tell resentment:
"I do not deny the reason you came.
But I no longer allow you to be my internal Ministry of Truth."

41. REPENTANCE

Repentance is not the phrase "I'm sorry if I offended anyone."
It is not a phrase at all.
A phrase can be a beginning.
Or it can be scenery behind which a person quickly changes guilt into lighter clothes.
Real repentance begins where the word "if" is gone.
Not "if I hurt you."
I hurt you.
Not "if I was wrong."
I was.
Not "if that is how you felt."
No.
I acted in a way that made you have to feel it.
Repentance is when a person finally takes their hands off another person's pain and stops measuring it by their own convenience.
Because we often think:
if I did not mean to hurt you, the wound should be smaller.
No.
A knife does not become softer because the hand did not intend to go that deep.
Repentance does not like an audience.
In public it quickly becomes a performance.
With exactly the right tiredness in the eyes.
With pauses.
With a low voice.
With readiness to accept any punishment, especially if everyone can see how nobly you accept it.
Real repentance is often very ugly.
It sits on the edge of the bed.
Says nothing.
Does not know what to do with its hands.
It is no longer searching for the wording that will save face.
Because your face is not the main thing once you have finally seen what you did to another person's soul.
Repentance is not self-flagellation.
Self-flagellation can be proud too.
It says:
"Look how deeply I punish myself."
Repentance is quieter.
It asks:
"What do I have to change so I stop bringing people the same knife in different wrapping?"
Asking forgiveness matters.
But what matters even more is not turning forgiveness into a washing machine where you regularly throw your dirty behaviour.
Repentance is when a person returns not to the one they hurt demanding relief for their own soul.
But to themselves—with a shovel, a bucket, silence, and the long work of clearing the place inside them where the evil kept coming from.

42. THE INNER COURT

The inner court opens without a bell.
Without robes.
Without a state emblem on the wall.
Without a clerk monotonously reading out the case.
One day you simply can no longer postpone the hearing.
Everyone has arrived.
Memory—with folders.
Shame—with damp hands.
Pride—in an expensive suit, panic badly concealed in its eyes.
Guilt—silent.
It always sits closest to the exit.
Hope comes too.
Not as a lawyer.
Not as a witness for the defence.
It simply sits on the last bench so you will know:
the verdict does not necessarily have to be an execution.
The most frightening thing about this court is that you know every participant.
The prosecutor is you.
The accused is you.
The witness is you.
The one who spent years cleaning up the documents is you too.
And so is the one who still remembered the truth at night even while speaking very convincingly by day about how everything was more complicated than it looked.
The inner court does not ask whether you are good.
That question is too broad.
It asks more precisely:
where were you afraid?
Where did you stay silent?
Where did you call your fear of abandonment love?
Where did you disguise indifference as exhaustion?
Where did you turn a wound into an identification card authorising you to wound others?
Where were you right so cruelly that afterwards truth itself could not look people in the eye for a long time?
Beautiful phrases do not work in this court.
It has heard too many of them.
"I didn't mean to" does not work.
"Circumstances forced me" does not work.
"Everyone does it" does not work.
Maybe everyone does.
But not everyone is on trial today.
Today your name is on the door.
And still, the inner court does not exist to destroy a person.
Self-destruction is another way of escaping responsibility.
A beautiful one.
Dramatic.
With a black cloak and music.
Responsibility is much more boring.
It says:
"Stand up.
Admit it.
Repair what you can.
Do not repeat what you already understand.
And keep living without the right to pretend again that you did not know."
Perhaps that is why God does not always break us with truth.
Sometimes He simply removes all our lawyers from the room.
Leaves a chair.
Silence.
A mirror.
And a small light above the exit.
Not for escape.
For return.

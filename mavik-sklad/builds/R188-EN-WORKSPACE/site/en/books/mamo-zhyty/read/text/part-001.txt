Chapter 1: Room No. 7
The quiet morning in the pediatric oncology ward was broken only by the faint beeping of medical equipment and the occasional footsteps of nurses. Light filtering through the blinds drew pale stripes across the walls of Room No. 7, where Viktoriya, a twenty-eight-year-old woman with tired eyes still full of love, sat beside the bed of her little boy, Maryanchyk. He was only eight, and his small body had already been exhausted by the fight against medulloblastoma—an aggressive form of brain cancer that left almost no chance.
She carefully adjusted the blanket over her son as he slept fitfully. His light-brown hair, once thick and curly, had become thin and brittle after chemotherapy. She ran a hand over his pale cheek, feeling every hollow, every uneven place in that fragile child's skin. Suddenly the image of her husband, Dmytro, rose in her memory. He had been killed in the war while defending Ukraine from Russian aggression. Both of them had been far too young to die. A year earlier, when the news of his death had come, she had thought it was the greatest grief a person could survive. But fate had proved crueler, forcing her now to stand between life and death with her own child in her arms.
The door opened quietly, and nurse Olena appeared in the doorway—a young but already experienced woman with a gentle smile and eyes that had seen too much children's suffering without losing the ability to care. A mother of an adult daughter herself, she felt the pain of every parent in the ward and always tried to give them something to lean on. She had brought the morning medication.
"Good morning, Viktoriya. How is our Maryanchyk?" Olena whispered so she would not wake the boy.
"Morning, Olena. It was a hard night. He was nauseous again. But now he seems to have fallen asleep for a little while," she answered without taking her eyes off her son.
"I understand. We'll do everything we can to make him more comfortable," the nurse said, placing the medication on the bedside cabinet. She always tried to find words of support because she knew how difficult this ward was for parents. "Dr. Roman Andriyovych will be here soon. He wanted to speak with you."
Viktoriya's heart tightened. Every visit from Roman Andriyovych, the head of the department, carried anxiety with it. He was an excellent doctor, wholly devoted to his work. He had dedicated his life to fighting childhood cancer after losing his own sister to an incurable illness, and that loss had become his personal reason to fight for every child's life. His words were often careful, carrying a hidden pain that betrayed how hopeless some situations were.
A few minutes later Roman Andriyovych entered the room. Tall, silver beginning at his temples, he always wore a serious expression, though genuine concern showed in his eyes. Beside him stood a young resident, Anna Olehivna, who had only recently joined the team and was trying to absorb everything she could from the senior doctor.
"Good morning, Viktoriya," he said quietly, nodding in greeting. "How is Maryanchyk feeling?"
"Good morning, Roman Andriyovych. He's sleeping for now," she replied, rising from the chair.
The doctor approached the bed, examined the boy carefully, checked the readings on the monitor, then turned back to her.
"I need to speak with you in my office. We received some test results during the night," he said. His voice was gentle, but the seriousness in it had not changed.
A cold current ran through her body. She nodded, understanding that another wave was coming. She only stroked her son's hair, silently promising him she would fight until her last breath.
"I'll be right back," she whispered, leaving the room behind the doctor and the nurses, who watched her with sympathy. Every step felt heavy. She knew that on her shoulders rested not only her own fate, but the tiny and desperately precious life of her son.

Chapter 2: A Ray of Hope
Roman Andriyovych's office was filled with a silence that felt heavier than noise. Viktoriya sat opposite the doctor, hands clasped tightly in her lap. Anna Olehivna stood nearby, her face just as tense.
"Viktoriya, I won't hide anything from you," Roman Andriyovych began, his voice calm but firm. "Maryanchyk's condition is very serious. The tumor is progressing, and unfortunately the standard treatments are no longer producing the result we need."
Her heart dropped into an abyss. She had heard words like these before, but each time they sounded like a sentence being handed down. Tears rose into her eyes, but she blinked hard and forced them back. She had no right to weakness. Not now.
"But..." the doctor continued, and that one word sounded to her like a breath of air, "we are not giving up. I've consulted extensively with colleagues at leading clinics around the world, using every professional contact and every year of experience I have. There is one very new experimental treatment protocol being developed in Germany."
She froze, eyes fixed on him. Experimental? The word frightened her and gave her a tiny spark of hope at the same time.
"The protocol has not yet passed every stage of clinical testing, but the preliminary results in comparable cases are encouraging. It is our only chance, however faint," Roman Andriyovych explained. "We've contacted the clinic, and they are willing to take Maryanchyk. The treatment itself will be free for us thanks to a charitable foundation supporting the protocol."
A shadow of relief crossed her face, followed immediately by tension. Free? It sounded impossible, and she had long since stopped believing in miracles without a price.
"But there is one more 'but,'" the doctor said after a pause, looking at her with understanding. "If the protocol works, Maryanchyk will need long and very expensive rehabilitation. Months, perhaps years, of intensive work with physiotherapists, speech therapists, neuropsychologists. The cost will be significant. The foundation covers the treatment, but not the recovery afterwards."
Her breath caught. She was a widow. Her husband had been killed in the war, leaving her alone with a sick child. All their savings had long ago disappeared into previous stages of treatment. Where was she supposed to find that kind of money?
"We understand what an enormous burden this is," Roman Andriyovych added gently. "But we believe this chance is worth fighting for. We'll help with the documents and with arranging the trip. As for the money... perhaps there are charities, foundations, people willing to help."
Anna Olehivna, who had been silent until then, stepped forward. "Viktoriya, I've seen how Maryanchyk fights. He's a very strong boy. And so are you. We'll stay beside you however we can."
She looked at them—these people who had become a second family through this struggle. They gave her hope even when her own strength had almost run out. She felt something inside her harden into resolve again.
"I'll do everything I can," she said, quietly but firmly. "I'll find the money. I won't give up a single chance for my son."
She knew this would be the hardest battle of her life. A battle not only for Maryanchyk's survival but for his future. A battle in which she would have to search for help, knock on every door, ask, beg. But for her son she was ready to do anything.
As she left the doctor's office, she felt the full weight of responsibility settle on her shoulders. But now she had a goal. A ray of hope worth holding onto.

Chapter 3: A Hard Decision
She sat on a hard chair in the small parents' lounge adjoining the pediatric oncology ward. On the table, among empty cups and abandoned magazines, lay her notebook with a list written in a trembling hand. "My son's rehabilitation" stood at the top. Beneath it were figures that looked astronomical, impossible. She had spent the sleepless night turning every possible option over in her mind, and none of them seemed real. The boy was asleep in his room, and she could not leave the hospital. Her heart was there beside him.
The door opened quietly, and Anna, Viktoriya's older sister and the only close family she had left, appeared in the doorway. Their parents had been killed in the first days of the full-scale invasion while trying to flee their home in Bucha. That loss had drawn the sisters even closer and made them one another's support.
"You still haven't slept?" Anna asked softly as she hugged her. "How's the little one?"
"Sleeping. I can't sleep, Anya. I don't know what to do," Viktoriya said, pointing to the list. "It's an enormous amount of money. Where am I supposed to get it?"
Anna picked up the list, her face turning serious. She was practical, level-headed, always the one who could find a way through the most impossible situations.
"Let's think together," she said. "Maybe we can approach some of the large international foundations? Or organize a fundraising campaign online? I can help with that. I know volunteers."
They went through one option after another: sell the apartment—but where would they come back to? Take out a loan—but how would they repay it? Ask local business owners for help. Every idea had its own "but," its own risks, its own weaknesses. Hope flared and faded like a candle in the wind. Viktoriya closed her eyes, and bright memories rose before her: her son laughing without a care as they built towers of sand by the sea; his shout of triumph the first time he rode a bicycle without training wheels; his warm, tight hugs that could chase away any sadness. Those moments of pure childhood joy were the force driving her forward.
"What if... what if I joined the military?" she said suddenly. Her voice was barely audible but steady. Anna stared at her in disbelief.
"What? Vika, what are you talking about?" Alarm entered her voice. "That isn't a way out! You're a woman. You have a child who needs you!"
"That's exactly why," Viktoriya said, standing, her eyes burning with determination. "They pay. And they pay well. I could serve as a medic, or somewhere in the rear. I don't want to risk my life pointlessly, but he... he has to live. This rehabilitation is his chance at a normal life. I can't just sit here and wait for him to fade away."
Anna was silent, trying to absorb what she had heard. Her sister—frail, exhausted, and yet so strong—was ready to take such a desperate step.
"But who will stay with him?" Anna asked at last. "If you leave, who will take him to Germany? Who will be with him there?"
Viktoriya looked at her sister, pleading in her eyes. "You, Anya. You're the only person I can trust him to. You'll go with him. You'll be his mother there while I'm here earning money for his future."
Anna felt her heart tighten. She loved the boy as though he were her own, but the responsibility... it was enormous.
"I... I don't know, Vika," she whispered. "This is a very serious decision. What if... what if something happens to you?"
"It won't," Viktoriya said, stepping toward her and holding her tightly. "I'll come back. I have to come back. For my son. And you'll be his guardian angel there. You know I don't see another way. This is our only chance."
Tears filled Anna's eyes. She looked at her sister's exhausted but determined face and understood that Viktoriya would not back down. And she could not leave her alone in this fight.
"All right," Anna finally said with a small nod. Her voice was almost a whisper. "I'll go with him. I'll stay with him. But you... promise me you'll be careful. Promise me you'll come back."
Viktoriya hugged her more tightly. Pain, fear, hope, and an unstoppable resolve mixed inside her. She knew this decision would change all their lives forever. But for her son she was ready for anything.

Chapter 4: A New Front
The decision had been made. Hard, painful, but the only one that felt possible. Viktoriya wasted no time. The next morning, leaving her son in the care of Olena—the nurse who had become almost family—she went to the district Territorial Recruitment and Social Support Center. Her steps were firm, though everything inside her tightened with anxiety.
The enlistment process was slower than she wanted, but she was prepared for bureaucracy. She passed the military medical examination, where her physical condition, despite sleepless nights and constant stress, was judged satisfactory. The doctors reviewed her history carefully and found no contraindications to service. When one of them asked about her motivation, she answered simply: "To protect my child. To earn the money for his life." There was no rhetoric in her voice, only an unbreakable determination that seemed to impress even the military doctors, who had already heard every kind of story.
Then came the recruiting center of a combat brigade actively looking for specialists. Viktoriya told them about her work as a wedding videographer.
"I know cameras. I know how to shoot from height. I know how to fly drones," she explained, surprising the officer who had been looking skeptically at her slight build. "Before the war I filmed weddings. Now I'm ready to film the enemy—to conduct reconnaissance, record positions, correct fire."
At the mention of drone skills, the officer visibly became more interested. The need for qualified UAV operators was enormous. After a short interview and document check, she received her assignment. She was designated a drone operator. This was her new front, her new way to fight for her son's life.
A few days later she was already at a training center. It was a completely different life. Early wake-ups, physical drills, exhausting lessons in tactics, camouflage, communications. And, of course, combat drone training. The hands that had once guided a camera to catch the best angle for happy couples now learned to grip a controller. The eyes that had searched for perfect light in a wedding video now learned to distinguish targets on a screen, enemy positions from civilian objects.
It was hard. Physically and emotionally. She missed Maryanchyk until her chest hurt—every breath, every weak but familiar smile. She remembered him running through grass chasing butterflies, the two of them building sandcastles on the beach. Every night before sleep she looked at the photograph she kept under her pillow and repeated to herself: "For you, sweetheart. For you, I'll endure anything."
Around her were people driven by reasons of their own—men and women who had left their civilian lives to defend the country. They became her new family, her support. She felt she was not alone. She was part of something larger, something terribly important. And that gave her the strength to keep moving, one step at a time, toward the only goal that mattered: save her son, and come back to him alive.

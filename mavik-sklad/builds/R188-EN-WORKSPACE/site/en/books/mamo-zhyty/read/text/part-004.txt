Chapter 13: A Year of Waiting
And so a year passed.
A year filled with thousands of small victories and countless invisible tears Anna shed at night when the boy was asleep. Every day was a challenge, but also a small miracle.
Maryanchyk had almost fully recovered from the terrible illness that once seemed unbeatable. His body, once exhausted and fragile, had grown stronger. Childish curiosity had returned to his eyes, along with the small sparks of mischief that delighted her so much. His pallor gave way to healthy color. He could run again, laugh again, even get into trouble now and then like any eight-year-old boy who had almost forgotten pain.
Every day of rehabilitation was another step toward an ordinary life, and Anna poured everything she had into it—her soul, her love, every nerve in her body. She had been his hands, his legs, his voice when he could not manage for himself, and his unshakable faith when her own faith faltered.
In the autumn he was supposed to start at a German school. For both of them, it was enormous. A symbol of beginning again. A promise that the future they had fought so desperately to build might actually arrive.
But there was still no news of her sister, and that silence was the worst thing of all.
It was heavier than bad news because it left room for every nightmare imagination could produce.
Contact with the Ukrainian military was difficult. Diplomatic requests disappeared into bureaucratic corridors and returned with nothing. Anna felt isolated in a foreign country, suspended in a vacuum. There was no way for her to take part in the search for information. Her hands were tied.
Her only source was the occasional call from volunteers who sometimes passed along meager, unverified rumors. Those rumors did not help. They only deepened the uncertainty and ate at her from within.
There was no family left back home. Their house was destroyed. Their parents were dead. The anchor that might have held them there no longer existed.
All the bridges had burned.
Anna understood that their life was here now, in Germany, with all its difficulties and all its possibilities.
Through that entire year, Maryanchyk remembered his mother.
Her face, her voice, her hands—all of it remained alive in his child's memory despite the passing time and the new world around him. He remembered her laughter filling their small home. Her gentle hand stroking his hair. Her bedtime stories, where goodness always won in the end.
Those memories were treasures he carried inside himself.
Anna had, in practice, become a mother to him—the closest person in his life, his friend, his support. But he still remembered Viktoriya's face, the feel of her touch, even the smell of her somewhere deep in memory, warm and familiar.
He often told Anna about those memories: how he and his mother had gone to the zoo and fed giraffes; how she had taught him to ride a bicycle, holding tightly to the back of the seat; how she hugged him before sleep and promised she would always be there.
Every story tore Anna's heart open because she knew how much pain lived beneath those bright memories, and how hard it was for him to live without his mother.
Viktoriya came to him in dreams almost every night.
Sometimes she was there beside him, holding him, reading books, her face as vivid and alive as in old photographs.
Other nights she disappeared into smoke, called for help, her voice full of despair, and the boy woke in tears with a scream on his lips.
Yet even after those nightmares he believed the nightmare of her captivity would end.
He believed in a miracle.
He believed she would come back because his mother had promised.
That child's faith was both blessing and curse for Anna. She knew how cruel reality could be, and how devastating it would be if that faith proved empty.
Every night she prayed that his faith would be stronger than whatever truth she might one day have to tell him.

Chapter 14: An Unexpected Meeting
Despite every difficulty and the constant anxiety over her sister, Anna's life in Germany slowly settled into a rhythm.
Her days were filled with caring for her nephew, his rehabilitation, and her work at the Schwarz estate. She learned to live inside that new rhythm and to find small happiness in the boy's progress, his smiles, his growing strength.
But loneliness could still become unbearable—especially in the evenings after Maryanchyk fell asleep and the Schwarz house sank into silence.
During one of their regular visits to the rehabilitation center, where Maryanchyk was working with a physiotherapist, Anna happened to meet Serhiy Krupovych.
He was a Ukrainian soldier, a frontline reconnaissance scout recovering from a serious wound. Scars covered his face, and his eyes looked tired, but an unbroken will still lived in them. He moved on crutches, yet held himself upright with quiet dignity.
Their first conversation was only a few words about Ukraine and the pain they shared.
Serhiy often saw Anna with Maryanchyk and noticed her patience, the boundless love with which she cared for the boy. He watched her help him through difficult exercises, read to him, and light up whenever Maryanchyk made another small step toward recovery.
Before long they began spending a great deal of time together.
Serhiy spoke about the front, his comrades, the difficulty of adjusting to peaceful life after living through hell. Anna listened and felt his stories echo inside a soul already full of fear for her sister.
She told him about her nephew, his progress, her hopes and fears.
In one another's company they found comfort. Understanding. Something both of them had been missing in a foreign country.
One day they were sitting in the rehabilitation center cafe when Maryanchyk ran toward them holding a drawing.
"Aunt Anya, look what I drew!" he shouted, handing her a colorful picture of a large family.
Next to him was a woman with long hair labeled "Mom Vika," and then another figure: "Aunt Anya."
Serhiy looked at Anna in surprise.
"Aunt Anya? I thought he was your son..."
Heat rushed into her face.
She had never hidden the fact that Maryanchyk was her nephew, but she had never made a point of explaining it either.
Now the time had come.
Anna drew a deep breath, looked directly into Serhiy's eyes, and began telling him the truth.
She told him about her sister. About Viktoriya's decision to join the military. About their parents, killed in the war. About Maryanchyk's illness and surgery. About the day Viktoriya disappeared.
She told him everything she had carried alone for so long, and as she spoke, she felt an enormous weight begin to lift from her.
Serhiy listened in silence, his expression growing more serious with every sentence.
He was struck by Viktoriya's strength, her sacrifice, her desperate fight for her son.
But even more, he was struck by Anna herself—her endurance, her endless love for the boy, her willingness to carry such a burden.
He no longer saw only a woman sitting across from him.
He saw a fighter holding a different, invisible front.
When she finished, he reached out and gently took her hand.
"Anna... I don't know what to say. You... you're an extraordinary woman."
There was no pity in his eyes. Only deep understanding and respect.
Something new moved through her then, something warm.
Support.
Recognition.
The understanding she had been missing for so long.
She looked at his tired but strong face, and their eyes met like two souls that had passed through hell and somehow not lost the ability to love.
And so, within the walls of a German rehabilitation center, among pain and hope, a relationship began between them.
For both of them it became a ray of light inside the darkness—a promise that life might still begin again, and that perhaps they could find refuge and strength in each other.

Chapter 15: Saying Goodbye to Hope
Days became weeks, weeks became months.
Their relationship deepened with each passing day, becoming an island of calm and understanding in the rough sea of their lives.
They saw one another every day—at the rehabilitation center, where Maryanchyk continued his therapy, or at a small cafe nearby where they could simply talk, exchange thoughts, share what hurt.
For Anna, Serhiy became not only the man she loved, but a friend, a support, someone who understood her pain without forcing her to explain it.
He was also her living connection to Ukraine, to the world she had left but that continued to live inside her.
His own rehabilitation was reaching its successful end.
He could walk confidently without crutches now, though he still limped. His face, once exhausted and gaunt, looked healthier, and the old determination had returned to his eyes.
Anna watched him gradually come back to life and was happy for him.
At the same time, fear grew inside her, because she knew what recovery meant.
It meant the moment of his return to the front was getting closer.
One evening, after Maryanchyk had fallen asleep in his hospital bed, Anna and Serhiy sat together in her small apartment on the Schwarz property.
Trees rustled quietly outside the window. Inside, the room held a warm stillness broken only by their breathing.
They were close, their bodies entwined, their sense of one another almost painful in its intimacy.
Every touch, every kiss held not only desire but the knowledge of how fragile their happiness was.
His strong arms closed around her. She felt the warmth of his skin, heard the quickened beat of his heart. Her fingers traced the scars that marked the hell he had survived, and to her those scars seemed beautiful because they were part of him.
Their mouths met in a long hungry kiss that stole breath and thought until only closeness remained.
In moments like that she could forget the war, her sister, the boy's illness.
There were only the two of them. Their tenderness. Their love—so unexpected, so necessary it felt like air.
"Serhiy," Anna whispered against him. "I'm so afraid. For you... for Vika... for us."
He held her tighter.
"I know, Anya. This war takes everything we have. But we have to hold on. For the people beside us, and for the people waiting."
"What happens next?" Her voice trembled. "You'll go back to the front and I'll still be here. How are we supposed to survive that?"
"We will," he said firmly. "We've already survived so much. We're strong. And we'll find Vika. I promise you, Anya. When I get back to Ukraine, I'll do everything I can. I have contacts. I know where to look."
A tiny spark of hope stirred inside her.
His words, his certainty, gave her something to hold onto.
"I believe you, Serhiy. I just... I need that belief so much."
"Anya..." His voice roughened with emotion. He stroked her hair gently. "There's something I have to tell you."
Her heart tightened.
She knew what was coming. She had known for a while and had kept pushing the thought away, trying to live inside each moment they still had.
"I'm going back to Ukraine," he said.
The words landed like a sentence even though she had expected them.
"My rehabilitation is over. They need me there. My men are waiting. I can't sit here while people are dying there, while my country is burning."
Tears filled Anna's eyes.
She pulled closer to him, as though holding him tighter might hold the moment together.
"Serhiy... no. Please..." Her voice shook. "You've only just recovered. You've been through so much. Stay. Stay with me. With him. He loves you so much."
Serhiy eased back just enough to look into her eyes.
His face held pain, but also a determination she knew she could not move.
"Anya, I can't be anything else. I'm a reconnaissance soldier. It's my duty. I won't be able to live peacefully knowing my comrades are there and I'm here. I have to go back and fight. For Ukraine. For our future. So Maryanchyk can live in a peaceful country."
He saw her pain and her tears, and it tore at him.
But his decision was final.
He was a soldier, and in his own mind his place was still at the front.
"I'll come back, Anya. I promise," he whispered, kissing her forehead. "I'll do everything I can to come back to you. To both of you. Just wait for me."
Anna said nothing. Tears ran down her cheeks.
She understood him. She had already seen what war takes and what it makes of people. She knew he could not choose differently.
But the pain of losing his daily presence was unbearable.
She was letting go of another person she loved, another ray of light in her life.
That night was full of tenderness, pain, and bitter farewell.
Her heart seemed split between love for him and fear for his life.
But she knew she had to let him go.
Fighting for his country was his choice, his road.
Her road was to wait.
And to continue fighting for the boy.

Chapter 16: A Bitter Farewell and a Child's Hope
The morning of Serhiy's departure was gray, as though the sky were grieving with them.
Anna, Serhiy, and Maryanchyk stood outside the rehabilitation center. His suitcase waited beside him, ready for the long road back to war.
The air was full of words none of them could say, full of fear and bitterness.
Over the past year Maryanchyk had become deeply attached to him.
For a boy growing up without a father, Serhiy had become his own "Uncle Serhiy"—the man who always found time to play, told stories, listened seriously to a child's dreams.
Despite everything Maryanchyk had survived, a child's innocence still lived in his bright eyes, keeping the full cruelty of the adult world at a distance.
"Uncle Serhiy, will you find my mom?" he asked suddenly, looking up with large trusting eyes. "And Dad too? Aunt Anya says they're somewhere far away, but you're a scout. You can find them, right?"
Serhiy felt his heart tighten.
He looked at Anna standing nearby. Her face was pale, her eyes filled with pain beyond words.
He knew the boy had not been told the whole truth.
Maryanchyk believed his father had simply "gone missing" or perhaps "been captured," like his mother.
In reality Dmytro had been killed near the beginning of the war. His body had never been recovered, and officially he remained missing in action.
Anna had never been able to give the boy another blow like that—not while he was fighting for his own life.
"I'll... I'll do everything I can, Maryanchyk," Serhiy said, his voice rough.
He dropped to one knee so they were eye to eye and hugged the boy tightly.
He felt the small body press into him, searching for protection and warmth.
Inside that embrace was all the pain of a child who desperately needed a father's love, a man's solid presence.
Serhiy held him close, feeling both his fragility and the stubborn faith inside him.
And then Serhiy's own memories broke through the walls he had spent so long building around them.
He remembered the day his life split into before and after.
The city burning under shelling.
His house reduced to ruins.
The smell of smoke and dust. People screaming.
And the moment he found them—his wife, Olena, and their little daughter Mariyka—beneath the wreckage.
Their faces were damaged, fear still frozen in their eyes.
He had held their cold hands and felt his world collapse around him.
He had promised himself then that he would take revenge, that he would keep fighting so no other child would have to survive such grief.
That pain, that loss, was his private hell, a hidden cross he carried everywhere.
"You're so strong, Maryanchyk," he whispered, forcing those memories back. "You're going to be very happy. And I'll never forget you."
He released the boy and stood.
His eyes met Anna's.
Love, fear, and unspeakable sadness were all there.
"Be careful, Serhiy," she whispered.
"You too, Anya. Take care of yourself and the little one."
He hugged her tightly one last time, feeling the warmth of her body and her tears against his shoulder.
"I'll come back. I promise."
Then he turned and walked away without looking back.
Anna watched until his figure disappeared around the corner.
Maryanchyk stood pressed against her leg and waved, his child's faith still too young to understand what a real goodbye could mean.
Her heart tightened with pain, but she knew she had to stay strong.
For the boy.
For her sister.
And for the man walking back toward death and destruction to fight for the future all of them still hoped to share.

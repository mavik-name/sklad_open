CHAPTER 8. SIX MONTHS OF SILENCE

After the reading night, Artur expected life to become easier to understand.
That was a mistake.
Life did not become easier to understand. It simply moved on to the next level of difficulty and pretended that had been the plan all along.
On Sunday he woke at eleven with the feeling of a man whose body had slept while his brain had spent the whole night sitting beside the laptop, waiting to find out whether Olya approved of the third paragraph.
Two empty cups stood on the table.
A throw lay on the sofa.
His novel was open on the laptop.
And on the sticky notes — someone else’s handwriting, which in a single night had done more for the text than dozens of polite promises to “read it at the weekend”.
Artur picked up one of the notes.
“The text is alive. Don’t hide it.”
He stared at those words for a long time.
He used to want someone to say, “You’re a genius.”
Now, for some reason, “the text is alive” carried more weight.
Because genius is convenient. You can leave a genius untouched. Put him in an imaginary museum and get angry that people are not buying tickets.
Something alive has to be fed, let out, treated, taught to walk and, sometimes, simply allowed to breathe.
That was much more troublesome.
He opened his email.
The automatic reply from the first publishing house was still sitting in his inbox like silence in official form.
“Manuscript review time: three to six months.”
Artur reread the sentence.
Three months sounded like a test.
Six sounded like literature checking whether the author would dissolve into everyday life before it answered.
He wrote to Olya:
“Three to six months of waiting. Is that normal?”
She replied a few minutes later:
“For publishers — yes. For an author’s nervous system — no.”
“What do I do?”
“Don’t stare into your inbox like it’s a well. The water won’t appear any faster.”
Artur smiled.
Then she added:
“And don’t start rewriting everything you’ve already sent. The submission is out. Let it do its job.”
That mattered.
Somewhere deep inside him, a panic-stricken editor had already woken up and wanted to recall the email, replace the blurb, rewrite the biography, rename the file, add a comma, remove a comma and send an apology for existing at all.
Artur wrote:
“So don’t touch it?”
Olya:
“Don’t touch it out of panic. Those are different things.”
“What about out of reason?”
“Reason is allowed. In measured doses.”
Artur put down his phone.
Then opened the spreadsheet “Publisher_Submissions”.
Over the past few days it had begun to resemble the control room for his hopes.
Publisher.
Contact.
Requirements.
Date sent.
Response.
Comments.
The first row was already filled in.
The second was waiting.
So was the third.
Artur looked at his three novels in the “LITERATURE” folder.
Once, they had seemed like three pieces of evidence that the last few years of his life had not been wasted.
Now they looked like three grown children who finally had to be let out of the flat, without him standing at the door straightening their collars.
He began preparing submissions for other publishers.
Not chaotically.
Not resentfully.
Not with a letter whose subtext read, “Please save me from my own internal sarcasm.”
Professionally.
A separate letter for each publisher.
A different emphasis for each.
Here — the psychological line.
There — the social conflict.
Elsewhere — the contemporary voice.
A strong protagonist for one.
An ethical choice for another.
Artur suddenly felt less like a traitor to art and more like a translator.
He was translating his novel into the language of people who not only had to love literature, but also count print runs, risks, shelf space, logistics, and whether a stranger would choose to pull out a wallet instead of a phone.
It still annoyed him.
But it no longer humiliated him.
That evening he sent three more emails.
The next day — two.
Then another one.
Then he submitted the second novel to two publishers, the third to three, and the first to several more.
After a week the spreadsheet looked serious.
Artur looked at it and said aloud:
— My novels have more of a social life than I do now.
His phone flashed.
Denys:
“So, Tolstoy, conquered the publishing market yet?”
Artur:
“Sent submission packages to ten editorial teams.”
Denys:
“Wow. You’re not a writer anymore. You’re a logistics manager for spirituality.”
“Very funny.”
“Thank you. I’m working on the personal brand of the protagonist’s best friend.”
Artur put down the phone and looked at his inbox.
Empty.
Not exactly empty.
There were promotional emails.
A message from the bank.
A discount on trainers.
An offer to take a course called “How to Write a Book in 21 Days”.
Artur opened it out of professional hatred.
The course promised:
“Have you always dreamt of writing a book but never known where to start?”
Artur looked at his three finished novels.
— I knew where to start. I didn’t know how to make anyone reach the end.
The course promised “structure”, “motivation”, “a sellable concept”, “an author journey” and “unpacking your expertise”.
The last phrase made Artur feel as though somewhere inside the language a zip had broken.
He closed the email.
But the word “structure” stayed behind.
It used to make him suspicious. As though someone wanted to walk into a novel with a tape measure and say, “You’ve got too much soul per square metre here.”
But after the blurb, the synopsis, the letters, Olya, the bar, the spreadsheet, the automatic replies and self-publishing priced like a car, structure no longer looked like the enemy.
It looked like an unpleasant but useful tool.
Like a dentist.
Artur opened ChatGPT.
“I have three novels submitted to publishers. The waiting time is up to six months. What should an author do during that period?”
ChatGPT replied quickly, as though it had been waiting for exactly this:
“While waiting for responses from publishers, an author can work on a new text, develop their author presence, research the market, prepare materials for future submissions, and avoid fixating on the wait for a response.”
Artur reread the last point.
— Thank you, digital Buddha. Very specific.
He typed:
“Is working on a new text a betrayal of the novels I’ve already written?”
ChatGPT replied:
“No. Working on a new text does not devalue previous ones. On the contrary, it reflects an author’s professional development. Waiting for responses from publishers should not block the creative process.”
Artur leaned back in his chair.
Should not block the creative process.
It sounded sensible.
And dangerous.
Because somewhere inside, he had been counting on having earned the right to wait now. Ceremonially. Slightly tragically. Like a man at a railway station who refuses to board another train because his destiny has not yet arrived at the platform.
But apparently destiny did not like being waited for with hands in pockets.
He wrote to Olya:
“AI says I should work on a new text.”
Olya:
“Smart machine.”
“You don’t mind?”
“Why would I mind?”
“Well, we read the first novel.”
“So? The first novel won’t die because you start a fourth.”
Artur looked at the sentence.
Then wrote:
“It feels as though I’m betraying it.”
Olya did not reply immediately.
Then:
“You don’t betray a text by writing the next one. You betray yourself when you turn waiting into a way of standing still.”
Artur stared at the screen for a long time.
Then opened a new document.
The cursor blinked.
The blank page looked at him without the bureaucratic self-importance the synopsis page had possessed.
This time there was something different in it.
Not a demand.
An invitation.
Artur saved the file.
At first he wanted to call it:
“Novel_4”
Then added:
“skeleton”
It became:
“Novel_4_skeleton”
It was strange.
Almost indecent.
As though he were assembling a wardrobe rather than starting a novel.
Artur typed the first line:
“Protagonist:”
And stopped.
He had begun the previous three novels differently.
With an image.
With a sentence.
With pain.
With a line that arrived in the middle of the night and suddenly demanded four hundred pages for itself.
And here — “Protagonist:”.
Dry.
Unromantic.
Almost office-like.
He was about to close the file when he remembered Olya’s words:
“Don’t hide the text.”
Then Denys’s:
“If you can’t say it in simple words, maybe you’re afraid to understand what it’s actually about.”
Artur returned to the document.
He wrote:
“Protagonist: a man who has learned to lose beautifully but has not learned how to act after a loss.”
He looked at it.
Smiled.
— Dangerously autobiographical.
Then below:
“What he wants: to prove he did not waste his life on a dream.”
“What stands in his way: he confuses loyalty to the dream with refusing to change.”
“The protagonist’s inner lie: if the text is strong enough, the world will find it on its own.”
Artur stopped.
It was too close.
Not to the fictional protagonist.
To him.
He got up, poured himself some water, came back.
Looked at the document.
Once he would have recoiled from an approach like this. A real novel, he would have thought, could not be broken into bullet points like a presentation for management.
But now he saw something else.
The bullet points were not writing the novel for him.
They were simply asking questions.
Uncomfortable ones.
Useful ones.
The same questions a good editor asks, only without the green dress and tired smile at five in the morning.
Artur wrote:
“Truth the protagonist has to reach: a template can be used not to become like everyone else, but to learn how to be yourself more precisely.”
He reread it.
It was not a novel yet.
It was a skeleton.
Dry, awkward, without skin, without voice, without breath.
But a skeleton is not death.
It is what holds a body up.
The phone rang.
Denys.
Artur answered.
— Busy? — Denys asked.
— Started the fourth novel.
A pause.
— Sorry to interrupt the reproductive function of literature.
— Very funny.
— No, seriously. You sent three novels out into the world and you’re already having a fourth. Your dreams reproduce faster than some people we know get married.
— I’m not writing it yet. I’m building the skeleton.
— The skeleton?
— Yes.
— So you’ve finally admitted a novel ought to have a spine before it starts complaining about the world?
— You could put it that way.
— Olya’s influencing you.
— Not just her.
— Wow. We’ve got group therapy now.
Artur looked at the document.
— You know, I used to hate templates.
— You still hate them. You’re just less helpless about it now.
— Maybe.
— Oh, your “maybe” is back?
— No. This one’s operational.
— All right. What’s the fourth novel about?
Artur hesitated.
He had never liked talking about unfinished work. He felt an idea had to be hidden until it became strong enough to survive another person’s gaze.
Now he thought: perhaps that was exactly why some ideas grew up so lonely.
— It’s about a man who realises the world isn’t going to become less templated, — Artur said. — So he decides to use the template as a training ground.
— Training ground for what?
— Professionalism.
Denys was quiet for a moment.
— Sounds sensible. Where are the fights, sex and betrayal?
— Denys.
— I’m just asking as a representative of the market.
— There’ll be conflict.
— Conflict with the template?
— With himself.
— Cheapest conflict to produce. No sets required.
Artur laughed.
— But seriously, — Denys said. — I like it. You used to write as though the world were a judge who finally owed you an acquittal. Now it sounds as though you’ve walked out of court and opened a workshop.
Artur fell silent.
— That’s a good line, — he said.
— Don’t write it down. I’m starting to charge intellectual rent.
— I’ll write it down without attribution.
— And that is exactly how a literary career begins.
After the call Artur returned to the document.
He added a few more points.
“First turn: the protagonist accepts the rules of the game he despised.”
“Midpoint: he gets the result he wanted, but sees that he could lose his voice.”
“Final dilemma: use the template as a mask or as a tool.”
Then stopped.
He sat in front of the skeleton of the fourth novel and did not feel like a traitor to art.
He felt like a craftsman.
Once, that word had seemed lesser to him than “artist”.
Now he was no longer sure.
An artist could wait for inspiration.
A craftsman came to the table even when inspiration was stuck in traffic.
An artist could resent being misunderstood.
A craftsman asked, “Where exactly did the reader get lost?”
Artur opened ChatGPT.
“Can a template be useful to a strong author?”
The answer was predictably balanced:
“Yes. A template can be useful not as a limitation but as a tool for checking structure. An experienced author can use a template to identify weak points, organise material and strengthen the dramatic structure without losing their own style.”
Artur read it.
— Sometimes you sound like an oven manual. But there’s sense in it.
He rewrote it for himself:
“A template is not the text. It’s a gym for the text.”
Thought about it.
Added:
“But you shouldn’t live at the gym.”
The next few days settled into a new rhythm.
Morning — work.
Daytime — corporate emails, customer journeys, calls in which grown adults seriously discussed “the user’s emotional experience” as though the user were a houseplant.
Evening — the fourth novel.
Not pages.
Not yet.
The skeleton.
Artur studied his own idea not as a prophet but as a bridge engineer who knows that if the structure collapses, the poetry of the span will save no one.
Olya wrote sometimes.
Not every day.
That was good.
Daily messages would immediately have turned everything into too fast a genre, and Artur was beginning to value things that did not accelerate merely because they could.
One evening she wrote:
“How’s number four?”
Artur:
“So far it looks like a skeleton in a spreadsheet.”
Olya:
“That’s normal. A skeleton isn’t the enemy of the body.”
“I’m not used to it.”
“Because before, you wanted a novel to be born with a voice straight away. Sometimes it’s born with a spine first.”
Artur:
“Have you and Denys formed some sort of spine conspiracy?”
Olya:
“The spine is an underrated part of romance.”
Artur smiled.
Then wrote:
“Can you look at the skeleton when I’m no longer ashamed of it?”
Olya:
“I can look when you are ashamed too. Shame is often more honest than pride.”
He did not reply straight away.
It was one of those sentences he did not want to ruin with a quick joke.
So he simply wrote:
“All right.”
And went back to the file.
By the third week of waiting, Artur had almost stopped checking his inbox every half-hour.
Almost.
That “almost” mattered.
Total victory over email was impossible. An author waiting for a publisher’s reply always has a small dog inside him sitting by the door.
At least this dog no longer barked at every promotional email.
The publishers were silent.
The world did not collapse.
The novels did not disappear.
The fourth was slowly assembling itself.
And one evening Artur realised silence came in different forms.
There is the silence in which you are being ignored.
There is the silence in which you punish yourself by waiting.
And there is workshop silence.
Nothing applauds in it.
No one says, “Bravo.”
No one leaves a like.
There is simply a table.
A screen giving off light.
And you do your work.
Artur opened “Novel_4_skeleton” and, for the first time, wrote not a bullet point.
A sentence.
The first sentence of the fourth novel.
He did not know whether it was good.
He did not ask ChatGPT.
He did not send it to Olya.
He did not show Denys.
He simply left it on the page.
Let it live for a while.
And that was new too.
Before, he had needed immediate confirmation that his existence was not pointless.
Now he was beginning to understand: not every sentence has to walk onto the stage at once.
Some have to learn how to stand first.
Artur closed the laptop sometime around two in the morning.
The inbox was silent.
The editorial teams were silent.
Three novels waited for their readers somewhere in other people’s inboxes.
And the fourth had just taken its first step.
For the first time in a long while, Artur did not feel that silence meant the absence of an answer.
Sometimes silence was space.
And space could be filled.
Not with complaint.
Not with waiting.
Not with resentment towards a world that had once again failed to appreciate him on schedule.
With work.
Artur turned off the light.
In the darkness the room grew calmer.
A notebook lay on the table.
On the first page of a new spread he had written:
“A template is a training ground. Voice is what survives the training.”
Artur looked at the sentence and did not cross it out.
By morning it might seem pompous.
Or it might turn out to be true.
He decided to give it the night.

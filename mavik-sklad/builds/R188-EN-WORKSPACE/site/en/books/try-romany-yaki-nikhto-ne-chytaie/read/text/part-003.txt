CHAPTER 3. A SEVEN-HUNDRED-CHARACTER BLURB

Artur quickly understood: writing a novel is like building a house. Writing a blurb is like explaining that house to someone who has three seconds, is in a bad mood and already lives somewhere else.
He was sitting at the kitchen table in his flat on the twenty-first floor of a Kyiv residential complex, staring at a document called:
“Blurb_attempt_1”
There was one sentence on the screen:
“He thought the worst thing was losing himself. But it turned out to be far worse to realise that the world had not even noticed.”
Artur liked the sentence.
Which was precisely why he did not trust it.
Writers suffer from a dangerous illness: if the author likes a sentence too much, it may either be genuinely good or simply his favourite way of lying to himself.
Artur read it aloud.
— Fine.
Then in the voice of an imaginary publisher:
— And what am I supposed to do with that?
Then in the voice of someone opening the email on the metro between stations:
— Too much pathos. Next.
He deleted the sentence.
Then pressed Ctrl+Z.
The sentence came back.
— Don’t celebrate, — Artur told the monitor. — You’re still on probation.
He opened ChatGPT and wrote:
“I need a blurb of 700 characters. Alive. With some nerve. No bureaucratic language. No ‘at the centre of the story’. No ‘a dramatic tale’. No ‘will change everything’. And none of that dreadful tone that makes it sound as though the book is being described by someone who hates books.”
ChatGPT replied almost at once:
“Understood. Send me a short description of the novel, or allow me to use the previous analysis of the text.”
— Permission granted, — Artur said. — But no crematorium for the soul.
A version appeared a few seconds later.
The blurb was better.
Not good, but better.
Artur read it, got up, walked a circuit of the kitchen, opened the fridge, looked inside, took nothing, closed it and returned to the laptop.
This was his standard method of literary analysis.
— You’re close, — he told the chat. — But close is when the arrow wounds the person standing next to the target.
He began editing.
Deleted two adjectives.
Then three.
Then an entire sentence.
Then changed “struggles” to “tries not to break.”
Then realised it sounded like the description of a motivational marathon.
Deleted it.
Outside the window Kyiv glowed with hundreds of windows. Someone was editing a video. Someone was learning English. Someone was convincing themselves not to text an ex. Someone was planning to go to bed at eleven and, at ten forty-five, was already opening a series for just one episode — like a person who had lost to themselves, but comfortably.
Meanwhile Artur was trying to write seven hundred characters that would persuade a stranger that four hundred pages were worth their attention.
By one in the morning he had five versions.
The first sounded like an advert for a psychological training course.
The second — like an obituary.
The third — like a grant application.
The fourth was good, but it was not about that novel.
The fifth was the one Artur hated least.
He read it aloud:
— In a world where a person can easily be replaced by a function, the protagonist tries to remain himself even when everyone around him is telling him to become convenient. It is a story about choice, loneliness and the price of inner freedom…
He stopped.
— No.
Deleted everything.
The empty page looked back at him with white superiority.
And then the phone rang.
Denys.
Artur answered.
— Are you asleep? — Denys asked.
— No.
— Knew it. You’re either writing or suffering because you’re writing.
— It’s called the creative process.
— Normal people call it “you need to leave the house.”
— Why are you calling?
— We’re getting together Saturday. Bar, people, life. Coming?
Artur looked at the empty document.
— I don’t know. I need to write a blurb.
A pause.
— You’re not coming to the bar because you’re fighting seven hundred characters?
— If you want to phrase it in the most humiliating possible way — yes.
— Artur, I respect you. But sometimes you behave as though you don’t want to become a writer, you want to prove that suffering has a degree in literature.
— Thank you very much for the support.
— I am supporting you. Support just has to be honest. You’re a good guy. You’ve got a decent job, decent looks, a decent chance at an indecently good life. And you’re sitting there trying to pack your soul into a character limit.
— Those seven hundred characters could change my life.
— No. The blurb won’t change your life. What will change it is when you stop behaving as though the world is obliged to work out for itself how talented you are.
Artur fell silent.
Denys had a gift for becoming unbearable at exactly the moment he said something accurate.
— You sound like ChatGPT after a corporate training session, — Artur said.
— And you sound like a man who lost an argument with a printer.
— I didn’t lose.
— Then come Saturday.
— I’ll think about it.
— Don’t think. You think too much. That’s why you have three novels and zero dates.
— It’s not zero.
— Dates with your laptop don’t count.
— At least it doesn’t ask when I’m getting married.
— Because it doesn’t care.
— Exactly. A healthy foundation for a relationship.
Denys laughed.
— All right, writer. Go to sleep. And remember: if you can’t explain your novel briefly, that doesn’t mean the novel is deep. Maybe you’re just afraid to understand what it’s really about.
Denys hung up.
Artur held the phone to his ear for another few seconds.
— I hate practical people, — he said. — Sometimes they’re accidentally useful.
He looked at the document again.
Perhaps the blurb really was not a betrayal of the novel.
When one person meets another, they do not immediately tell them their entire life either: childhood, traumas, credit history, list of exes and the reason they still have no normal sleep schedule.
They say a few things.
Give the other person a chance to become interested.
A blurb is not a confession.
It is a first date.
And on a first date you do not read your entire diary aloud. If you do, there is no second date.
Artur opened a new document.
This time he did not write “blurb.”
He wrote:
FIRST DATE WITH THE NOVEL
And below it:
“He spent his whole life trying to be himself, until he realised that the world pays far more willingly for people who know how to be convenient. But when convenience becomes a condition of survival, freedom stops being a beautiful word and becomes a problem. This is a novel about a man who must choose: remain himself without guarantees, or become useful at the cost of his own voice.”
Artur reread it.
Counted the characters.
Fewer than seven hundred.
He copied the text into ChatGPT.
“Assess it.”
The answer appeared quickly:
“This version has a stronger authorial voice, a clear conflict and understandable philosophical tension. It works as a basic blurb, but the genre and narrative hook should be made more specific.”
Artur leaned back in his chair.
— Of course. Because happiness in literature always needs to be made more specific.
He added two more sentences.
Removed one.
Changed “must” to “is forced to.”
Changed it back to “must,” because “is forced to” made it sound as though the hero had been summoned to the metaphysical draft office.
At two in the morning, the first blurb was finished.
It did not convey the entire novel.
It could not.
But it was no longer the enemy.
It was a door handle.
Not the door.
Not the room.
Not the house.
Only the handle.
But without a handle, even the most beautiful door remains a wall.
Artur saved the file:
“Blurb_novel_1_live_version”
Then thought about it and renamed it:
“Blurb_novel_1_for_publisher”
Then again:
“Blurb_novel_1_not_embarrassing_to_send”
That was more accurate.
He closed the laptop.
The flat went quiet.
A minute later his phone flashed with a message from Denys:
“I sent you the address of the bar. Don’t invent some literary reason not to come.”
Artur looked at the message.
Then at the dark laptop screen.
Then typed:
“I’ll be there.”
Pressed “send.”
Only then did he realise that in the past hour he had done two dangerous things: written a blurb and agreed to go out into life.
For one evening, that was more than enough.

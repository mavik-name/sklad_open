CHAPTER 1. THREE NOVELS NOBODY READS

Artur was thirty-three — the age by which Jesus had already completed his mission, while Artur still could not get anyone to read even the first thirty pages of his novel.
He did not take this with religious tragedy, but he did feel a certain statistical grievance.
Because three novels are no joke.
This was not “I started something once.”
It was not a file on the desktop called “Book_idea_new_definitely_later.”
These were three finished texts. Beginning, middle, ending, characters, conflicts, editing, night-time doubts and coffee after which the heart starts working like an emergency siren.
The three novels sat on his laptop in a separate folder.
The folder was called:
“LITERATURE”.
Inside were three more folders:
“Novel 1. Final”
“Novel 2. Final”
“Novel 3. Final”
Each contained several files:
“final”,
“final_2”,
“definitely_final”,
“do_not_touch”,
“do_not_touch_revised”,
“version_after_sleepless_night”.
Artur knew that if archaeologists of the future ever found his laptop, they would conclude that ancient Ukrainian writers hid their manuscripts inside sacred labyrinths so that the weak in spirit could never reach the final version.
The problem was not that Artur did not write.
The problem began where the text was supposed to meet a reader.
— Sure, of course, send it over. I’ll read it at the weekend, — said friends, acquaintances, colleagues, former classmates and one woman Artur had almost gone on a date with, except that instead he sent her his manuscript.
After saying this, the person did not disappear entirely. No. They might like his gym photos, comment on stories about coffee, wish him a happy birthday, ask for the number of a good dentist.
But the moment the novel came up, they developed a delicate form of amnesia.
— Have you read it? — Artur would ask two weeks later.
— Started it, — the person would say.
In modern culture, “started it” could mean anything: opened the file, saw the page count, got frightened, closed the file, went to watch a video in which a man explains the history of the Roman Empire through pizza in twenty seconds.
— And?
— Very atmospheric.
Artur hated the word “atmospheric.”
It was a life belt for people who had read nothing but wanted to remain decent human beings. “Atmospheric” meant: I have no idea what this text is about, but I believe there is something in there.
At the office, Artur worked as a manager for a company that sold corporate solutions to businesses. Nobody fully understood what exactly those solutions solved, apart from the sales department, but they spoke about it with such confidence that clients sometimes bought before asking their first question.
Every day Artur wrote emails, presentations, commercial proposals and short copy for the website. He was paid to persuade people of the value of a product that had forty-seven functions, twelve of which actually worked and three of which anyone needed.
In the evenings he went home and wrote novels in which everything worked, but nobody wanted to pay for them.
There was a certain injustice in that.
Almost elegant.
— You take this too seriously, — Denys said one day as they drank coffee in the office kitchen.
Denys was one of those people who considered themselves practical because they had never had a dream lasting longer than the instalment plan on their phone.
— Take what seriously? — Artur asked.
— Writing. That’s not how it works now. You have to make it simpler.
— Simpler how?
— Short texts. Posts. Videos. Personal brand. People have to see the author.
— I want them to see the text.
Denys looked at him with the pity of a man who had just encountered a monk in a supermarket.
— Artur, the text isn’t going to sell itself.
— What sells the text?
— The author.
— So I’m supposed to become an advert for my own book?
— Exactly.
Artur took a sip of coffee.
— Writers used to be the voice of an age.
— They did, — Denys agreed. — Now the age wants the voice to know how to edit Reels.
Artur said nothing.
The worst thing about some pieces of nonsense is their practical usefulness.
After work he went to the gym.
The gym was a place where the world behaved more honestly than literature. If you lifted a barbell, it went up. If you ran ten kilometres, the app showed ten kilometres. If you trained for three months, your body changed.
Novels were different.
You could spend three years rewriting scenes, cutting unnecessary dialogue, changing the ending, polishing a sentence until it shone, and then hear:
— Send it over. I’ll read it at the weekend.
And that was it.
Artur’s body responded to discipline.
The world did not.
At thirty-three he had an athletic build, a decent job, a good smile, a respectable wardrobe and three novels whose social life was more modest than that of a cactus on a windowsill.
Women noticed him.
He could see that.
Not because he was vain, but because he had eyes.
Sometimes he even thought he ought to stop. Meet a normal woman. Get married. Have children. Three of them. For some reason he always thought of three. Perhaps because the three novels already existed and somewhere deep down Artur believed life should appreciate symmetry.
But then he would go home, open the laptop — and start writing again.
Because the dream was shameless.
It did not ask for time.
It took it.
One evening, after yet another message saying “haven’t got to it yet, but I definitely will,” Artur opened the file containing his first novel and stared at the first page for a long time.
The text was good.
He could feel it.
Not “genius.”
Not “a masterpiece.”
Not “the literary event of the year which foolish humanity has not yet deserved.”
Artur was not an idiot. He understood that any text could be improved. But he also knew the difference between a raw manuscript and a work into which years had been poured.
This was a work.
And real things have an unpleasant habit: they begin to demand justice.
Artur opened his browser and typed:
“where can I publish my novel for free”
The first results were platforms for aspiring authors.
The banners promised:
“Here you will be heard!”
“Become part of a creative community!”
“Publish, read, get feedback!”
Artur registered on one of the platforms.
The username ArthurWriter was already taken.
Arthur_Author — taken too.
Arthur_Real_Author — also taken.
After his fifth attempt, the system suggested:
Arthur_384729
— Thank you, — Artur told the monitor. — Exactly how I imagined my entrance into literature.
He uploaded the first chapter of the novel, added a description, genre, tags and a cover he quickly put together in a free service, then clicked “Publish.”
A message appeared on the screen:
“Congratulations! Your work is now available to readers.”
Artur felt a small thrill.
As if he had released a pigeon into the sky.
An hour later, the pigeon came back with its first comment.
“Interesting! Come visit me too. I’ll be happy to support you in return!”
Artur blinked.
Then came a second:
“Great style! A like from me. If you don’t mind, please rate my novel ‘Dark Kiss of the Dragon’.”
The third was particularly warm:
“Very deep. You can tell the author has a soul. Looking forward to some support in return.”
Artur opened the statistics.
Average reading time: 18 seconds.
The chapter contained twenty-seven thousand characters.
He leaned back in his chair.
— Either I have an audience capable of reading at superhuman speed, — he said aloud, — or I’ve joined a club for literary metabolism.
The next two days confirmed the latter.
The platform lived by a simple law: everyone supported everyone else with great enthusiasm, but nobody risked going as far as page two.
It had the friendliness of a market where every seller bought air from the stall next door so that the neighbour would later buy fog from them.
— Your text is incredible!
— Thank you! Yours too!
— Did you read it?
— No, but you can feel the energy.
Artur tried to read other people’s work honestly.
He did not last long.
The first novel began with the line:
“She ran through the dark forest, unaware that her fate had long ago been written in blood upon the pages of eternity.”
The second began almost the same way, except this time it was a man running, the forest was cursed and the blood belonged to an ancient order.
The third was called “Heart of the Alpha Vampire” and opened with the dedication:
“To all who believe in true love and immortal passion.”
Artur closed the laptop.
— I have nothing against vampires, — he told himself. — But why are all of them so emotionally dependent?
After a week, his novel had forty-three likes, twenty-seven comments and not a single sign that anyone had finished even the first chapter.
The most honest comment came from a user called Poet_Without_Borders:
“Haven’t read it yet, but I can see the potential.”
Artur even liked the comment.
For the honesty.
Then he closed the platform and sat for a long time in front of the first page of his novel.
He no longer felt like arguing with readers.
There were hardly any readers there.
There was a community of people equally afraid of being unseen, trading visibility like small change.
Artur realised something unpleasant: writing a text did not mean you had found a person who would actually sit down and read it.
It was not a defeat.
But it was now a task.
He opened a new tab.
The cursor blinked in the search bar.
Artur hesitated for a few seconds, then typed:
“how to tell if my novel is actually good”
The search engine offered forums, courses, advice, lists of mistakes and videos featuring the faces of people promising to teach him to write bestsellers in six weeks.
Artur looked at all of it and closed the tab.
Suddenly he did not want another community, another course, another stranger explaining how to be an author properly without having read a single one of his sentences.
He stood up, picked up the laptop and left the flat.
It was already getting dark outside. Kyiv glowed with windows, headlights, café signs and that indifferent evening beauty that always irritates a person slightly when things inside them are not nearly so beautiful.
Artur went into a café near the metro, sat by the window and ordered coffee and a croissant he had no intention of eating.
He needed a place where he could do something foolish and pretend it was part of the working process.
He opened the laptop.
The first page of his novel was on the screen again.
The text was silent.
The platform was silent.
His friends were silent.
The world, as usual, was busy with itself.
Artur opened a new tab.
If people won’t read, he thought, perhaps I should start with a machine.
At least a machine won’t say “atmospheric.”
And it won’t ask for anything in return.

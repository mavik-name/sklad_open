CHAPTER 7. READING NIGHT

The door closed.
The flat went quiet.
Not simply quiet in the way a flat on the twenty-first floor of a Kyiv apartment complex goes quiet at night. So quiet that even the refrigerator seemed to understand the delicacy of the moment and decided not to hum unless absolutely necessary.
Olya stood by the entrance, taking off her coat, and Artur suddenly became acutely aware of three things.
First: there was a woman in his flat.
Second: this woman was beautiful, intelligent and looking at him as though she could see not only his T-shirt but every draft of his personality.
Third: a pair of track pants was lying on the chair in the living room.
Not merely lying there.
Living there.
Artur stepped forward and quickly swept them behind the back of the sofa.
Olya noticed.
— Don’t worry, — she said. — I’m an editor, but I’m not that cruel. I only edit other people’s track pants at a separate rate.
— My place is usually tidy.
— That’s what a man says immediately after hiding evidence.
— That was a stylistic edit to the space.
— Not bad. The space is less autobiographical now.
She walked into the room.
The flat really was tidy. Books on the shelves, a laptop on the table, a dumbbell by the wall, a cup beside the laptop, several printed pages, a pen, sticky notes, a charger, another book on the floor near the sofa.
It was the flat of a man who wanted a family but, for the time being, lived with deadlines.
— Nice place, — Olya said.
— Thanks.
— A little like an author who’s been waiting for guests for years and still has no idea what to do with them when they finally arrive.
— I can offer you tea.
— See? Progress already. You’re not panicking. You’re serving the panic.
Artur went to the kitchen.
— Black or green?
— Whichever people drink when they’re about to tell the truth.
— Strong, then.
— And no sugar.
— Why?
— Truth with sugar turns into a motivational post.
Artur put the kettle on and took out two cups. In the kitchen he leaned both hands against the worktop for a few seconds.
Only a few hours earlier he had been sitting in a bar, joking about self-publishing costing as much as a car and automated replies from publishers. Now Olya was in his flat.
She could have gone home.
Could have said, “See you.”
Could have left him with that familiar promise, “I’ll read it tomorrow,” which in the literary world meant roughly the same thing as “I’m starting a new life on Monday.”
But she had come.
To his place.
At night.
To read a novel.
It was so implausible that even Artur’s dream seemed to be standing off to one side, nervously asking whether it was definitely invited.
— Artur? — came Olya’s voice from the room.
— Yes?
— Where’s the file?
— On the desktop. Folder called “Literature”.
A pause.
— Seriously?
— What?
— The folder is called “Literature”?
— What should it be called?
— I don’t know. “Heart for Export”. “Dangerous to Open”. “Male Vulnerability Stored Here — Handle with Care”.
— I like simple names.
— You called a folder containing three novels “Literature”. That’s not simplicity. That’s an attempt to occupy the whole continent at once.
Artur came back with the tea.
Olya was sitting at the table in front of the laptop. The folder was open on the screen. Inside it — three novels.
— Which one first? — she asked.
Artur set down the cups.
— The one I sent to the publisher.
— Title?
He told her.
Olya repeated it aloud.
In her voice the title sounded different. Not like a file. Not like work. Like something that might actually exist outside him.
Artur felt awkward.
— What? — she asked.
— Nothing.
— You have the face of a man who has just heard his own title without the internal defences switched on.
— Occupational hazard again?
— No. It’s just obvious.
She opened the file.
The first page appeared on the screen.
Artur sat beside her. Not too close. Not too far. As though there had to be enough room between them for both the novel and every possible misunderstanding the night might contain.
Olya rested her fingers on the touchpad.
— I’ll read silently. Sometimes I’ll ask questions. Sometimes I’ll argue. Sometimes I’ll be quiet. Not every silence means catastrophe.
— Which one does?
— You’ll know.
— Wonderful. I’ve always dreamt of a night governed by a system of non-verbal threats.
She smiled.
— Don’t dramatise.
— I’m a writer.
— That’s not an excuse. It’s an aggravating circumstance.
Olya began reading.
At first Artur watched the screen. Then her face. Then the screen again. Then decided that watching someone read your text was a distinct form of self-torture.
Her eyes moved across the lines.
Every now and then she frowned slightly.
Once, she almost smiled.
Artur nearly leaned forward, but stopped himself in time.
A reader’s smile is like a signal from space. You desperately want to decode it at once, but the best thing you can do is leave it alone so you don’t break it.
After a few minutes Olya said:
— The first sentence is good.
— You already wrote that.
— Now I’m confirming it officially.
— Can I put that in a review?
— “The first sentence is good” is a weak sales pitch for four hundred pages.
— It’s a breakthrough for me. Some people didn’t always survive even the first sentence before.
— Don’t distract me.
He fell silent.
The room held only the soft noise of the city beyond the window, now and then the cooling kettle in the kitchen, and the quiet click of the touchpad.
Artur suddenly realised how strange the scene was.
If anyone from the bar could see them in the flat now, the conclusion would be obvious. A man and a woman after midnight, tea, dim light, Kyiv outside the window, both of them a little post-alcohol, a conversation that had shown every sign in the taxi of heading somewhere dangerous.
And yet the hottest thing in the room was a Word file.
— This is good, — Olya said.
Artur turned his head.
— Where?
— This scene. The dialogue holds. The protagonist feels alive.
— Alive is good?
— In literature, yes. In real life, depends on the protagonist.
She read a few more pages.
— This is unnecessary.
Artur tensed.
— What exactly?
— The description of the room. You’ve already shown us he’s lonely. You don’t need to surround him seven more times with empty cups, a cold lamp and rain.
— It’s atmosphere.
Olya slowly turned towards him.
— Artur.
— What?
— Never defend something unnecessary with the word “atmosphere”. It’s like covering a corpse with a rug and saying it’s warmer that way.
He said nothing.
Then he picked up a pen and wrote on a sticky note:
“Don’t defend corpses with atmosphere.”
Olya looked at the note.
— I’m starting to be proud of you.
— That’s almost intimate.
— Don’t get carried away. I haven’t reached page three yet.
They laughed.
The tension eased a little.
Olya kept reading. Sometimes she was silent for so long that Artur wanted to call an emergency literary ambulance. Sometimes she asked precise questions.
— Why does he react like this here?
— Because he’s afraid of losing control.
— We can see that later. In this scene he’s just being rude.
— He isn’t rude. He’s defending himself.
— The reader doesn’t owe you a hundred-page line of credit. Give them at least a hint.
Artur wrote it down.
— Good turn here.
He wrote that down.
— Here you explain something you’ve already shown.
He wrote that down.
— This is funny.
Artur looked up.
— Really?
— Yes.
— That matters.
— Why?
— Because sometimes I don’t know whether something is actually funny or I’ve just impressed myself.
— That’s basically the central illness of authors. They confuse the reader’s laughter with the ego’s internal applause.
— Are you sure you don’t want to open a sanatorium for writers?
— No. They’d start writing about the sanatorium and refuse to follow the regime.
At around two in the morning they moved from the table to the sofa.
Not because the scene had finally begun heading in the expected direction.
The chairs simply turned out to have less stamina than literature.
Olya sat with the laptop on her knees. Artur sat beside her with the notebook. Between them stood two cups and a plate of biscuits he had found in a cupboard and which, fortunately, had not yet acquired historical value.
— Do you always keep biscuits for late-night editors? — Olya asked.
— No. They’re the bachelor emergency reserve.
— As in war, apocalypse or woman in the flat?
— Exactly.
— Good to know you prepared equally well for every scenario.
She took a biscuit and returned to the text.
Artur looked at her and caught himself feeling good.
Not “victorious”.
Not “everything is finally going to work out”.
Just good.
Someone was reading his novel in his flat. Not out of politeness. Not for a reciprocal like. Not because an algorithm had recommended it. Because she had become interested.
It was another kind of intimacy.
Quiet.
Dangerous.
Almost defenceless.
Olya suddenly said:
— That’s a good line for the woman.
Artur became alert.
— You sound surprised.
— A little.
— Why?
— Because men often write women as if a woman were a man with hair who stares mysteriously out of a window.
— I tried.
— It shows. But here, — she scrolled down, — here she doesn’t talk like a living person. She talks like a function designed to develop the protagonist.
— That hurts.
— It’s supposed to. Otherwise nothing grows.
— They say that at the gym too.
— See? Literature and biceps have more in common than you thought.
At three in the morning Artur yawned.
Olya noticed.
— Falling asleep?
— No.
— Liar.
— Yes.
— We can stop.
— No.
— Artur, this isn’t a marathon.
— Which is why I’m still alive.
Olya lowered the laptop lid without closing it completely, keeping one finger between the lid and the keyboard.
— What do you want more right now: for me to keep reading, or for me to tell you I already like it?
Artur thought about it.
— Both options sound suspiciously pleasant.
— Choose.
— Keep reading.
Olya nodded.
— Correct answer.
— Why?
— Because an author who needs praise more than being read doesn’t last long. Praise is sugar. Reading is bread.
Artur wrote it down.
— You speak in quotations and then act surprised that I write everything down.
— I’m not surprised. I’m simply watching you turn a night with a woman into the minutes of an editorial meeting.
Artur glanced at her.
— You opened the laptop.
— Yes. But you took out the notebook.
— Self-defence.
— Against what?
— Happiness. It’s badly structured.
Olya laughed.
— That may be the most accurate thing anyone could say about you.
They kept reading.
Sometimes Olya argued.
Sometimes Artur defended himself.
Then gave in.
Then found an argument.
Then they both laughed because the argument turned out to be beautiful and useless.
— You love your weak scenes, — Olya said.
— They’re mine.
— Bad criterion. Parents love children who draw on the wallpaper too, but that doesn’t turn the wallpaper into a gallery.
— You’ve just insulted half of family art.
— It’ll survive.
Towards morning the sky outside began to lighten.
Kyiv turned grey slowly, as though it too were reading a difficult manuscript and had not yet decided whether to wake up.
Olya had reached page seventy-two.
Artur had long since stopped asking, “Well?” It was his first adult decision of the night.
He simply sat beside her and took notes.
Sticky notes covered the table:
“Cut the room.”
“Protagonist is rude, not defensive.”
“Hint earlier.”
“Don’t hide corpses with atmosphere.”
“Praise is sugar, reading is bread.”
“Woman is not a function.”
At last Olya closed the laptop.
— That’s it. I need at least two hours of sleep or tomorrow I’ll start editing people instead of texts.
Artur looked at the time.
Five in the morning.
— You read seventy-two pages.
— Yes.
— In one night.
— Yes.
He said nothing.
Olya tilted her head.
— Don’t make that face as though I’ve just carried you out of a burning building.
— Haven’t you?
She smiled, tired but warm.
— The text is alive, Artur.
He did not answer.
There was no joke for that sentence.
Olya stood and picked up her bag.
— But it needs work.
— I know.
— Not “someday”. Not “after the publisher replies”. Now. You have strong material, but in places you behave like a homeowner who won’t let guests throw out an old sofa because his trauma once sat on it.
Artur smiled.
— You edit in metaphors even at five in the morning?
— That’s not me anymore. That’s sleep deprivation.
He walked her to the door.
The hallway was quiet. Fortunately, the concierge could not see this ending; otherwise a version of events would have been born in her eyes that no synopsis could survive.
Olya put on her coat.
— Thanks for the tea.
— Thanks for reading.
— That sounds more intimate than it should.
— Because it is.
She looked at him.
For a second something hung between them that had no name. Not a promise. Not the start of a relationship. Not a random night. Something more fragile and more interesting: possibility.
Olya touched his shoulder.
— Don’t ruin the text by taking offence at the edits.
— I’ll try.
— And get some sleep.
— That’s less realistic.
She smiled.
— Then at least don’t send me ten messages straight away explaining why I’m wrong.
— Can I send five?
— Two.
— Three.
— One.
— Deal. Two.
— You bargain like an author whose favourite scene is being cut.
— It’s my core skill.
She opened the door.
Before leaving, she looked back.
— And yes. The first sentence really is good.
The door closed.
Artur stood barefoot in the hallway, hair wrecked by the sleepless night, wearing a T-shirt, his head full of edits and his heart behaving in a distinctly unprofessional manner.
An open notebook lay on the table in the room.
For the first time, the novel had come back to him not as silence, not as likes, not as an algorithm’s score.
But as someone else’s handwriting in the margins of his night.
Artur went to the laptop, opened the file and looked at the first page.
Then at the sticky notes.
Then at the first page again.
And wrote at the top of a new document:
“Edits after reading night.”
He wanted to be a writer.
Apparently that meant more than writing.
It also meant surviving the moment when someone finally read you.
At nine in the morning Artur sat in the office with the face of a man physically present on the planet while his soul was still finishing page seventy-two.
Denys appeared beside his desk with coffee and the expression of an investigator who already knew the answer but intended to enjoy the interrogation.
— Well? — he said.
Artur looked up.
— Well what?
— Don’t pretend you don’t know. I saw you leave with Olya.
— And?
— And now I want to know whether our Artur has finally taken a step towards a family, three children and normal human chaos in the bathroom.
— Denys…
— No details, if you please. I’m a cultured man. I only need the general vector.
— We read.
Denys froze.
— What?
— The novel. She read my novel.
— Is that what we call it now?
— No. That is literally what we call it.
Denys put the coffee on the desk.
— Wait. A beautiful editor goes home with you after a bar. Night. Rain. Twenty-first floor. And you… read?
— Yes.
— Until when?
— Five.
Denys slowly sat on the edge of the neighbouring desk.
— Five in the morning?
— Yes.
— Do you realise normal people can make mistakes, regret them, make up again and order breakfast in that amount of time?
— We had sticky notes.
— God.
Denys rubbed his face.
— I don’t know whether to be proud of you or call a specialist.
— She read seventy-two pages.
— Artur, in the male world, the sentence “she was at my place until five in the morning” does not normally end with a page count.
— In the writing world it does.
— Which is why it’s so sparsely populated.
Artur picked up his coffee.
— She said the text is alive.
Denys was about to reply, then stopped.
For the first time in the conversation, his face became more serious.
— She really said that?
— Yes.
— And?
— And she said it needs work.
— That already sounds like a relationship.
Artur smiled.
— With the text?
— I don’t know yet. But if a woman reads your novel until five in the morning and doesn’t block you afterwards, that is either a professional feat or the beginning of a very strange romantic arc.
— She’s an editor.
— That doesn’t rule out romance. It just means the foreplay happens through comments in the margins.
Artur burst out laughing.
Denys leaned closer.
— Still, just so I understand correctly: no spicy moments at all?
Artur thought.
— There was one.
— Oh!
— She said my favourite scene was unnecessary.
Denys closed his eyes.
— You’re hopeless.
— But read.
— That sounds proud, certainly. But I can’t explain it to your mother that way.
— Don’t tell my mother anything.
— Too late. I’ve already told her in my head: “Your son spent the night with a woman, and they did not sin because they were editing a manuscript.” She’ll either cry or start praying for your masculinity.
— Denys.
— What?
— Work.
— I can’t. I’ve just discovered that intimacy can come in DOCX format now.
Artur leaned back in his chair.
He was tired. Very tired. His head throbbed. His eyes burned. The work emails on the screen looked as though they had been written in a language invented by people without souls but with KPIs.
And still he felt good.
Not because Olya had gone home with him.
Not because the night might have looked different.
Because it had become exactly what it was: strange, funny, honest and far more intimate than he had expected.
Denys took a sip of coffee.
— All right, writer. I admit it: that was a spicy night.
— Really?
— Yes. Your spice simply wasn’t physical. It was editorial. Pepper from the revisions, with an aftertaste of sleep deprivation.
— At last you’ve said something accurate.
— Write it down.
— Not anymore. I’m tired.
— Then remember this: if a woman reads you until five in the morning, don’t be too quick to feel offended that she didn’t get undressed. Maybe she did something more dangerous.
— What exactly?
Denys put his cup on the desk.
— Saw what’s inside you.
Artur fell silent.
Denys rarely spoke quietly. And almost never precisely. So at moments like this Artur wanted to check whether it was really him or some upgraded morning version.
— That one belongs in the quote book, — Artur said.
— No. That one’s mine. I’ll write a novel one day too.
— About what?
— A man who spends his whole life giving brilliant advice to a writer, while the writer only records the editor’s lines.
Artur laughed.
A new email flashed onto the screen.
Sender: Olya.
Subject: “First notes”.
Artur stopped laughing.
Denys immediately leaned closer.
— From her?
— Yes.
— Open it.
— It’s personal.
— After a night of reading? Artur, the two of you are already a public service.
Artur opened the message.
It was short:
“Good morning. I slept a little and changed my mind about one scene: don’t cut it completely, but move it earlier. And one more thing: your protagonist gets more interesting when he stops being right. Think about that. P.S. The biscuits were awful. Consider this a domestic edit.”
Artur read it.
Then read it again.
Denys watched him.
— Well?
Artur smiled.
— She wrote.
— I can see that.
— About the scene.
— Of course. Your erotic life happens through structure.
Artur closed the message.
— She said the protagonist becomes more interesting when he stops being right.
Denys nodded.
— Smart woman.
— Yes.
— So what are you going to do?
Artur looked at the open work document, then at his email, then at the notes on his phone.
— First I’ll get through the workday.
— And then?
— Then I’ll rewrite the scene.
Denys smiled.
— And call her?
Artur took a sip of coffee.
— Maybe.
— Oh no. Not your “maybe”. With you, that word means the decision has already been made but you still want to walk around it carrying a philosophical flashlight.
Artur looked at him.
— Fine. I’ll call her.
Denys raised his cup.
— To progress. You finally have a woman who has read more pages of your novel than most of your friends have read messages about it.
— That’s a low bar.
— At least it’s realistic.
Artur turned back to the computer.
The workday was beginning.
Ahead were emails, presentations, customer journeys, corporate decisions and other forms of paid absurdity.
But somewhere beneath all of it — beneath the coffee, lack of sleep, Denys’s jokes, sticky notes, edits and Olya’s email — lived one simple fact.
He had been read.
Not all of him.
Not perfectly.
Neither mercilessly nor indulgently.
For real.
And it turned out to be far more dangerous than not being read at all.
Because now the novel could no longer retreat into the quiet “LITERATURE” folder and pretend the world owed it something.
Now it had to work.
Just like Artur.

Many of the trips were not memorable for anything in particular.

Except the people.

The routes sometimes blurred together. The people almost never did.

There was a young woman whose transfer was being paid for by her father, a serviceman. She did not say a single word during the entire journey.

There was a teenage girl who crossed the border on foot at Shehyni and was travelling to visit her father, also a serviceman, who had finally been granted leave.

There was a well-off woman who had flown in from England to visit her mother.

And there was another woman, also from England, travelling to Kyiv to sign an agreement with a surrogate mother who was to carry her child. Very well read. A genuine bibliophile. When she found out I was a writer, she became interested in my books. I hope at least one of them worked for her.

There were lawyers who spent entire journeys on the phone handling cases worth millions and occasionally apologised to the driver for discussing things he could not possibly understand anyway.

Sometimes I did understand.

I simply gave no sign of it.

A transfer driver hears a great deal in general. And pretends not to have heard even more.

There were things more exhausting than the actual driving too. More exhausting even than the nights when the passenger slept peacefully behind you while you poured energy drinks into yourself so that, at drop-off, you could still look as fresh as a cucumber.

Accounting.

Photo of the speedometer at pickup. Photo of the speedometer at drop-off. Arrival at base. Departure from base. CRM. Expenses. Cash. Fuel. Hotels. Till. Recalculations.

At some point, I began to suspect the Mercedes existed mainly so there would be something to enter into the CRM.

I had imagined the system more simply: you pay the expenses, hand in the cash, the company reimburses what you spent, subtracts its share, calculates yours — everybody happy.

In practice it was slightly more complicated. Especially for someone who processes letters far better than numbers.

And still, I liked the job.

The ending arrived unexpectedly.

I had two bookings left to complete: take two women and a child to Przemyśl, then dash to Kraków and bring a passenger back to Lviv.

An ordinary working day.

I arranged pickup with the clients. Loaded their things. We got in. We drove.

Using information from drivers’ chats, we chose the checkpoint with the lightest traffic. The passengers needed Przemyśl, which is practically next door to Shehyni, but there were more than one hundred and sixty cars waiting there. Six to eight hours.

So we headed through Rava-Ruska.

There was almost no queue. And sure enough, we entered the checkpoint almost immediately.

Then it began.

For some reason, the client decided to pay me before we had even gone through control.

I took the money and thought: bad sign.

Don’t celebrate before you clear the hurdle.

But I was relaxed about my documents. Everything was in order.

I approached the first window. The border guard took my passport.

“Show me Reserve+.”

“Here.”

He looked.

Then suddenly the window closed.

A few seconds later it opened again.

“Excuse me, I need to step away for a minute.”

I watched him run toward a service room with my documents. A minute later he returned.

“Could you come over for a moment?”

I did.

“When did you last update Reserve+?”

“Who knows? I have a deferment until the end of mobilisation. I’m a father of many children.”

“Update it, please.”

I updated it.

Looked at the screen.

And…

Damn.

The line saying “deferment until the end of mobilisation” had disappeared.

I understood everything without a word.

“Please go to the office.”

While I waited outside the door, I called the company.

“I have a force majeure. Reserve ate my deferment.”

“Could be a system error.”

“Could be. The system will let me check that — just not now.”

The next update would not be available for several hours.

The door opened.

I entered the office.

And…

Sitting behind the desk was the same border guard who had turned me back on my first international run.

The same one.

He looked at me.

I looked at him.

For one second, I wanted to say:

“Well? Miss me?”

I did not.

A border checkpoint is still not the best venue for stand-up comedy.

Curtain.

The worst part was not even that I could not leave the country.

The worst part was that the company could not find replacement transport for these particular passengers. There were no available drivers with permission to cross the border.

The women had a flight.

Time was moving.

I went back to the car. They looked at me the way people look at a driver when much more than cabin comfort suddenly depends on him.

“What now?” one of them asked.

“Now we change the route.”

“To what?”

“A very premium one. Part of the journey will have to be on foot.”

At first they did not understand whether I was joking.

I was already calculating the time.

Then a mad idea appeared in my head — mad, but effectively the only one that could work.

I put everyone back in the car and tore off toward Shehyni. There was a queue of one hundred and fifty-seven cars there, but we no longer needed the vehicle crossing.

We needed the pedestrian one.

I drove the women and child right up to the crossing.

“Take your money back,” I said.

“What about the trip?”

“I didn’t get you to Poland. So there’s nothing to charge for.”

“But you…”

“Five zloty per person for the bus on the Polish side. Then straight ahead. If we don’t stand here debating my noble soul, you might even catch your flight.”

They laughed. Nervously, but at least they were laughing.

Gratis.

Free.

Roughly three hundred euros saved on a transfer they had almost already paid for. For me — no fee. For the company — fuel lost.

But that could be dealt with later.

Right now, the only thing that mattered was making it in time.

I got them to the crossing before the first shift change.

The women took their luggage. The child looked back at the Mercedes as if she could not quite understand why a premium transfer had suddenly ended at a turnstile and a walk.

“Straight ahead from here,” I said. “And don’t get lost.”

They waved and disappeared into the stream of people.

I turned back.

Only then did I feel how tired I was.

I could have been angry at Reserve+. At the border guard. At the system. At the next run collapsing. At the lost earnings.

But all of that somehow moved to the side. I was thinking about only one thing: I hoped they made it.

I was almost back at the base when a message arrived:

“We’re at the hotel. Przemyśl.”

Phew.

It worked.

And for the first time that whole month, numbers did not irritate me.

Zero for my fee.

Minus fuel for the company.

Three passengers where they needed to be.

The balance came out right.

And I think that was the moment my month as a transfer driver ended.

I had entered the profession imagining myself as Jason Statham.

Premium Mercedes. Beautiful roads. Passengers wearing expensive perfume. Hundred-dollar tips.

Instead I got thousands of kilometres, sleepless nights, energy drinks, borders, CRM, other people’s suitcases, other people’s stories, new readers, several future plots and dozens of voices that now live in my head.

And you know what?

I think those voices were exactly why I went there.

Nothing particularly remarkable.

A short route: Lviv to Rava-Ruska. I was taking a young woman to catch a train to Warsaw.

Five hundred hryvnias in tips and another potential reader — intelligent, well read, interesting to talk to.

For some reason, the hotel doorman looked surprised when I collected her. I waited for the passenger, opened the car door for her, closed it once she was seated, and carried her suitcase to the boot.

“Been in the profession long?” the doorman asked.

“Not very,” I said.

“You can tell straight away. Your lot usually sit in the car smoking while the passenger throws their own suitcase in the boot and climbs in. Haven’t seen one like you in a long time.”

I still do not know whether that was a compliment to me or a verdict on the entire profession.

Probably both.

FOURTH RUN

I had barely made it home when another booking came in:

Shehyni to Porubne. From the Polish border to the Romanian one.

Almost immediately, the client called.

“Oh, Mr Driver, we’re already waiting for you! How long will you be?”

“Within an hour.”

I ended the call and phoned the company at once.

“Did you hear what kind of clients these are? They sound Roma to me. Are you sure they can pay?”

“Go to the address. The managers will check everything now.”

By the time the managers were checking, I had already reached the passengers.

Two women.

More precisely, one woman and one girl of about twenty. Barefoot.

They got into the car, and I immediately caught the smell of people who had been on the road for a long time, in summer heat, without much opportunity to freshen up.

I wrote in the messenger:

“My passengers are Roma women. And they smell.”

The company did not take deposits, but I was asked to stop at a bank on the way so the women could withdraw cash and make a prepayment.

So we did.

On the road, they told me the Polish border guards had refused to let them through at a pedestrian crossing. Now they were worried about whether the Romanians would admit them. Their husbands were waiting in Germany, where they had gone to work, and the women missed them badly.

They asked me to wait near the border. Just in case they were refused and had to try another checkpoint.

I tried to reassure them.

“The only difference between Roma and Romanians is that Romanians have their own country and Roma don’t. Almost brotherly peoples. They’ll probably let you through.”

The joke was weak, obviously. But the women laughed.

After a few hours on the road, they stopped being “the Roma women who smell” from my message to the manager. They became two women who simply wanted to get to their husbands. The smell seemed to disappear. Or perhaps I had simply got used to it.

When they vanished through the gate of the pedestrian crossing, I even felt a little sad.

I had hurried to get them there before the border shift changed. If the guards refused them, after the change they would still have one more chance.

But it worked the first time.

Soon their husbands called me. They thanked me and said the Romanian guards had let the women into Romania, so I could go.

I was already approaching Ivano-Frankivsk when the phone rang again.

“Are you the driver who brought the girls to Romania?”

“Yes.”

“Could you bring them all the way to us in Germany? They crossed the border and now they’re standing there and don’t know what to do!”

“No,” I said. “I don’t have my documents with me because I wasn’t planning that kind of trip. What were you thinking before?”

There was a brief silence at the other end.

“Oh, we thought they wouldn’t let them through!”

FIRST TIME “ABROAD”

At last, a foreign calf had crapped in our garden too.

I got my first transfer abroad.

More precisely, I was supposed to collect passengers in Lublin, Poland, and take them to Kyiv.

Their plane was due at 11:45 Polish time. I left at five in the morning Ukrainian time, even though the drive to Lublin itself should have taken about four hours. But there is a war on, a border is a border, inspections can be stricter, so I gave myself a generous buffer.

At six in the morning I flew into the checkpoint.

Hurray!

No queue.

First window: Ukrainian border guards.

Behind it sat a young man. I could see he had fought — one arm was still bandaged from a wound. He looked at me with tired eyes.

“What grounds do you have for leaving the country?”

I handed him my certificate showing I was a father of many children.

“Your Reserve+.”

I showed him the military registry app.

He scanned the QR code.

Pause.

“What other documents do you have?”

A chill ran down my back.

“Isn’t that enough? I’ve already crossed the border during the war.”

“That was before Reserve+ was introduced.”

“I have scans of every document on my phone. I can show you.”

“That doesn’t work for me. I need the originals.”

“But understand, I can’t carry the originals of every family document around with me all the time. If something happens to me and, God forbid, they burn or get lost, what is my family supposed to do?”

“I can’t help you. Come with me.”

Half an hour outside the shift supervisor’s office, and I received an official refusal to cross the border.

Furious, I tore home with little regard for speed limits. My family handed me a folder of original documents like a relay baton — and I headed back to the border.

I got lucky the second time.

Still no queue.

A different shift now.

I handed the new border guard my passport and Reserve+, preparing to ceremonially produce my precious folder.

He did not need it.

He could not have cared less whether I had the originals of the very documents whose absence had kept me in the country two hours earlier.

My head filled with curses aimed at the previous young man’s sharpened sense of social justice.

Although somewhere underneath the swearing, I understood him.

He was young. He had already smelled gunpowder. Already been wounded. And even after that wound he was standing here at the border while some grey-haired, bearded Gandalf drove around in a premium Mercedes asking to be let into Poland.

Maybe he had every right to be angry.

And a border guard is better off being too cautious than not cautious enough.

Fine.

We got past that.

Literally.

And then Poland began.

At the beginning of the full-scale war, Polish support for Ukraine had seemed almost unconditional. A few years later, war fatigue had accumulated, politics had changed, and public attitudes had changed with them. At the border, you could feel it particularly sharply.

I simply had the bad luck to choose the wrong lane.

Of the three gates, I picked the one staffed by a young customs officer. Early twenties. I remember thinking: how can someone that young already be a customs officer?

Ahead of me were three cars and two vans.

She made the passengers in the vans take out all their luggage.

Suitcases lay on benches with their bellies cut open, clothes spilling out like intestines, while the girl methodically rummaged through them.

She opened every little bottle.

Even perfume.

Opened it.

Sniffed it.

Found nothing prohibited.

Then every car and van was sent for additional inspection and over the pit.

The girl went with them.

And disappeared.

We waited.

At least there was a roof over us — the heat was incredible.

The two neighbouring gates moved.

Not quickly.

But they moved.

We stood still.

An hour and a half.

Karl.

An hour and a half of simply standing there.

From time to time we could see her near the vehicle-scanning hangar or by some service buildings.

At last she came back.

Smiling, she took my passport.

“Open the bonnet.”

I opened it.

She ordered the passengers in the vans behind me to put all their luggage on the benches again.

And disappeared again.

Another half hour.

She returned.

“How much fuel is in your tank?”

I answered.

She nodded and walked to the end of our queue, where she began animatedly talking about something with the Polish border guards.

Ten minutes passed.

Twenty.

Forty.

An hour.

We sat in our cars and watched through the rear-view mirrors as our customs officer chatted energetically with her colleagues.

The queue remained silent.

I suspect everyone’s internal monologue was being conducted in roughly the same language.

Another hour and a half passed like that.

When she finally grew tired of socialising, a miracle occurred.

Within about fifteen minutes, everyone got their passports back.

We smiled politely.

“Dziękuję pani.”

Because we are civilised people.

And at last they let us into Poland.

Naturally, I was no longer going to make it to Lublin.

I was not even going to make it to Zamość, where a reserve driver was supposed to hand over the passenger he had collected from the airport.

So I took over my client almost immediately beyond the barrier.

Said hello.

Loaded the luggage.

Turned the Mercedes around.

And joined the queue back into Ukraine.

My first international transfer.

I had left Ukraine in order to drive a few metres, collect a passenger, and go straight back.

The return was less epic.

But not much faster.

Another four hours — and we were back in Mother Ukraine.

That was my first trip abroad as a transfer driver.

I never did see Lublin.

CHAPTER 3. SEW OR RIP

He woke to sparrows.
Not an alarm clock. Not anxiety. Not some profound thought that had descended on him during the night like grace upon an exhausted prophet with a bad book cover.
Sparrows.
The sun was already up outside, fresh and shameless and entirely without sympathy for people who had suffered a small literary death in five open tabs the night before.
The sparrows were making such a racket it sounded like a congress of minor MPs debating grain, rooftops and the moral decline of cats. Staying asleep would have required serious effort. Or death. Or being a writers' platform capable of failing to notice new authors even when they were practically lying on its doorstep holding a sign that said, "Come on, read one paragraph, you're not animals."
He lay with his eyes open, staring at the ceiling.
The dream would not let go.
Memory is strange. It can show no sign of life for years, lying somewhere in the dark basement of the mind under old newspapers, adolescent embarrassments, the smell of resort dust, failed conversations and shame over things nobody but you remembers anymore. Then—bang. It drags a scene from forty years ago back into the light with such precision that it might have been restored in 4K and streamed straight into your brain without warning.
He had dreamed about the resort town.
The one where he grew up.
Once an all-Union resort. Now a little tired, a little patched up, a little proud of its past, like an ageing performer no longer invited onto major stages but still putting on a jacket with shiny buttons for the sanatorium guests and waiting for somebody to recognise him.
In his youth the resort lived by the season, but loudly.
In summer the town filled with people, suitcases, the smell of corn, cheap perfume, mineral water, suntan lotion, wet towels, chlorine, market cherries and the human desire to spend at least two weeks pretending life did not end with the factory, the field, the queue, the plan, the quota, Party meetings and domestic exhaustion.
People came there to treat their livers, kidneys, joints, nerves, stomachs, backs, marriages, self-respect and everything else that in the Soviet Union was officially called "improving one's health" and unofficially meant "feeling that you haven't yet been written off as worn-out inventory."
The mineral-water pump rooms opened in the morning like temples for people carrying bottles. The mud baths smelled as though the earth had decided to remind humanity of its future, but therapeutically. Ozokerite was applied to backs, knees and hope. In sanatorium dining rooms, buckwheat looked like a disciplined punishment and compote like a liquid that had survived both the fruit and the cook.
The holidaymakers were all sorts.
They came as families, couples, work collectives, groups, on vouchers, through connections, through the trade union, on medical indication and with no indication at all beyond a powerful desire to escape their own lives for at least fourteen days.
The guests from Central Asia were special.
Back then, as a boy, he understood none of the contexts, cultural layers or that imperial porridge in which all peoples were supposedly brothers while every brother knew perfectly well who in the family was entitled to the larger piece of sausage.
He simply saw pictures.
Summer evenings. Parkland around the boarding houses. Streetlamps. Paths. The smell of dust and flowers. And processions that had seemed almost theatrical to him then.
Three, four, sometimes five female figures in paranjas walked in front. In the resort heat they moved like living dark silhouettes, as though someone had cut several figures from the night and sent them walking under the lamps. Behind them came a man in a nutria-fur hat despite the sticky July heat. He walked solemnly, as though he were not merely out for an evening stroll but leading a small state on a diplomatic mission.
To an adolescent mind it was incredible.
School said one thing.
The newspapers said another.
The cinema showed a third.
And in the evening, down the path came a man in a nutria hat with his own little domestic universe in paranjas moving ahead of him.
The Soviet Union had a gift for banning things that existed perfectly well and proclaiming things that did not exist even as a prototype. It was a country where everyone was equal, but some had special food rations. Where there was no sex, but maternity hospitals never closed. Where censorship did not exist, but everybody knew exactly what not to write. Where polygamy was forbidden, but some men arrived at the resort as though the law had been left somewhere between the desert and the railway station.
There were others too.
Solitary men.
Men who stayed not in boarding houses but in private rooms. They had supposedly come for treatment: to drink the water, take baths, lie under ozokerite packs and tell the family back home that the doctor had ordered it. In reality, some were looking less for health than for a brief holiday from every form of respectability.
The locals knew.
Everyone in a resort town knows everything. They simply do not call everything by its proper name, because if things suddenly began to be named honestly, half the town would lose its extra income and the other half its illusion of moral superiority.
Nearly every second house was having something added to it.
A room. A veranda. A summer kitchen. A separate entrance. A shower in the yard. Another room. Another veranda. Another shower that did not work in winter but still sounded convincing in the advert.
His parents had an insulated veranda too.
It was the pride of the family and proof that human beings were better at adapting to history than history was at adapting to them. Holidaymakers stayed there in summer. Holidaymakers stayed there in autumn. Holidaymakers stayed there in winter too, because if a veranda was insulated, it would be a sin not to monetise the warmth.
It was almost never empty.
Visitors brought suitcases, smells, accents, stories, strange habits, money, neuroses, gifts and that peculiar resort mood in which a person has not yet had time to rest but already behaves as though a holiday relieved them not only of fatigue but of part of their conscience.
Some carried naswar.
Some carried dried herb from the Chuy Valley.
Some carried black pellets of hashish that looked almost mystical to the boy, as though somebody had rolled night itself between their palms and brought it halfway across the country in a pocket.
And almost every one of them had several bank-wrapped bundles of "wooden" roubles: yellow-brown hundreds and green fifties, the same Soviet currency that, in the right hand, became a universal translator. Because if you arrived in a new town and wanted to find out quickly what was what and what cost what, the shortest route was to find the local zhuzhyky—the streetwise fixers.
The zhuzhyky knew everything.
Where the wine was cheaper.
Where the music was better.
Where you could get a taxi.
Where you did not need a taxi.
Where they sold decent corn.
Where not to go after ten.
Where the girls were.
Where the police were.
Where the police pretended not to know where the girls were.
The world rested not on laws but on people like that, people with no official position who knew the routes.
And then one day Mahmoud moved into their veranda.
In the dream he looked exactly as memory had chosen to preserve him: broad face, gold teeth, the smell of powerful cologne, a nutria-fur hat as proof that a real man did not let the weather dictate his style.
Mahmoud was a combine-harvester driver.
He worked, in his own words, like Papa Carlo. The boy was not entirely sure Mahmoud knew who Papa Carlo was, but the phrase sounded convincing. In the Soviet Union everybody worked like Papa Carlo, even people who actually sat in offices smelling of cheap tobacco, ink and authority over other people's certificates.
Mahmoud had not come to the resort with his family.
He was alone.
That immediately explained a great deal to the adults and something to him as well. He was no longer a child. He thought about girls too, although mostly in a vague, awkward, solemn way, as if they were something unattainable that walked around town in summer dresses and could turn an ordinary boy into a mute lamppost with a single glance.
Mahmoud settled in, carried in his suitcase, took off his shoes, inspected the veranda, left money on the table, went to the shower, splashed around for a long time, then emerged in a clean shirt, wearing so much cologne it was less as though he had applied it than entered into a political alliance with it.
— If somebody needs a girl, they look for one at the Puck, — he said. — At the disco.
— No, no, — Mahmoud waved him off. — I don't want wait. Disco later. I need now. My friends were here. Said who knows everything. Catch taxi.
That simple.
No metaphysics. No self-reflection. No blog post about bodily freedom and the trauma of patriarchal structures.
"Catch taxi."
There was something almost primordial in its clarity.
He caught a taxi.
A white-and-yellow GAZ-24 Volga stopped with the air of a vehicle that had been waiting specifically for them ever since it rolled off the production line. Back then a taxi was supposed to be a Volga. Not because it was comfortable, but because this was how the state imagined comfort: heavy bodywork, springy seats, the smell of petrol, vinyl and a driver's distrust of passengers.
Mahmoud sat in the front. The boy was put in the back, because in stories like this a boy must always be either a witness or a future neurotic. Sometimes they are the same thing.
Mahmoud pulled out a yellow-brown hundred-rouble note of the "wooden" money and handed it to the driver.
— Take me to a brothel.
The driver's expression did not change.
Not even an eyebrow.
A seasoned old hand, He would think now. Back then, he simply thought grown men possessed an astonishing ability not to be surprised in situations where a boy was already falling off a chair internally.
— We live in the USSR, — the driver said evenly. — We don't have sex here.
Mahmoud pulled out a second hundred.
Another yellow-brown one.
— Listen, dear friend, let's not. I didn't come for political briefing. I came to resort. Man needs rest. Drive.
The driver looked at the money.
Then at the road.
Then into the mirror at the boy sitting there red as a tomato that had just been appointed responsible for the moral condition of the district.
— Fine, — the driver said. — But if anything happens, I had nothing to do with it.
— Of course, dear friend, — Mahmoud beamed with gold teeth.
Then he turned to the boy and winked.
— Just don't tell your mama. She'll curse us both.
It was probably the first conspiracy of the adult world he had ever been included in, not because anybody trusted him but because he happened to be there.
They drove around town for about fifteen minutes.
The taxi driver circled so diligently it was as if he were taking them not to a particular place but around the perimeter of sin, so it would not recognise itself in the mirror too quickly. The streets were familiar and yet had suddenly become different. Kiosks, fences, trees, signs—everything looked like scenery for a play in which the adults had known their lines for years and the boy had been handed a part without rehearsal.
At last the car turned into a side street.
They stopped at the back entrance of a women's clothing atelier.
He knew the atelier itself.
They made dresses, suits, skirts and blouses there. Women came in with measurements, patterns, fashion magazines, hopes of becoming a little more beautiful and disappointment when the seamstress said, "That won't sit like that on your figure."
From the front entrance it was a respectable place.
The back entrance, as it turned out, had broader philosophical possibilities.
— Here, — the driver said, pointing. — And I was never here.
They got out.
Mahmoud strode confidently towards the door. The boy walked beside him, feeling fear, curiosity, embarrassment and that same stupid male interest all fighting inside him at once—the thing that had already awakened but did not yet possess experience, language or brakes.
They went in.
A long corridor.
Rooms on either side, full of work.
Women sat at sewing machines. Fabric rustled. Scissors clicked. Somebody marked lines in chalk on a dark dress. Somebody steamed a sleeve. Somebody argued about lining. The air held the smell of cloth, hot irons, women's perfume, dust and the special concentration found in places where people genuinely know how to make things with their hands.
It was an atelier.
A real one.
Not a metaphor. Not a signboard. Not a front in the cinematic sense.
They sewed here.
Which made the whole situation even more absurd.
They entered the central room.
A woman in her mid-fifties came towards them. She was dressed in a way that made one thing immediately clear: she did not merely work with clothes, she knew what a person demanding respect should look like even in a room full of rolls of fabric.
— Oh, I think you may have got lost, — she said in Ukrainian. — Were you wanting to order something?
— Yes, dear lady, I do, — Mahmoud blossomed so brightly the gold in his mouth could have lit a small stairwell during a power cut.
— Please, sit down, — she said, pointing to chairs beside a little table. — Choose your models. I'll be with you in a few minutes, I just need to see some clients out.
There were catalogues on the table.
Mahmoud sat down with such ceremony it was as if he had just been handed the menu in a restaurant serving not food but fulfilled desires.
He picked up a catalogue.
Began to leaf through it.
The pages showed women in dresses. Proud, elongated, slender women with expressions that said, "I know you can't make this, but go on, try."
The boy sat beside him and barely breathed.
Everything was wrong.
For some reason the brothel looked like an atelier. For some reason women were sewing. For some reason Mahmoud was leafing through models. For some reason Madame behaved as though this were a perfectly ordinary working day in which a man in a nutria hat might arrive with a boy and a mouthful of gold teeth to choose a cut.
A few minutes later she returned.
— Well, what will you choose?
Mahmoud raised the catalogue and began stabbing at the photographs with his finger.
— This one. This one. This one. And this one. And this one too.
Madame looked at him with a smile containing more experience than surprise. She even switched to Russian.
— You're going to have all of these sewn?
Mahmoud's grin stretched across every gold tooth.
— No, dear lady, — he said. — I'm going to rip through every one of them!
And the dream ended there.
He lay in bed staring at the ceiling.
The sparrows quarrelled outside. Sunlight pushed into the room. Somewhere in the flat life was starting up: water in the pipes, quiet footsteps, a wardrobe door, the first cough of the morning.
He did not move.
Why this?
Why now?
Why had memory dragged this old resort farce out of the basement—Mahmoud, the taxi driver, the atelier, the catalogue and the line that had once seemed like nothing more than an indecent joke from the adult world?
"I'm going to rip through every one of them."
He closed his eyes.
Opened them again.
And suddenly understood that the dream was not about sex.
Not even about Mahmoud.
Not about the resort either.
The dream was about him.
About his five tabs yesterday.
About the literary platforms.
About the cover.
About "follow back?"
About the blog where true bibliophiles demanded that works bearing an AI badge be hidden so they would not interfere with the search for true literature among reciprocal likes, magic academies and comments on blurbs nobody had read.
He had walked into a literary atelier.
At least that was what he thought.
He had brought fabric.
Perhaps not the best. Not expensive. Not silk. Maybe rough linen with stains, creases, crooked thread and the smell of an old wardrobe. But his own. He had brought a text he wanted to sew into something resembling a shape for his own pain. Something he could show another person and say: look, this is how it fits me on the inside.
But now he was no longer sure he had read the sign on the door correctly himself.
In the dream, Mahmoud was looking for a brothel and therefore saw a brothel even where sewing machines were standing. He was not lying. He simply wanted something so badly that desire became his translator of reality.
"This one. This one. And this one."
But the dream had slipped him a worse question.
Which kind was he?
He wanted to think he had come to sew. To create. To stitch meaning to experience, pain to humour, shame to repentance, silence to voice. To take the torn pieces of life and make at least some kind of shirt from them, something he would not be ashamed to wear in front of people.
But what if he had come to rip as well?
Not in Mahmoud's sense.
Worse.
What if he had come to rip open other people's comfortable fabric? To show everybody that beneath their glossy cuts there was emptiness, cheap seams, loose threads and a lining made of fear?
Maybe that was why nobody read him.
Nobody likes someone arriving with scissors and saying:
— I'm not destroying anything. I'm just going to show you the truth.
Truth matters, of course.
But form matters too.
Even a surgeon does not walk into a ward saying:
— Right, I'm going to cut you up a little now. Don't twitch. It's for your own good.
Even though that is exactly what a surgeon does.
Humanity invented anaesthesia, gowns, protocols and the word "operation" because without them even the most useful truth would look like an assault with a bladed weapon.
He sat up in bed.
Rubbed his face.
Remembered the taxi driver.
"We don't have sex here."
And the platform:
"Completely free from censorship."
He remembered how the driver had taken the money and driven not where Mahmoud had asked but where he himself had decided.
He remembered how the platform had proclaimed freedom and two minutes later asked for a tick in a box.
He remembered Mahmoud, who at least had been honest about what he wanted. Crude, funny, indecent, but honest. He did not lecture anyone on the ethics of leisure. Did not call his lust a spiritual quest. Did not write a blog post on "the importance of supporting female subjectivity in the resort environment." He simply said, "Drive."
Against that background, Mahmoud suddenly looked almost like a moral authority.
That was frightening.
When a man in a nutria-fur hat with gold teeth who asks a boy to take him to a brothel looks, in retrospect, more honest than digital bibliophiles, civilisation has clearly travelled an interesting road. Not necessarily upwards.
Mahmoud wanted to rip through them and said so.
Modern people came to rip things apart too, but called it "supporting authors," "community," "platform ethics," "defending the purity of literature" and "responsible use of technology."
Mahmoud was indecent.
They were respectable.
Which was precisely what made them worse.
But even that was not the worst part.
The worst part was that he himself might be one of them.
Because you can come to sew and behave as though you came to rip.
You can talk about pain while really wanting other people to admit that you are right.
You can call a text a confession while writing it like a verdict.
You can come to literature's atelier carrying your own cloth, then feel insulted when nobody rushes to take the measurements of your soul.
He went to the kitchen.
The house was not fully awake yet, but the kitchen was already prepared to receive him as a temporary accommodation centre for male thoughts. Yesterday's cup stood on the table. In the sink was a plate somebody had heroically carried almost to the end of its route before giving up twenty centimetres from the finish line.
He poured some water.
Sat down.
Opened the laptop.
The five platforms looked at him from his browser history like five witnesses to yesterday's humiliation.
He opened none of them.
Instead, he opened a blank document.
Wrote:
"Some people come to literature to sew. Others come to rip. The worst begins when you are no longer sure why you came."
He looked at it.
Did not delete it.
Added:
"A text is fabric. If you don't know how to sew, don't be surprised when the reader sees not a shirt but a pile of rags you're trying to use to wipe his conscience."
It hurt.
Which meant it was close.
He remembered yesterday's line:
"People don't read because they've become stupid. They're simply tired of people who keep turning up to save them."
Yes.
There it was.
He had no right to approach the reader dressed as a saviour.
People do not like saviours. Especially uninvited ones. An uninvited saviour very quickly starts to resemble a person who barges into your kitchen, throws out your sausage, lectures you about nitrates and asks you to thank him for caring.
He did not want to be that person.
But he had come close.
Maybe that was why his "Why" had touched nobody. It did not invite. It stood over the reader like a stern relative over a teenager who was once again living incorrectly.
He wanted to be a voice.
He had become an instruction manual.
He wanted to tell the truth.
Instead, he sounded as though he had come to deliver a political briefing.
That made him smile.
Mahmoud would not have approved.
"I didn't come for political briefing. I came to resort."
Perhaps that was the whole point.
People do not come to a text for a political briefing.
Even if the text is about the end of civilisation, losing oneself, digital hypocrisy and the loneliness of an old nudist on a beach where everybody is naked, the reader still does not come there to serve a sentence.
The reader comes to feel.
To stay for a while.
To see himself.
To laugh where it hurts.
To cry, provided the author is not tugging at his sleeve saying, "Cry here, idiot, I worked hard on this."
The reader is not guilty because the author is in pain.
It was an extremely unpleasant thought.
Thoughts like that ought to carry a separate badge:
"Created by conscience. May damage self-esteem."
He opened "Why."
Read the first paragraph.
Yes.
Too far above the reader.
The second.
Too certain.
The third.
By then you could already hear a man who was not merely knocking on the door but trying to come in and inspect the meter on your soul.
He leaned back in his chair.
There was no need to delete everything.
No need to stage a St Bartholomew's Night over the text.
The skeleton was not the body's enemy.
But the spine needed straightening.
He took the first sentence and rewrote it.
It had been:
"Humanity has lost the ability to hear truth because it traded attention for comfort."
It became:
"I traded attention for comfort too. At the time, I just called it a hard day rather than the degradation of humanity."
He read it.
That was more honest.
Because repentance, if it is real, does not begin with the words "all of you."
Repentance begins with "me too."
He rewrote the second paragraph.
It had been:
"Modern people put themselves on display yet remain empty."
It became:
"I always despised people who put themselves on display until I realised I was doing the same thing, only in the dark and without a photograph."
He stopped.
Good.
Painful, but good.
This was no longer ripping.
This was sewing.
Not beautifully, not confidently, not skilfully. But the needle had entered the cloth.
He rewrote slowly.
Changed "humanity" to "I."
Changed "the world" to "we."
Changed "they" to "sometimes I."
Removed the sentences in which he sounded like a man at a lectern.
Kept the ones in which he sounded like a man at a kitchen table.
The lectern was the enemy.
The kitchen was a chance.
A lectern raises you above people. A kitchen sits you beside them. A kitchen has no podium, but it has tea, stains on the table, a bread bin, old bills, a child's magnet on the refrigerator and the possibility of saying something important as though you are not pronouncing sentence but simply cannot remain silent any longer.
He wrote a new title:
"Not Here for a Political Briefing"
Then laughed.
Not loudly. Just one corner of his mouth.
Mahmoud would have appreciated it.
Or maybe not. Mahmoud probably had little patience for the literary torments of a man who, instead of simply saying where he wanted to go, sat for a third hour deciding whether to sew or rip.
He imagined Mahmoud standing behind him smelling of cologne, gold teeth gleaming, saying:
— Listen, dear friend, you think too much. Either write or stop bothering people.
And he would have been right.
Which was unbearable.
Because when the Mahmoud inside you is right, an intellectual has a very difficult time saving face.
He returned to the text.
Wrote until the flat was completely awake.
Until there were footsteps in the corridor.
Until somebody opened the refrigerator.
Until his wife came into the kitchen and said:
— You're up already?
— Mm-hm, — he said.
— Writing something?
Instinctively, he wanted to close the laptop.
Not because there was anything shameful on it.
It was simply that a man writing about his own inner turmoil looks like someone caught trying on another person's dress in an atelier of spiritual self-undressing.
But he did not close it.
— Sort of, — he said. — Rewriting.
— Rewriting what?
He looked at the screen.
Then at her.
— Myself, probably.
His wife silently poured herself some water.
She looked at him with that expression containing both "you're philosophising again" and "at least you're not drilling into the walls at night," plus a little of the mute knowledge that makes marriage more dangerous than any review.
— The main thing, — she said, — don't forget to take out the rubbish.
And left.
He watched her go.
That was reality.
No fanfare.
No digital Pharisees.
No tennis-playing critic with GPT.
No Mahmoud in a nutria hat.
Just the text, the kitchen, his wife, the rubbish and the morning sparrows outside continuing to debate the world so loudly you would think they owned the copyright.
He saved the file.
The title remained:
"Not Here for a Political Briefing."
Then he stood up, tied the rubbish bag and went to the door.
The stairwell smelled of dust, old paint and somebody's fried breakfast.
As he walked downstairs, it suddenly occurred to him that perhaps literature began precisely here.
Not where you decide to tell humanity the truth.
Where you finally understand that humanity has not ordered any.
But the neighbour on the second floor has already taken out her bag.
And the caretaker is sweeping cigarette butts.
And yesterday's life is lying in the bin.
And if you cannot see more truth in this than in your own manifesto, it is still too early for you to open people's eyes.
Learn to look first.
He threw out the rubbish.
Went home.
Sat down at the laptop.
And for the first time opened the platform not to check the statistics.
But to replace the text.
The old "Why" could still be saved.
But not as a sermon.
As a confession.
Not as a knife with which he wanted to rip open the fabric of somebody else's self-deception.
As a needle with which he might at least try to stitch his own.
He clicked "Edit."
And thought:
Damn you, Mahmoud. Maybe you didn't show up in that dream for nothing after all.

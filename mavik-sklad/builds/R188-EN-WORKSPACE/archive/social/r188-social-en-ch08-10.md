## CHAPTER 8

The new project began with a contract that ran to more than a hundred pages.

She did not read it from first page to last.

The finance manager did.

The lawyer did.

The programme team pulled out the parts that mattered for the work.

What landed on her desk was a table of budget lines and rules: what they could pay for, under what conditions, and whose approval had to come first.

In the first week, the new assistant came to her with an invoice.

“There's a family asking for a refrigerator. Can we buy it quickly?”

“From which budget?”

“Well... this one.”

“Which line?”

The girl looked at the screen.

“Material assistance.”

“Are they in our target group?”

“Yes.”

“Do we have a needs assessment from social services?”

“Not yet.”

“Then we need the assessment first.”

“But the refrigerator broke now.”

“I understand.”

“They have six children.”

She looked up.

“I understand.”

The girl stayed where she was.

“What if we buy it now and they bring the paperwork tomorrow?”

Once, she would have asked the same thing.

“No.”

“Why?”

“Because if social services write tomorrow that they already have another refrigerator, or that the family doesn't meet the criteria, we'll be left with an expense the donor won't accept.”

“But...”

“Call social services. Tell them we need it today. If they send electronic confirmation, we go ahead.”

The girl ran off to make the call.

An hour later, the confirmation came through.

Two days later, the refrigerator was in the family's home.

The assistant brought her a photo.

“Here.”

“Good.”

“That was fast.”

“When everyone answers the phone, it is.”

She smiled and went back to her email.

She liked cases like that.

It was clear what was needed.

It was clear what they had done.

It was clear what had changed.

The things that did not fit were harder.

A few weeks later, a social-services worker called.

“I've got a stupid question for you.”

“Go on.”

“One of our families has a dead car. Without it they can't get anywhere. Village, lots of kids, school at the other end of the community. Could you cover at least part of the repair?”

She already knew the answer.

She opened the budget anyway.

Material assistance to households.

Services for children.

Transport for participants in programme activities.

Nothing.

“No.”

“That quickly?”

“I'm looking.”

“Look harder.”

“I can pay for their transport to our activities. I can't pay to repair the car.”

“And if they can't get the child to the psychologist you're paying for because they don't have a car?”

She ran a finger down the budget lines.

“That doesn't change the rules.”

The woman on the other end breathed out.

“I know.”

There was something familiar in the way she said it.

“Give me an hour.”

She called another department.

Then a partner organisation.

Then her manager.

In the end they found a small reserve from private donations that were not tied to a specific programme.

Not the whole amount.

Enough to get the car moving again.

The social-services worker messaged her:

“Saved us.”

She wrote back:

“Not me.”

That was true too.

The money was there.

Just not the right money.

A month later came the first case where they could not find any other money.

A woman she did not know called her.

She spoke quickly.

A teenager in a foster family needed long-term work with a specialist. Not one session. Months. Maybe longer.

“Social services gave us your number. They said you have a psychological-support programme.”

“We do, but it's for participants in a specific programme.”

“We're a foster family.”

“I understand. Which community are you in?”

The woman told her.

That community was not part of the project.

She checked again.

“I can't take you under this budget.”

“Why?”

“Because the programme operates in other communities.”

“What difference does it make to the child which community we live in?”

She was silent for a second.

“To the child, none.”

“Then?”

“To the contract, it does.”

The woman went quiet too.

“I can give you contacts for two organisations that work across a wider area.”

“Are they free?”

“One definitely is. I'll check the other.”

“Okay.”

She wrote down the number, promised to call back, and did.

One organisation was not taking new cases.

The other could put them on a waiting list.

She told the woman.

“Thank you.”

“If anything changes, message me.”

“Okay.”

The call ended.

For a few seconds she kept looking at the phone.

Once, the words *we're not taking cases at the moment* had sounded like an excuse.

Now she knew that sometimes there was a contract behind them. A territory. A target group. A budget line. A person who had no authority to move a number from one box to another.

That did not make the words any better.

They simply stopped being incomprehensible.

Her manager walked in without knocking.

The same half-finished coffee in her hand.

“Why are you sitting here?”

“Working.”

“No. You're sitting.”

She turned the screen towards her.

“Wrong community.”

Her manager read the note quickly.

“Happens.”

“But we have money for psychologists.”

“We do.”

“And some of it will be left over.”

“Might be. Might not.”

“But it's there now.”

Her manager put down the cup.

“It's not our money.”

She said it so simply that for a moment there was nothing to answer.

“I know.”

“The donor gave it for specific work in specific places. If we decide we know better, that's not partnership anymore. That's us inventing a foundation inside the foundation.”

“What if spending it differently would be the right thing to do?”

“Then you persuade the people who gave the money and amend the agreement.”

“What if that takes longer than the person can wait?”

Her manager looked at her.

Longer than usual.

“Then you find other money.”

“And if there isn't any?”

“Then there isn't any.”

She waited for more.

There was no more.

Her manager picked up the cup.

“Don't look at me like that. I didn't invent that answer.”

“I know.”

“And I don't like it either.”

She left.

That evening, she opened the old chat with the volunteer.

Not all of it.

She just scrolled through a few messages.

“Budget exhausted.”

“Closed for this quarter.”

“Declined.”

She did not read any further.

Closed it.

The next morning, during a meeting, the new assistant suggested paying for one more thing from the programme budget even though it was not covered by the programme.

She answered before she had time to think.

“We can't. Not our area.”

And carried on talking about the schedule.

Only a few minutes later did she realise what she had said.

Nobody else noticed.

## CHAPTER 9

When a year had passed since her first day at the foundation, nobody noticed.

Neither did she.

Her calendar held two meetings, a budget approval, and a session with consultants who were finishing a needs assessment of family-based care.

The foundation had commissioned the study together with a partner.

Not to prove anything in particular.

At least that was what the terms of reference said.

Surveys.

Focus groups.

Interviews with parents.

Social services.

Professionals.

Several hundred responses.

At the presentation, the consultant began with the methodology.

She listened for the first few minutes.

Then opened the report itself.

More than a hundred pages.

Charts.

Tables.

Percentages.

“Competency gaps.”

“Need for systematic psychosocial support.”

“Insufficient peer-support mechanisms.”

“Risk of emotional burnout.”

It was all familiar.

Not because she had read reports like this before.

Because she had seen the people.

The recommendations appeared on the screen:

regular training cycles;

supervision groups;

regional meetings;

recovery programmes;

support for parenting competencies.

She raised her hand.

“Do you have the raw responses?”

The consultant stopped.

“Some are in the appendices.”

“Not some. All of them.”

“We have the database. Why?”

“I want to look.”

After the meeting, they gave her access.

That evening she stayed late at the office.

She was curious.

She opened the spreadsheet.

The question read:

“What would help your family most right now?”

The answers came in no particular order.

“A psychologist for my son.”

“Stop changing our caseworker.”

“Money for fuel.”

“A nanny, even once a month.”

“A summer break for the children.”

“A decent psychiatrist less than a hundred kilometres away.”

“A chance to talk to other parents.”

“Nothing. Just don't get in the way.”

“Car repairs.”

“A speech therapist.”

“Two days of silence.”

“Training on sexualised behaviour.”

“Someone you can call in the evening, not only from nine to six.”

She scrolled.

Some answers repeated.

Others appeared only once.

Then she opened the relevant section of the report.

“Respondents demonstrate a high need for access to comprehensive psychosocial support services, development of parenting competencies, and peer-support mechanisms.”

It wasn't untrue.

“Two days of silence” could reasonably be called burnout prevention.

“Someone you can call in the evening” became access to support.

“A chance to talk to other parents” became a peer-support mechanism.

Even “just don't get in the way” could probably be filed somewhere if you wanted badly enough.

She smiled.

The next day she asked the consultant:

“Why didn't you leave more of the real answers in the report?”

“We left quotes.”

“I saw them. Five.”

“A report can't be four hundred lines of people talking.”

“And where did ‘two days of silence’ go?”

He thought about it.

“Burnout prevention, I suppose.”

“That's what I thought too.”

“Is that bad?”

“I don't know.”

He smiled.

“If you find out, let me know.”

They began designing the next programmes around the report.

Now she sat in those meetings not as the person “from the field.”

People expected decisions from her.

“How many groups can we realistically manage?”

“If we don't run everything at once, six.”

“Eight?”

“Then either we cut follow-up or people work nights.”

“Seven?”

“Seven.”

“Where?”

She named the communities.

“Why those?”

“Because there's someone there to carry on after we leave.”

“And the places where there isn't?”

“If we show up for two days and leave, there still won't be.”

They wrote it down.

In another section, they argued about residential training.

“Four days is enough.”

“For the programme, yes. For the people, no.”

“What does ‘for the people’ mean?”

“On day five they start talking to each other properly.”

Her manager looked at her.

“Scientific observation?”

“Fieldwork.”

They kept five days.

She was the one who suggested fewer lectures and more small-group work.

Bring in two experienced parents not as “participants” but as co-facilitators.

Plan the children's programme so parents were not getting phone calls every twenty minutes.

Don't print new notebooks.

Some of her suggestions were accepted.

Some weren't.

Fine.

A few months earlier, it would have irritated her when a good idea failed because it did not fit the donor's wording.

Now she simply looked for different wording.

Sometimes she found it.

Sometimes she didn't.

Her manager began leaving her meetings she could not get to herself.

“You'll go instead of me.”

“What if they ask something I don't know?”

“Say you don't know.”

“Very professional.”

“The most professional answer available.”

One day she walked into her manager's office and saw two cups.

One had coffee in it.

The other was empty.

“Sit down.”

“I'm scared already.”

“Good.”

Her manager slid a sheet of paper across the desk.

A new structure.

Their department was expanding. Her manager was taking on another major area and could no longer run the orphan-care portfolio herself day to day.

“I want you to run it completely.”

She read the job title.

Programme Lead.

“I've only been here a year.”

“And?”

“People have been here ten.”

“Let them.”

“Are you serious?”

“No. I drew an org chart for entertainment.”

She put the page down.

“I'll think about it.”

“Fine.”

“How long?”

“Until tomorrow.”

“That's not thinking.”

“You've been thinking about it for three months. You just didn't know.”

She took the sheet home.

Put it on the kitchen table.

Her phone lay beside it.

The photograph was still on the refrigerator.

She had stopped turning it face down a long time ago.

She sat.

Read the job description.

People.

Budgets.

Partners.

Reports.

Approvals.

Responsibility for the programme area.

At the bottom: access to the programme archive and full financial documentation within her area.

She finished reading.

Looked at the photograph.

Did not speak to it.

Did not think anything proper.

The next morning, she simply said:

“Okay.”

Her manager raised her cup.

“Congratulations.”

“Your coffee's cold.”

“I know.”

Two days later, they opened new folders for her on the shared drive.

There were so many she did not even bother looking.

Then.

## CHAPTER 10

She went into the archive not out of curiosity.

A partner was preparing a review before renewing a major programme and had asked for three examples of completed projects from previous years.

“Pick good ones,” her former manager said.

Formally, they were now peers, each responsible for a different area, but in her head she was still the manager.

“Do we have bad ones?”

“We have projects you don't show people when they ask for good ones.”

“Diplomatic.”

“I'm learning.”

She opened the archive.

Folders were organised by year.

Inside them: donors.

Programmes.

Contracts.

Procurement.

Reports.

Photographs.

Scanned acceptance forms.

She picked the first project she remembered herself.

Too recent. Couldn't use it.

The second was older and simple: material support for several institutions.

The third was a training programme for prospective foster parents.

Another was a children's camp.

She opened final reports and looked at what the work had looked like a few years before she arrived.

Very similar.

The same words.

Different templates.

Worse photographs.

More handwritten signatures.

In one file she found an invoice for printing notebooks.

She laughed.

Then stopped.

Notebooks were everywhere.

Closer to noon, she came across a folder with a title she had seen before in an old annual report.

“Comprehensive Professional Development Programme for Foster Parents and Parents Running Family-Type Homes.”

She opened it.

Five days.

Thirty-seven families.

Children included.

Residential format.

Training before lunch.

Training after lunch.

Psychologists.

Trainers.

A separate programme for the children.

She flipped through the agenda.

Three hours.

Lunch.

Another three.

Almost exactly what she herself had organised a few months earlier.

Only bigger.

She opened the photographs.

A good resort.

In the first picture, people sat in a conference room.

In the second, they were working in groups.

In the third, children were drawing something.

In the fourth, everyone stood outside with a banner.

Then came photographs that were not for the report.

Someone had probably dumped the whole camera roll into the shared folder.

The pool.

Children in the water.

Men around a table on the terrace.

Women in bathrobes in the spa corridor.

Nothing remarkable.

If she had been there, she would probably have had photographs just like them.

She closed them.

Opened the budget.

The total made her go back to the top line.

She checked whether this covered the entire project, perhaps several years.

Five days.

One event.

Thirty-seven families.

Ten million hryvnias.

She moved back from the screen.

Then moved closer again.

Started reading the lines.

Accommodation.

Meals.

Room hire.

Transport.

Children's programme.

Trainer fees.

Psychologists.

Materials.

Organisational costs.

Communications.

Coordination.

The figures were large.

But there was no single one she could point at and say: there.

She opened the procurement folder.

Requests for quotations.

Three options.

The selected resort was not the cheapest.

The justification said: sufficient family rooms, conference facilities, a room for the children's programme, on-site catering, secure grounds.

The second option was cheaper but did not have enough rooms.

The third was more expensive.

Fine.

She opened the trainer contracts.

Qualifications.

Experience.

Certificates.

Programme.

Fine.

Transport.

Routes.

Invoices.

Acceptance forms.

Fine.

She opened the final report.

“30 families participated.”

“30 hours of training delivered.”

“Children's programme provided.”

“Participants reported high levels of satisfaction.”

“Planned results achieved.”

She went through the appendices.

Feedback forms.

The same fours and fives.

“It was useful.”

“Thank you for organising this.”

“More practical work.”

“We'd like another one.”

One response said:

“The best part was that the children were occupied and we could sit through the training in peace.”

Another:

“Hadn't seen the other families in ages.”

And another:

“First time in years we all had a rest together.”

She went back to the budget.

Ten million.

Opened a calculator beside it.

Started dividing.

By thirty.

By five.

Then closed the calculator.

She did not know what she was trying to produce.

Cost per day?

Per family?

Per hour of training?

Why?

The work had been done.

People had come.

Stayed.

Eaten.

Listened to the trainers.

The children had been occupied.

The paperwork balanced.

The report had been accepted without comment.

She opened the donor's email.

“Thank you for the successful implementation of the programme.”

Below it was the old thread.

The project manager had asked permission to move a small amount between budget lines.

The donor approved it.

Another email clarified the number of participants.

Another confirmed final closure.

No conflict.

No complaint.

No “please explain where the money went.”

Because the money had not gone anywhere.

It was exactly where it was supposed to be.

She leaned back in her chair.

The assistant knocked on the door.

“Coming to lunch?”

“In a minute.”

“We're going now.”

“Go.”

“Want me to bring you something?”

“No, thanks.”

The door closed.

She looked again at the first page of the budget.

Total:

10,000,000.00 UAH

Below, in the final financial report:

Spent:

10,000,000.00 UAH

Balance:

0.00 UAH

She closed the file.

A few seconds later, she opened it again.

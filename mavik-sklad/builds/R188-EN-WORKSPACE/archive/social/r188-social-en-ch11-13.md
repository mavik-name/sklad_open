## CHAPTER 11

By noon the next day, she still had not chosen a third project for the partner.

The assistant peered into her office again and set a plastic container on the edge of the desk.

“You told me not to bring anything, but I did anyway. Soup.”

“I don't want it.”

“Then don't eat it. It can just sit there.”

“Thanks.”

“Just not until tomorrow. I'm serious.”

She nodded and went back to the screen. The ten-million folder was still open, but there was nothing left to look at. It was all there already: contracts, invoices, acceptance forms, photographs, emails, questionnaires, the final report. If the partner wanted an example of a properly closed project, this one would have done perfectly well.

She did not choose it.

She did not know why.

Maybe she simply did not want to explain to someone outside the foundation, once again, why a five-day residential programme for thirty-seven families had cost ten million and why none of the paperwork raised a single question.

She opened the neighbouring folder.

Six washing machines, several beds, two refrigerators and furniture for newly established families. No hotels. No trainers. Delivery notes, invoices, photographs after installation. In one picture, a man in socks stood in the middle of a room holding the washing-machine door, as if he could not decide whether he was supposed to close it for the photo.

She enlarged the image. In the background, one of the children was sticking their tongue out at the photographer.

It was a good project.

She marked it.

The next one was even simpler: several months of funding for psychologists working in local communities. Modest costs, lots of people, a dry report. The appendices contained consultation schedules and a few short anonymised case stories. In one, a teenager had returned to school after several months of work. In another, a family had asked for a different specialist because they “didn't click.” The foundation changed the specialist.

She marked that one too.

An hour later, six different projects were open in front of her, and they were ruining the simple picture that had almost assembled itself the day before.

In some places, money moved almost directly from the foundation's account to an object you could put in a room and photograph. Elsewhere, half the work consisted of people talking to other people. Some projects involved travel, hotels, room hire. One had such a small administrative budget that she checked the formula several times. Another had a much larger one because the donor required separate financial control, an external audit and an evaluation of results.

In the audit folder, she opened an invoice, looked at the amount and thought that the same money could have bought several more refrigerators.

Then she opened the auditor's report.

More than seventy pages.

She closed it.

The thought about the refrigerators did not go away, but she did not feel like arguing with seventy pages.

Closer to evening, her former manager came in. She came less often now and usually without coffee. Instead she carried a bottle of water that she forgot to finish in exactly the same way.

“Chosen?”

“Two.”

“You need three.”

“I can count.”

“That's something.”

She sat opposite and looked at the list of open folders.

“What are you doing, reading the entire archive?”

“No. Only half of humanity so far.”

“And?”

“Depends.”

“Bad conclusion for a report.”

“It's not for a report.”

Her former manager twisted the cap on the bottle.

“What are you looking for?”

She almost said nothing.

She didn't.

“That old residential programme. The one for thirty-seven families.”

“What about it?”

“Ten million.”

For a second her former manager looked at her, then nodded.

“I remember.”

“You were here then?”

“I was. Different programme.”

“Is that a lot?”

“For what?”

She gave a faint smile.

“I hate it when you answer like that.”

“Because the question is like that. For five days? A lot. For a large group with children, accommodation, programme activities, transport and everything else? You look at the budget. You looked at it.”

“I did.”

“Find anything?”

“No.”

“Well.”

That *well* irritated her slightly.

“If every line is fine, does that automatically mean the whole thing is fine?”

Her former manager did not answer at once. She unscrewed the cap and took a drink.

“No.”

She waited.

“And?”

“And what? I'm not sitting an exam.”

“You never thought about it?”

“About what exactly? Whether the money could have been spent better? All the time. You can ask that about every budget. Even when you're buying winter shoes for children, you can find someone who needs medicine at that very moment.”

“And what do you do with that?”

“Work.”

She said it without ceremony, stood up and took the bottle with her.

“For the third project, use the psychologists. It's solid, and the partners liked it.”

“I already marked it.”

“See? And you say you can't.”

“I didn't say that.”

“Then apparently I'm arguing with you all by myself now.”

At the door, her former manager stopped.

“Eat the soup.”

“How do you know about the soup?”

“This is an office. Everybody knows everything.”

She left.

The soup had been cold for a long time. She still ate half of it straight from the container without reheating it. At the same time, she finished the email to the partner and attached three projects: material support for families, psychological support, and one small programme for prospective foster carers. The ten-million residential programme stayed in the archive.

That could have been the end of it.

The next morning, she opened it again.

This time, the correspondence.

Email archives in old projects were stored strangely. Some messages were individual files. Some were scans. Someone had exported whole threads. Folder names varied too: `correspondence`, `emails`, `donor`, `important`, `important_2`.

In one folder she found a photograph of a cat.

Black. Very angry.

The file was called `final_budget`.

She laughed loudly enough for the assistant in the next room to call out:

“What is it?”

“Nothing. The archive.”

“I want an archive like that too.”

“No.”

She did not delete the cat.

At first it was not clear what she was looking for. She entered the programme title, then the surname of the old coordinator, then the year. Hundreds of documents appeared: room bookings, menu clarifications, children's lists, trainer approvals, transfer arrangements. Somewhere a suitcase had gone missing. Somewhere someone had demanded a cot even though the application said the child was eleven. Three emails later it turned out the year of birth had been entered incorrectly.

She read in fragments, and gradually stopped seeing the old team as the people who had “spent ten million.”

They had argued about the bus, searched for a missing invoice, replaced a participant the day before departure and asked the hotel not to put nuts in the dessert for one child.

Ordinary work.

The year was still in the search box. She removed the programme title and typed `medical`.

There were few results. At the time, the foundation had done almost no work with medical treatment. One file was about first-aid kits for institutions. Two more were about first-aid training. Then came several emails whose subject lines all began the same way: “Request regarding possible support.”

She opened the first.

An appeal from a small civil-society organisation. They were asking for help paying for a child's treatment. Medical records, an invoice and the mother's contact details were attached. At the bottom of the old thread was the foundation's reply.

Short.

“Thank you for contacting us. Unfortunately, financing medical treatment is not provided for under our current programmes.”

Then came contact details for two organisations that were handling cases like that at the time.

She read it again.

A normal answer.

She could have written exactly the same one today.

The second email was from a volunteer group. Another child. Another illness whose name she still could not remember after reading it twice. A smaller amount. The reply was almost the same, with one additional sentence:

“The budget for the current period has been approved and does not include the relevant category of expenditure.”

The third request had come from a hospital.

She opened the attachment, but it was a badly scanned two-page letter. Half the stamp had disappeared off the edge. The text asked the foundation to consider supporting several urgent cases. There was no list. The list had been a separate appendix, and it was not in this folder.

The foundation's reply sat beside it:

“Financing under current programmes is not provided for.”

The date was underneath.

She looked at the date longer than she looked at the sentence.

Then she went back to the residential-training folder and opened the first contract with the resort.

The dates were almost side by side.

That meant nothing.

The programme had been approved earlier. The funds had a designated purpose. One email had nothing to do with the other.

She knew all of that without anyone explaining it.

She opened the calendar for that year anyway. There was no proper calendar in the archive, only a quarterly activity table. She found the month and began checking dates.

The assistant knocked.

“We have the call with the community at two. Don't forget.”

“I won't.”

“You said that yesterday too.”

“And I didn't forget.”

“You came seven minutes late.”

“That's different.”

“Of course.”

The door closed.

She looked at the clock. Twenty-three minutes to the call.

She went back to the table and copied two dates into a separate column.

Then one more.

## CHAPTER 12

Before the call with the community, she managed to find the appendix.

It was not where it should have been. Not in the correspondence folder. Not with the medical requests either. She found it in the general archive under the name `appendix_final_correct`.

Someone had once been very determined not to confuse it with anything else.

The table was one page long.

Ten rows.

Each contained the child's age, a short description of the condition, the name of the intervention without unnecessary detail, an estimated cost, and the deadline by which the hospital needed an answer.

At the bottom was the total.

10,000,000 UAH.

At first she thought the number looked familiar only because she had been looking for it.

She counted the zeros.

Then counted them again.

Ten children. Different amounts. Some less than a million, some more.

Together, exactly ten.

Eleven minutes remained before the call.

She opened the old correspondence from that date.

The first email was from the foundation coordinator to management. Short. No dramatic language. The hospital had submitted an urgent request. They were asking the foundation to consider full or partial financing. The coordinator noted separately that there was limited time for decisions in these cases.

Below it was the finance manager's reply.

“There is no uncommitted balance in the medical programme.”

Further down, someone from management had asked:

“Can we revisit the budget of the family programme?”

She stopped moving the mouse.

This had not been in the other folder.

The next email came twenty-seven minutes later.

“Technically, yes. We would need donor approval and a budget amendment. The issue is that the residential event is already in active preparation.”

Then came a list.

Not a table. Just someone writing down what they knew.

Printing completed.

Hotel confirmed, deposit paid.

Transport partly contracted.

Trainers have confirmed the dates.

Participants have received confirmation.

Children's programme arranged.

Some materials purchased.

She finished reading and automatically opened the residential-programme folder. It was all there. Contracts, payments, emails, layouts, lists.

Now they stood in a different order.

Not as proof that the project had taken place.

As things that would have to be stopped.

Her phone lit up with a message from the assistant.

“2 min.”

She closed the archive and joined the call.

The community was arguing about a room for the next training. They had been offered the school's assembly hall, but the trainers wanted tables they could move. The school tables were bolted to the floor.

“They're not bolted down,” the woman from the community said. “They're just heavy.”

“Four of us dragged them last year,” a man she did not know cut in. “Metal legs. I was rubbing ointment into my back for two days afterwards.”

Someone laughed.

She asked them to send photographs of the room. Five minutes later they agreed to look at another space in the library.

Then they discussed the children who would have their own programme, lunch, water and the bus from the neighbouring village. Someone's dog was barking. The sound dropped twice. One man spent the entire call with his camera off, then switched it on at the end and turned out to be sitting in a car.

The meeting ended seven minutes early.

“See?” the assistant said, putting her head around the door. “We can do it when we want to.”

“Don't get used to it.”

“Too late.”

She waited for the door to close and went back to the old correspondence.

After the list of things already prepared came another question from the same coordinator:

“What are the cancellation losses?”

The finance manager did not answer immediately.

The hotel could return part of the deposit, but not all of it. They would need to check the transport contracts. The printing had already been paid for and produced. Formally, the trainers could be cancelled, but two were travelling from other cities and one had turned down other work for those dates. Participants had already been told their accommodation was confirmed.

At the end:

“If the donor approves, we can reallocate the budget. But this effectively means cancelling the event and restarting part of the project.”

The words were calm.

She knew very well what sat behind them.

Call the hotel. Pull out the contracts. Work out the penalties. Recalculate the budget. Write to the donor. Wait for approval. Explain it to the trainers. Apologise to some people. Refund other people's tickets. Work out what to do with the printed materials already carrying dates and the programme title. Inform the families who had already planned the trip. Change the calendar. Change the reporting period. Then spend several more months answering questions about why an activity included in the approved plan had not taken place.

Not a catastrophe.

Just a lot of work.

She realised she disliked that thought precisely because of the word *just*.

The next email was from the programme lead. A different surname. Someone she did not know.

“Colleagues, before we initiate amendments, please assess whether we have confirmation that the hospital can carry out all interventions within the stated timeframes if financing is provided. Please also take into account that 30 families have confirmed for the residential event, some with children. For many of them, this is their first in-person activity in a long time.”

An hour later, the hospital replied: yes, if payment was available, they were ready to proceed according to the stated schedule. Time was critical for some children. Two cases could tolerate a short delay.

Then another pause appeared in the thread.

Almost four hours.

She looked at the sent times as if the answer might be hidden inside those four hours.

Then came an email from someone in the foundation's management.

Not an order.

Not quite a decision either.

More a summary of a conversation that had obviously happened somewhere other than email.

“I propose that we do not stop the approved event. We already have significant organisational and financial commitments, and a rapid budget reapproval is not guaranteed. For the medical cases, please urgently check whether other partners and private donations can be mobilised.”

She read it twice.

Then opened the next message.

The medical-request coordinator did not argue.

“All right. I'm going to the partners.”

Twenty minutes later she sent a list of eight organisations they had already contacted or were about to contact.

So they had not refused to help.

They were looking for other money.

She sat still and kept reading.

One organisation replied that its budget was exhausted. Another could take one case but needed additional documents. A third did not finance that type of treatment. A fourth suggested a public fundraiser. Two more did not reply that day.

That evening, the coordinator wrote to the hospital that the foundation could not finance the whole request but was continuing to look for options.

Normal work.

Good work, even.

She knew how many people in situations like this simply forwarded a standard refusal and closed the email. Here, someone had called, written, forwarded documents, searched.

It did not make anything easier.

The next day another internal email appeared in the folder.

“Everything is confirmed for the event. The hotel needs the final number by 15:00.”

And a few hours later:

“We confirm 30 families.”

She opened the table of medical cases.

Ten rows.

Went back to the hotel email.

Thirty families.

The numbers stood beside each other only because she herself had opened two windows on one screen.

The people who wrote those emails might never have seen both documents at the same time.

She minimised one window.

Then restored it.

There was a knock on the door.

“Can I?”

The assistant came in with a folder.

“This needs your signature. And the bus price has changed because they calculated it without the second run.”

She took the papers.

“By how much?”

The assistant named the amount.

“Fine. But have them send a revised calculation. Not just a message.”

“Already asked.”

“Good.”

The assistant stayed where she was.

“You're a bit... today.”

She waited.

“A bit what?”

“I don't know. Frozen.”

“Reading the archive.”

“Ah. That explains it. Old contracts freeze me too.”

She smiled.

“Go work.”

“Already am.”

When the door closed, she signed the folder without looking. Then opened it immediately, checked the amount and signed again where she was actually supposed to.

There was one file left in the old correspondence called `decision`.

She did not want to open it straight away.

That was ridiculous.

The word meant nothing. In folders like these, `decision` could just as easily be the participants list.

She opened it.

It was a short set of minutes from an internal meeting.

No children's names.

No discussion of death.

Three items on the agenda.

First: the hospital's medical request.

Second: the residential programme for families.

Third: delayed furniture deliveries to two communities.

Under the first item, the minutes recorded that the request had been recognised as urgent, internal reallocation had been discussed, but given the stage of preparation of current activities, the decision was not to initiate a review of the approved budget. The responsible person was instructed to continue seeking external financing.

Under the second: confirm the event as planned.

Under the third: wait for the supplier's response until Friday.

That was all.

She did not feel angry.

That surprised her more than the document itself.

She read it and understood how they had arrived there.

If someone brought her a programme today with a hotel already booked, contracts signed, families assembled, materials printed, and beside it an urgent request that might be covered from some other source, she was not sure she would say anything different in the first ten minutes.

Maybe she would say exactly the same thing:

first, let's check the partners.

No need to break everything.

We'll find another source.

Her phone rang.

A social-services worker was asking about a family whose bed delivery had been promised by the end of the week. The driver was not answering.

She found the logistics officer's number, called and waited until he picked up. The vehicle would be there tomorrow afternoon.

“Definitely?” the social-services worker asked.

“Unless it breaks down on the way.”

“Don't say that.”

“Fine. It'll be there tomorrow.”

“Much better.”

She ended the call and wrote herself a reminder to check the delivery.

Then went back to the minutes.

The date was at the bottom.

She checked it against the hospital table.

Then against the email about the hotel's final headcount.

She saw nothing new.

Everything was already clear.

Through the wall, someone was arguing about whether to order tea bags for the next event or one big teapot. The assistant said loudly that nobody ever washed the big teapot afterwards.

She closed the minutes.

Not the archive.

Only the minutes.

A minute later she opened her email and replied to the community about the library.

## CHAPTER 13

The next morning, she did not open the archive straight away.

Two replies about the library were waiting in her inbox. One community had changed its participant numbers, and a trainer had written to say that another event overlapped with day three and asked whether two sessions could be swapped. She replied that they could, provided the second trainer agreed.

The second trainer had not replied.

Then the assistant brought in the bus invoice.

“This already includes the second run.”

“I see.”

“I checked the mileage.”

“And?”

“Fine.”

“Then send it for payment.”

The assistant lingered at the door.

“And the tables at the library move.”

“Finally, some good news.”

“But there are no curtains.”

She looked up.

“Why do we need curtains?”

“I don't know. They volunteered the information.”

“Fine. We'll survive.”

When the door closed, she spent another ten minutes finishing what was already on her screen.

Then she opened the old hospital folder.

Yesterday she had seen a ten-row table. Age, short description of condition, amount, deadlines. Names were not included there. For the decision, they probably had not been necessary.

The medical appendices were stored separately.

Ten folders, numbered 01 to 10.

She opened the first.

Medical extract, specialist opinion, invoice, covering letter. Some were scans, some photographs of pages taken on a phone. The old files opened slowly. One PDF even asked for permission to repair corrupted data. She clicked yes and waited while the software fixed something.

The first child was not hers.

Neither was the second.

At the third folder, the phone rang. A social-services worker could not find an email they had sent her yesterday. She forwarded it again and waited while the woman opened it.

“Got it?”

“No.”

“I've literally just sent it.”

“Oh. There. It's in Promotions. Why did it decide you're a promotion?”

“Because I'm very attractive.”

“I know that, but how does the email know?”

She laughed.

“Open the attachment.”

“Opening. Thanks.”

She went back to the archive.

The fourth folder contained twelve pages. She flipped past the first two. The third opened sideways. On the fourth, one line at the bottom was partly covered by the edge of another sheet.

She stopped scrolling.

Not because of the content.

Because of that edge.

Once, the volunteer had asked her to photograph that page again. She did not remember the whole conversation, only her own irritation. They had already photographed the documents several times. The hospital wanted one thing, then another. Something needed translating. Something else needed sending again. She had looked at the photo and said all the figures were visible.

The volunteer had agreed to send it as it was.

She enlarged the page.

Her daughter's name was in the header.

Below it, her date of birth.

She read both lines, then lowered her eyes to the middle of the page even though there was nothing there she did not already know.

Her knee hit the pedestal as she pushed her chair back. It rolled a little. She nudged it into place with her foot.

The page remained enlarged on the screen.

She opened the old chat with the volunteer.

It did not take long to find. She already knew the dates.

“They want the medical extract.”

A few messages later: photographs.

One.

Two.

Three.

This one.

The same sheet.

The same edge at the bottom.

Under it was her own message:

“Everything's visible.”

The volunteer had replied:

“Okay, sending it.”

The next day:

“Documents submitted.”

She set the phone on the desk and went back to the archived email. The incoming message from the hospital was from the same day.

She opened the table.

Row four.

Amount: 1,000,000 UAH.

She knew that number.

For three weeks she had watched how much was still missing from it. A hundred hryvnias arrived. Two hundred. Five hundred. The gap shrank so slowly that sometimes it seemed not to be shrinking at all.

Here, the million stood ready in a single row.

One million.

There were other amounts beside it. Smaller. Larger.

Together, ten.

She went back to the chat.

What followed was what she had already read many times after the death.

“Waiting.”

“Still no answer.”

“I reminded them.”

Then:

“Their budget is approved. They're not taking unplanned medical cases right now.”

For a long time she looked not at the sentence but at the time underneath it.

Inside the foundation, in those same days, they had asked whether the budget could be reviewed.

It could.

Then they had counted what was already paid for, who had already been notified, what would have to be cancelled, and decided to look for other money.

The volunteer had written exactly what she received on the outside.

The chat did not contain the hotel, the printing, the transport, the thirty families or the email saying, “All right. I'm going to the partners.”

Nor should it have.

Somebody dropped a spoon loudly in the kitchen.

Then another.

“Oh, come on,” a voice said.

She stood up.

She did not know why.

Just walked to the kitchen.

The accountant was sitting on the floor beside a cupboard, picking up cutlery.

“Don't help,” she said. “I've got it. I opened the wrong drawer and the whole thing slid out.”

“You don't have drawer stops?”

“We don't have a budget line for drawer stops.”

“Serious compliance failure.”

“I've been saying that for years.”

She poured herself some water.

“Are you eating? We're ordering.”

She looked at the clock.

“What are you getting?”

“I don't know. They've been deciding for twenty minutes. I don't care as long as there's no coriander.”

“Then get me something without coriander too.”

“I'll record it as a separate project result.”

“Only if it's measurable.”

The accountant waved a spoon at her.

She went back to her office.

The page with her daughter's name was still open.

The water trembled in the glass as she put it down. She waited until it stopped.

Then reduced the document to normal size.

Now it was a twelve-page medical extract again.

She went through the rest of the files in the folder. Invoice. Covering letter. Short form completed by the hospital. One field said the family was already running a public fundraiser. Another said a decision was required as soon as possible.

In the internal notes beside the case:

“Forwarded to partners.”

Further down:

“Awaiting response.”

And nothing else.

No note saying paid.

No note saying declined.

Nothing about how it ended.

She knew.

The archive did not.

She opened search and entered the case number. The system found several emails. In two, the coordinator asked partners to respond more quickly. One partner asked for an additional document.

Then the thread stopped.

She checked the date of the last message.

Her daughter had still been alive then.

The phone lay beside her. She did not touch it for several minutes, then opened the chat anyway.

After the message about the refusal came other foundations, other people, requests to share the fundraiser, links, documents.

She did not read everything.

She closed the chat on the message:

“Let's try these people too.”

She returned to folder 04.

Then looked at the others.

05.

06.

07.

And three more after that.

She suddenly realised she did not know what had happened to any of those children.

In the table, all ten existed only at the moment when the foundation had to make a decision. After that, the programme went its own way, the hospital went its own way, each family went its own way.

She made a copy of the table so she would not touch the archived file and added a column on the right.

At first she wrote `outcome`.

Deleted it.

Wrote `follow-up status`.

Left it.

She entered nothing in row four.

Lunch arrived earlier than promised. The assistant came in with a paper bag.

“Your syrnyky. No coriander. I checked.”

“Thanks.”

“Did you seriously make them check for coriander in syrnyky?”

“Not me.”

“The accountant said it was a matter of principle.”

She set the bag on the edge of the desk.

“Leave them there.”

“They'll get cold.”

“I know.”

The assistant left.

She opened folder 01 again. This time she did not read the medical details. She looked for a case number, correspondence, a last date, any mention of what had happened afterwards.

At 13:55, her phone reminded her about the two o'clock meeting.

She set another reminder for five minutes later and kept searching.
